3.1 Approach to malabsorption

Malabsorption is defective absorption of fats, fat- and water-soluble vitamins, proteins, carbohydrates, electrolytes, minerals and water. It presents as chronic diarrhoea, and its hallmark is steatorrhoea: bulky, pale, greasy, foul-smelling stools that float. The commonest causes are pancreatic insufficiency, coeliac disease and Crohn disease.

Absorption is a four-step relay; malabsorption is a dropped baton. (1) Intraluminal digestion: pancreatic enzymes and bile salts break food down. (2) Terminal digestion: brush-border enzymes (disaccharidases, peptidases) finish the job. (3) Transepithelial transport: enterocytes take up the products and package fat into chylomicrons. (4) Lymphatic transport: lacteals carry chylomicrons away. Fat needs all four steps, so fat is the first thing to escape into the stool when any step fails.

3.2 Coeliac disease (gluten-sensitive enteropathy)

Coeliac disease is an immune-mediated enteropathy triggered by eating gluten-containing cereals (wheat, barley, rye) in genetically predisposed people. It affects roughly 1% of Western populations and resolves when gluten is withdrawn.

Pathogenesis

Gluten’s alcohol-soluble fraction, gliadin, contains a peptide that resists gastric, pancreatic and brush-border proteases. Two arms of immunity then attack the mucosa.

!Gliadin resists digestion -> tTG deamidates gliadin -> HLA-DQ2/DQ8 presents it -> CD4+ T cells: IFN-γ -> *Villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia !Gliadin resists digestion -> Enterocytes make IL-15 -> CD8+ IELs kill enterocytes -> *Villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia CD4+ T cells: IFN-γ -> ~B cells: anti-tTG, anti-endomysial

Genes: almost every patient carries HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8, but so do about 40% of healthy people: the HLA type is necessary but not sufficient. Its absence practically rules coeliac disease out. Associations: type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroiditis, Sjögren syndrome, IgA nephropathy, Down and Turner syndromes, selective IgA deficiency (use IgG-based tests).

  • Biopsy the second part of duodenum or proximal jejunum (most gluten exposure)
  • Flattened mucosa, scalloped or reduced folds, mosaic pattern
  • Changes are proximal and reversible on a gluten-free diet
  • Intraepithelial lymphocytosis (CD8+ T cells): the earliest change
  • Crypt hyperplasia with increased mitoses
  • Villous atrophy: partial to total flattening
  • Lamina propria: more plasma cells, mast cells, eosinophils

Lymphs up, crypts up, villi down

The three microscopic features of coeliac disease in the order they appear. Gluten grains: Barley, Rye, Wheat (“BRaW” bread).

Clinical forms

  • Children (6–24 months, after gluten weaning): irritability, abdominal distension, chronic diarrhoea, failure to thrive, muscle wasting
  • Adults (30–60 years): diarrhoea, bloating, fatigue, iron-deficiency anaemia; extra-intestinal: short stature, delayed puberty, aphthous ulcers, arthralgia, osteopenia
  • Silent: positive serology + atrophy, no symptoms; latent: positive serology, no atrophy
  • Dermatitis herpetiformis (about 10%): itchy blisters on extensor surfaces; IgA in dermal papillae; also responds to a gluten-free diet
  • Serology: IgA anti-tTG (most sensitive), IgA/IgG anti-deamidated gliadin; anti-endomysial (most specific). Confirm by biopsy and response to diet
  • Complications: enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma (EATL, the commonest coeliac-associated cancer, from IELs) and small-intestinal adenocarcinoma
  • Refractory coeliac disease: symptoms and atrophy persist despite a strict diet; investigate for lymphoma

Lactase (disaccharidase) deficiency

Lactase sits on the brush border. Undigested lactose stays in the lumen and holds water (osmotic diarrhoea); colonic bacteria ferment it to gas and acids, causing bloating, flatus and acidic stools after milk. The biopsy is normal; the defect is biochemical.

  • Congenital: autosomal recessive, rare; explosive frothy diarrhoea with the first milk feeds
  • Acquired: lactase gene switched off after childhood (common in Asian and African populations) or transient after viral or bacterial enteritis
  • Diagnosis: hydrogen breath test; treatment: avoid lactose

Abetalipoproteinaemia

A rare autosomal recessive disease: mutation of microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTTP) means enterocytes cannot load lipid onto apolipoprotein B, so no chylomicrons leave the cell.

  • Infancy: failure to thrive, diarrhoea, steatorrhoea
  • Biopsy: enterocytes with clear, lipid-vacuolated cytoplasm (oil red O positive)
  • Blood: absent apoB lipoproteins; acanthocytes (burr cells)
  • Deficiency of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K): retinal and spinocerebellar degeneration

Whipple disease

A rare systemic infection by Tropheryma whipplei, a Gram-positive actinomycete, usually in middle-aged men. Bacteria-laden macrophages pack the lamina propria and block the lacteals, so absorbed fat cannot leave (a lymphatic cause of malabsorption).

  • Small-bowel mucosa thick and shaggy; yellow flecks
  • Mesenteric nodes enlarged
  • Villi expanded and blunted by sheets of foamy macrophages in the lamina propria
  • Macrophages full of PAS-positive, diastase-resistant granules (bacteria in lysosomes); rod-shaped bacilli on EM
  • Dilated lacteals, fat droplets
Clinical: the triad of diarrhoea (malabsorption), weight loss and arthralgia, plus fever, lymphadenopathy, skin pigmentation, endocarditis and CNS disease (dementia). Similar macrophages fill joints, valves, nodes and brain. It responds to antibiotics.

Foamy Whipped cream in a CAN

Foamy PAS+ macrophages; Cardiac, Arthralgia, Neurological involvement.

Whipple vs Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) in AIDS: both fill the lamina propria with PAS-positive foamy macrophages. MAC bacilli are acid-fast positive (ZN); T. whipplei is acid-fast negative.

3.4 Inflammatory bowel disease

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic, relapsing condition caused by inappropriate mucosal immune activation against the normal gut flora in genetically susceptible people. Its two forms are Crohn disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC). Both usually begin in the teens and twenties; they are commonest in whites and in industrialised countries (the “hygiene hypothesis”), and rising wherever lifestyles westernise.

Pathogenesis

!Susceptibility genes -> Weak barrier, poor microbe handling -> Gut bacteria reach lamina propria -> Excess T-cell response -> *Cytokine injury: TNF, IFN-γ, IL-17 -> Weak barrier, poor microbe handling ~Smoking (CD), diet, microbiome -> Gut bacteria reach lamina propria
  • Genes (CD): NOD2, an intracellular sensor of bacterial peptidoglycan; loss-of-function variants let microbes survive in the mucosa. ATG16L1 and IRGM impair autophagy and Paneth-cell defence
  • Immune response: TH1 and TH17 cells drive CD (anti-TNF drugs work); UC may involve TH2-type cytokines (IL-13). Defects of IL-10 or its receptor cause severe infant colitis
  • Epithelium: leaky tight junctions; abnormal Paneth-cell antimicrobial granules
  • Smoking raises the risk of CD but lowers that of UC; UC may first appear after stopping smoking

Crohn disease

Can affect any part of the gut from mouth to anus, most often the terminal ileum, ileocaecal valve and caecum. At diagnosis about 40% have small-bowel disease alone, 30% small bowel and colon, and the rest colon only. Lesions are separated by normal bowel (skip lesions) and inflammation is transmural.

  • Sharply demarcated skip lesions
  • Earliest: aphthous ulcers → long serpentine, linear ulcers along the bowel axis
  • Cobblestone mucosa: islands of oedematous mucosa between ulcers
  • Fissures → fistulae, abscesses, perforation
  • Thick, rubbery wall → strictures (“string sign” on barium)
  • Creeping fat: mesenteric fat wraps the serosa
  • Neutrophils in crypts (crypt abscesses), crypt destruction, ulcers
  • Chronicity: distorted, branched crypts; pyloric and Paneth-cell metaplasia
  • Transmural inflammation with lymphoid aggregates
  • Non-caseating granulomas in about 35% (any layer, nodes); their absence does not exclude CD
  • Submucosal fibrosis, muscular hypertrophy
Clinical: intermittent mild diarrhoea, fever and abdominal pain; about a fifth present acutely with right lower quadrant pain mimicking appendicitis. Terminal ileal disease causes B12 and bile-salt malabsorption (gallstones, oxalate kidney stones), iron deficiency and hypoalbuminaemia. Fistulae run to bowel, bladder, vagina and perianal skin. Disease recurs after resection.

Ulcerative colitis

Confined to the colon and rectum. It always involves the rectum and spreads proximally in a continuous sheet (proctitis → left-sided colitis → pancolitis), and it is limited to the mucosa and superficial submucosa. Severe pancolitis may inflame the last few centimetres of ileum (backwash ileitis).

  • Continuous disease from the rectum; abrupt line where normal colon starts
  • Red, granular, friable mucosa; broad-based ulcers
  • Pseudopolyps: islands of regenerating mucosa; mucosal bridges
  • Wall not thickened, serosa normal, no strictures
  • Chronic: flat, atrophic mucosa without folds (“lead-pipe” colon)
  • Crypt abscesses, crypt distortion, Paneth-cell metaplasia
  • Inflammation limited to mucosa and superficial submucosa
  • No granulomas, no transmural inflammation
  • Healed disease: mucosal atrophy, submucosal fibrosis
Clinical: relapsing attacks of bloody diarrhoea with stringy mucus, lower abdominal cramps relieved by defecation. Toxic megacolon: inflammation damages the muscularis propria and its nerves, the colon dilates and may perforate. Colectomy cures the bowel disease (but not all extra-intestinal disease).

  • Joints: migratory polyarthritis, sacroiliitis, ankylosing spondylitis
  • Eyes: uveitis; skin: erythema nodosum, pyoderma gangrenosum; finger clubbing
  • Liver: primary sclerosing cholangitis, commoner with UC (and p-ANCA)

Colitis-associated neoplasia

The most feared long-term complication of UC and colonic Crohn disease. Dysplasia arises in chronically inflamed mucosa, is often flat and multifocal, and tends to acquire TP53 mutations early (the reverse of the APC-first sporadic pathway; see Chapter 4).

Risk rises with four things: (1) duration: it climbs sharply 8–10 years after onset; (2) extent: pancolitis carries more risk than left-sided disease; (3) severity of active (neutrophilic) inflammation; and (4) coexisting primary sclerosing cholangitis. Hence surveillance colonoscopy starts about 8 years after diagnosis, or at diagnosis if PSC is present.

3.5 The mimics: infective, pseudomembranous and tuberculous colitis

Time is written in the crypts. Acute infective colitis inflames the mucosa but leaves the crypt architecture intact. IBD has been injuring and repairing the mucosa for months, so it shows chronicity: branched, distorted crypts, basal plasma cells and metaplasia. A biopsy can therefore separate a first attack of dysentery from IBD.

Pseudomembranous colitis (Clostridioides difficile)

Antibiotics (classically third-generation cephalosporins, clindamycin, fluoroquinolones) wipe out the normal flora, and toxin-producing C. difficile overgrows. Its toxins A and B disrupt the epithelial cytoskeleton, causing barrier loss, cytokine release and cell death. Risk is highest in the elderly, hospitalised and immunosuppressed.

  • Colon studded with raised yellow-white plaques (pseudomembranes) that coalesce
  • Mucosa between plaques is relatively normal early
  • Surface epithelium denuded; lamina propria full of neutrophils, fibrin thrombi in capillaries
  • Damaged crypts distended by mucopurulent exudate that erupts like a volcano (“summit lesion”) and spreads into a pseudomembrane of mucin, fibrin and neutrophils
Clinical: fever, leukocytosis, cramps, profuse watery diarrhoea, dehydration, hypoalbuminaemia. Diagnosis: detect C. difficile toxin (or toxin genes by PCR) in stool. Treatment: oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin (metronidazole in mild cases); faecal microbiota transplant for recurrence. Pseudomembranes are not specific: ischaemia and other necrotising infections can produce them.

Intestinal tuberculosis: the great Crohn mimic

Bacilli reach the gut in swallowed sputum from pulmonary TB, in unpasteurised milk (M. bovis) or by the blood. The ileocaecal region is the favourite site because of its abundant lymphoid tissue and relative stasis. Forms: ulcerative, hypertrophic (a fibrotic ileocaecal mass that mimics carcinoma) and ulcerohypertrophic. Ulcers follow the lymphatics, which run around the bowel, so they are transverse (girdle) ulcers that heal by fibrosis into strictures.

Pakistan is among the top five high-TB-burden countries (incidence about 266 per 100,000), and abdominal tuberculosis is common. Before labelling a young adult with ileocaecal ulcers as Crohn disease, exclude TB (biopsy for caseation and AFB, GeneXpert, chest X-ray): steroids and anti-TNF drugs given for presumed Crohn disease can make TB disseminate. Remember also typhoid, for which Pakistan faced an extensively drug-resistant (XDR) outbreak in Sindh from 2016 and became the first country to add typhoid conjugate vaccine to routine immunisation (2019). Typhoid ulcers are oval and longitudinal over Peyer patches; TB ulcers are transverse.

A 24-year-old man from Peshawar has 4 months of right lower abdominal pain, colicky episodes after meals, low-grade evening fever and weight loss. Colonoscopy: transverse ulcers and a narrowed ileocaecal valve. Biopsy: confluent granulomas with central caseation and Langhans giant cells; a few acid-fast bacilli.

Reasoning: ileocaecal disease fits both Crohn disease and TB, but transverse ulcers + caseation + AFB settle it: ileocaecal tuberculosis with early stricture. Start antituberculous therapy; steroids would have been dangerous.

  2. Four phases: lumen, brush border, enterocyte, lymphatics.
  3. Normal D-xylose = intraluminal cause (pancreas, bile).
  4. Coeliac: gluten + HLA-DQ2/DQ8; tTG deamidates gliadin.
  5. Coeliac biopsy: IELs up, crypts up, villi down; proximal.
  6. Anti-tTG IgA most sensitive; anti-endomysial most specific.
  7. Coeliac cancers: EATL, small-bowel adenocarcinoma.
  8. Tropical sprue: whole small bowel; B12 + folate; antibiotics.
  9. Lactase deficiency: osmotic diarrhoea, normal biopsy, breath test.
  10. Abetalipoproteinaemia: MTTP, vacuolated enterocytes, acanthocytes.
  11. Whipple: PAS+ foamy macrophages; diarrhoea, weight loss, arthralgia.
  12. Crohn: skip, transmural, granulomas, fistulae, creeping fat.
  13. UC: rectum, continuous, mucosal, pseudopolyps, toxic megacolon.
  14. Colitis cancer risk: duration, extent, activity, PSC.
  15. TB ulcers transverse, caseating; typhoid ulcers longitudinal.
