21.1 Non-neoplastic cysts and polycystic ovary syndrome

Most ovarian “lumps” in young women are functional cysts: follicles or corpora lutea that failed to involute. They usually regress; their danger is rupture or torsion. (Endometriotic “chocolate” cysts: Chapter 20.)

  • Follicle cyst (commonest): unruptured Graafian follicle lined by granulosa/theca cells; thin wall, clear fluid, usually 1–1.5 cm; may make oestrogen.
  • Corpus luteum cyst: yellow luteinised lining, haemorrhagic contents; rupture causes haemoperitoneum that mimics ectopic pregnancy.
  • Theca lutein cysts: bilateral, multiple; luteinised theca cells driven by high hCG (mole, choriocarcinoma, multiple pregnancy, ovulation induction).

Polycystic ovary syndrome (Stein–Leventhal syndrome)

A common endocrine disorder of young women (about 6–10% of women of reproductive age) defined by hyperandrogenism, chronic anovulation (oligomenorrhoea, infertility) and polycystic ovaries; two of the three are enough (Rotterdam criteria). Many patients are obese and insulin-resistant, and they carry a higher risk of type 2 diabetes and endometrial carcinoma.

!Insulin resistance, hyperinsulinaemia -> *Theca cells overproduce androgens High LH : FSH ratio -> *Theca cells overproduce androgens *Theca cells overproduce androgens -> Hirsutism, acne *Theca cells overproduce androgens -> Follicles arrest: cysts, anovulation *Theca cells overproduce androgens -> Conversion to oestrone in fat -> !Unopposed oestrogen: endometrial hyperplasia

Morphology: ovaries twice normal size, grey-white, with a smooth thickened fibrotic cortex over many subcortical cysts 0.5–1.5 cm (“string of pearls” on ultrasound). The cysts are lined by granulosa cells over a hyperplastic, luteinised theca interna; corpora lutea are absent.

21.2 Classification of ovarian tumours

Ovarian tumours are classified by the tissue they resemble: the surface (Müllerian-type) epithelium, the germ cells, or the sex cord–stroma that makes hormones. A fourth group comes from elsewhere. Age is a strong clue: germ cell tumours dominate in children and young women, epithelial tumours after 40.

!Ovarian tumours -> *Surface epithelium: 65–70% -> Serous, mucinous, endometrioid, clear cell, Brenner !Ovarian tumours -> *Germ cells: 15–20% -> Teratoma, dysgerminoma, yolk sac, choriocarcinoma !Ovarian tumours -> *Sex cord–stroma: 5–10% -> Granulosa, thecoma–fibroma, Sertoli–Leydig !Ovarian tumours -> *Metastases: about 5% -> Krukenberg; Müllerian, breast, GI primaries

Risk factors for ovarian carcinoma

  • Nulliparity and family history (the two most important)
  • Germline BRCA1/BRCA2 (high-grade serous); Lynch syndrome
  • Endometriosis (endometrioid, clear cell)
  • Protective: long-term oral contraceptives, multiparity (fewer ovulations)

Why ovarian cancer is found late: a free-floating ovary causes only vague bloating, while malignant cells shed onto the peritoneum (omental cake, ascites). Most cases present in stage III–IV, making it the most lethal gynaecological cancer.

21.3 Surface epithelial tumours

Each epithelial type comes in three grades of behaviour: benign (cystadenoma), borderline (low malignant potential: epithelial proliferation and atypia but no stromal invasion) and malignant (cystadenocarcinoma, with stromal invasion). The more solid tissue and papillae in a cyst, the more likely it is malignant.

Serous tumours

The commonest ovarian epithelial tumours, with cells resembling fallopian tube epithelium (ciliated columnar). Most are benign, but serous carcinoma is the commonest malignant ovarian tumour. It follows two pathways. Low-grade serous carcinoma develops stepwise from cystadenoma and borderline tumour (KRAS, BRAF mutations; indolent). High-grade serous carcinoma, far commoner, begins as serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma (STIC) in the fimbria, carries TP53 mutations (plus BRCA1/2 in hereditary cases) and presents with peritoneal spread.

Mucinous tumours

Lined by tall columnar mucin-secreting cells without cilia, resembling endocervical or intestinal epithelium. Most are benign; KRAS mutations are common even in benign ones. They may grow to enormous size.

  • Large, multilocular cystic mass, often weighing kilograms
  • Smooth outer surface; loculi filled with sticky, gelatinous mucin
  • Usually unilateral (only about 5% bilateral)
  • Single layer of tall columnar cells with basal nuclei and apical mucin (no cilia)
  • Borderline: stratification, atypia, no invasion
  • Carcinoma: crowded atypical glands invading stroma

Pseudomyxoma peritonei (abdomen filled with jelly-like mucin) is usually spread from a mucinous tumour of the appendix, not a primary ovarian tumour. Likewise, bilateral mucinous ovarian tumours should make you look for a primary in the GI tract (stomach, colon, appendix, pancreas).

Other epithelial tumours. Endometrioid carcinoma: endometrium-like tubular glands; arises in endometriosis; PTEN, ARID1A, β-catenin mutations; 15–30% coexist with endometrial carcinoma. Clear cell carcinoma: clear glycogen-rich and hobnail cells; endometriosis, ARID1A loss. Brenner tumour: nests of transitional-type cells with grooved “coffee-bean” nuclei in dense fibrous stroma; almost always benign.

21.4 Germ cell tumours

These mirror the testicular germ cell tumours, but in the ovary the overwhelming majority are benign mature cystic teratomas. Malignant germ cell tumours occur in girls and young women and announce themselves with markers (AFP, hCG, LDH).

Mature (benign) cystic teratoma: dermoid cyst

The commonest ovarian germ cell tumour, usually in women of reproductive age. It contains mature tissues from all three germ layers, with ectoderm dominating, hence “dermoid”. It arises from an ovum after the first meiotic division, so it is 46,XX (genetically female). About 10–15% are bilateral.

  • Unilocular cyst with a wall like skin
  • Filled with sebaceous (cheesy) material and matted hair
  • Rokitansky protuberance (dermoid plug): a nodule in the wall containing teeth, bone, cartilage
  • Keratinised squamous epithelium with hair follicles, sebaceous and sweat glands
  • Mature neural (glial) tissue, cartilage, bone, respiratory and GI epithelium, thyroid
  • All tissues mature: no immature neuroepithelium
Complications: torsion (acute abdomen), rupture with chemical (granulomatous) peritonitis, infection, malignant transformation in about 1% (usually squamous cell carcinoma in older women), and paraneoplastic anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis from its neural tissue.
  • Immature (malignant) teratoma: adolescents (mean about 18 years); bulky, solid; graded by the amount of immature neuroepithelium (primitive neural tubules, rosettes).
  • Monodermal teratomas: struma ovarii (thyroid tissue; may cause hyperthyroidism) and ovarian carcinoid (carcinoid syndrome without liver metastases, as its venous blood bypasses the liver).

Dysgerminoma

The ovarian counterpart of the testicular seminoma. Rare overall (about 2% of ovarian cancers) but about half of malignant germ cell tumours; occurs in the second and third decades, sometimes with gonadal dysgenesis.

  • Usually unilateral, solid, often large
  • Soft, fleshy, grey-white to tan cut surface
  • Sheets and cords of large uniform cells with clear, glycogen-rich cytoplasm, central nuclei, prominent nucleoli
  • Thin fibrous septa infiltrated by lymphocytes; occasional granulomas
  • IHC: OCT3/4, NANOG, KIT (CD117), PLAP
Serum LDH raised (modest hCG if syncytiotrophoblast present). Malignant but highly radio- and chemosensitive: excellent outlook.

Yolk sac tumour (endodermal sinus tumour)

  • Second commonest malignant germ cell tumour (children, young women); secretes α-fetoprotein (AFP) and α1-antitrypsin; grows fast but is chemosensitive.
  • Micro: lacy (reticular) network of flattened to cuboidal cells; Schiller–Duval bodies (a central capillary wrapped by tumour cells, inside a space lined by tumour cells: a primitive glomerulus); PAS-positive hyaline droplets containing AFP.
  • Choriocarcinoma (hCG) and embryonal carcinoma occur mostly within mixed germ cell tumours.

21.5 Sex cord–stromal tumours and metastases

These copy the hormone-making cells around the oocyte (granulosa, theca, Sertoli, Leydig) or the stroma, so the clue is often hormonal: feminisation or virilisation.

Granulosa cell tumour

Most occur in postmenopausal women (adult type, with a FOXL2 mutation); a juvenile type affects girls. Many secrete oestrogen: precocious pseudopuberty in girls, and in older women endometrial hyperplasia or carcinoma and breast changes. All are potentially malignant and may recur many years later.

  • Unilateral, solid and cystic
  • Cut surface yellow (lipid) with haemorrhage
  • Small cuboidal to polygonal cells in cords, sheets, trabeculae
  • Call–Exner bodies: tiny follicle-like rosettes around eosinophilic fluid
  • Pale grooved “coffee-bean” nuclei; IHC and serum inhibin

Fibroma, thecoma and Meigs syndrome

  • Fibroma: commonest sex cord–stromal tumour; solid, hard, white; bland fibroblasts and collagen; hormonally inactive.
  • Thecoma: plump, lipid-laden spindle cells; yellow cut surface; may secrete oestrogen.
  • Meigs syndrome: ovarian fibroma + ascites + hydrothorax (usually right), which resolves after removal. Fibromas also occur in Gorlin (basal cell naevus) syndrome.

Sertoli–Leydig cell tumour

  • Young women; secretes androgens → defeminisation then virilisation (amenorrhoea, breast atrophy, hirsutism, clitoromegaly)
  • Tubules of Sertoli cells with Leydig cells in the stroma; DICER1 mutations in many

Metastases and Krukenberg tumour

  • Commonest metastases come from Müllerian sites (uterus, tube, peritoneum); non-Müllerian from breast and GI tract
  • Krukenberg tumour: bilateral ovarian metastases of mucin-filled signet-ring cells in a cellular stroma, classically from gastric carcinoma

21.6 Gestational trophoblastic disease

GTD is a spectrum of abnormal trophoblast proliferation: hydatidiform mole (benign) → invasive mole → choriocarcinoma (malignant), plus the rare placental site trophoblastic tumour. All make hCG, which is used to diagnose and follow them. Moles are commoner at the extremes of reproductive age and after a previous mole.
