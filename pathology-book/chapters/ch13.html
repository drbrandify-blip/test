13.1 Classifying leukaemias

White-cell neoplasms are named by where they live and how far they mature. A leukaemia arises in the marrow and spills into the blood; a lymphoma forms masses in lymph nodes or other tissues (Chapter 14). The line is blurred: one tumour can present either way (ALL vs lymphoblastic lymphoma; CLL vs SLL).

Leukaemia is a malignant, clonal proliferation of haematopoietic stem or progenitor cells that replaces the normal bone marrow and usually appears in large numbers in the peripheral blood.

Two questions name a leukaemia: which lineage (myeloid or lymphoid) and how mature (acute = blasts that cannot mature; chronic = cells that mature but proliferate to excess). Together they give the classic four: ALL, AML, CLL, CML. The WHO sorts myeloid neoplasms by what happens to maturation: AML (blocked; blasts ≥ 20%), MDS (disordered and ineffective → cytopenias) and MPN (normal, but production excessive → high counts: CML, PV, ET, PMF). Lymphoid neoplasms are covered in Chapter 14.

Risk factors

  • Inherited: Down syndrome, Fanconi anaemia, Bloom syndrome, ataxia–telangiectasia
  • Radiation, benzene, smoking
  • Chemotherapy: alkylating agents, topoisomerase II inhibitors → therapy-related AML/MDS
  • Viruses: HTLV-1 → adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma
  • Prior MDS or MPN → AML

Why does a leukaemic child bleed with a “normal” WBC? Symptoms come from what is missing. Blasts crowd out normal stem cells, so neutrophils, red cells and platelets fall. A normal count made of blasts protects nobody: read the smear.

13.2 Acute leukaemias: ALL and AML

An acute leukaemia needs two classes of mutation working together. One hits transcription factors that drive differentiation, so cells stay as blasts. The other switches on growth signals (tyrosine kinases, RAS), so blasts keep dividing. Cells that cannot mature and will not stop accumulate, and the marrow fails.

Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL)

ALL is a neoplasm of precursor B or T cells (lymphoblasts) that mainly involves the marrow and blood. When the same cells form a mass (usually thymic) with little marrow disease, it is called lymphoblastic lymphoma.

Who and why

  • Commonest cancer of childhood; peak about 3 years; about 85% are B-ALL
  • T-ALL: adolescent boys with a mediastinal (thymic) mass
  • B-ALL: loss-of-function mutations in B-cell transcription factors (PAX5, E2A, EBF); T-ALL: activating NOTCH1 mutations (over 70%)
  • Hyperdiploidy and t(12;21) ETV6–RUNX1 in children; t(9;22) BCR–ABL (Ph+) in about a quarter of adults

Clinical picture

  • Abrupt fatigue, fever, bleeding (marrow failure)
  • Bone pain and sternal tenderness (marrow expansion, subperiosteal infiltration)
  • Generalised lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, testicular swelling
  • CNS spread (headache, vomiting, cranial nerve palsies): commoner than in AML
  • T-ALL: mediastinal mass compresses SVC or airway
  • WBC high, normal or low; anaemia, thrombocytopenia
  • Lymphoblasts: small to medium, high N:C ratio
  • Condensed chromatin, inconspicuous nucleoli
  • Scant blue agranular cytoplasm; no Auer rods
  • Marrow hypercellular, packed with lymphoblasts
  • TdT positive (> 95%); MPO negative; PAS coarse block positivity
  • B-ALL: CD19, PAX5, CD10 (CALLA); T-ALL: CD3, CD7, CD1

Prognosis: good with age 2–10 years, low WBC, hyperdiploidy (trisomy 4, 7, 10), t(12;21) and rapid blast clearance; poor with age under 2 (often KMT2A/11q23 rearranged), adolescence or adulthood, WBC over 100,000/µL, t(9;22), hypodiploidy and minimal residual disease after induction. About 95% of children remit and 75–85% are cured; adults fare much worse. Drugs reach the CNS and testes poorly (“sanctuary sites”), so CNS-directed therapy is standard.

Acute myeloid leukaemia (AML)

AML is a neoplasm of myeloid progenitors in which at least 20% of the cells in the marrow (or blood) are myeloid blasts. Maturation is arrested, so blasts accumulate and normal haematopoiesis fails.

Mainly adults; incidence rises with age, peaking after 60. Mutations that block differentiation (RUNX1, CBFB and RARA fusions, NPM1) combine with mutations that drive growth (FLT3, KIT, RAS). Epigenetic genes (DNMT3A, TET2, IDH1/2) are often mutated too.

  • Myeloblasts: larger, delicate (fine) chromatin, 3–5 nucleoli
  • Fine azurophilic granules; Auer rods: red, needle-like, specific for myeloid lineage
  • Monoblasts: folded, lobulated nuclei; no Auer rods
  • Aleukaemic leukaemia: few or no blasts in the blood
  • Hypercellular; ≥ 20% blasts
  • MPO and Sudan black B positive; non-specific esterase (NSE) in monocytic types
  • CD13, CD33, CD117 (KIT), CD34; CD14, CD64 in monocytic types
  • Myeloid sarcoma (chloroma): a tissue mass of myeloblasts

Clinical features of AML

  • Marrow failure: anaemia, infections (bacterial and fungal), bleeding
  • DIC in APL
  • Monocytic types: gum hypertrophy, skin infiltration (leukaemia cutis)
  • Very high counts: leukostasis in lungs and brain; hyperuricaemia
  • Most adults remit, but only about 15–30% stay disease-free for 5 years; APL, t(8;21), inv(16) do best

Acute promyelocytic leukaemia (APL): the leukaemia that bleeds

  • t(15;17)(q24;q21) fuses PML with the retinoic acid receptor-α gene (RARA). The fusion protein represses genes needed for maturation, so cells stop at the promyelocyte stage.
  • Smear/marrow: abnormal promyelocytes with heavy azurophilic granules, often bilobed nuclei, and bundles of Auer rods (“faggot cells”).
  • The granules release tissue factor-like procoagulants and fibrinolytic activators → DIC, often present at diagnosis. Haemorrhage is the main early cause of death.
  • All-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) in high doses overrides the block and forces maturation; arsenic trioxide degrades PML–RARA. Most patients are now cured.

Three classic traps. (1) A normal or low WBC does not exclude acute leukaemia (aleukaemic leukaemia): examine the marrow. (2) Auer rods prove a myeloid blast, but monoblasts lack them, so their absence does not exclude AML. (3) Down syndrome raises the risk of both: acute megakaryoblastic leukaemia (M7) in children under 5, ALL in older children.

13.3 Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL)

CLL is a neoplasm of small, mature-looking B cells involving the blood, marrow and lymphoid organs, diagnosed when the absolute count of clonal B lymphocytes in the blood exceeds 5000/µL. When the same tumour lives mainly in lymph nodes it is called small lymphocytic lymphoma (SLL): one disease, two addresses.

Who and why

  • Commonest leukaemia of adults in Western countries; less common in Asia
  • Median age about 60; men twice as often as women
  • Deletion of 13q14 removes miR-15/16, which normally restrain BCL2 → BCL2 overexpression; also del(11q) (ATM), del(17p) (TP53), trisomy 12
  • Survival signals flow through the B-cell receptor via BTK (the target of ibrutinib)

Clinical picture

  • Often asymptomatic; found on a routine count
  • Fatigue, weight loss; generalised painless lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly
  • Hypogammaglobulinaemia → bacterial infections
  • Warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and immune thrombocytopenia
  • Richter transformation to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma: a rapidly enlarging mass, poor outlook
  • Lymphocytosis (> 5000/µL, often far higher)
  • Small round lymphocytes with dense clumped chromatin and scant cytoplasm
  • Many smudge (basket) cells: fragile cells crushed during smearing
  • A few larger prolymphocytes
  • Node diffusely effaced by small lymphocytes
  • Pale proliferation centres of prolymphocytes and paraimmunoblasts: pathognomonic
  • Marrow, splenic pulp and hepatic portal tracts infiltrated

CLL accumulates; it does not race. BCL2 keeps the cells alive long after they should have died, but they divide slowly: hence the indolent course (many patients live over 10 years) and the success of the BCL2 inhibitor venetoclax.

Immunophenotype: CD19+, CD20 dim, CD5+, CD23+, dim surface Ig with one light chain. Mantle cell lymphoma is CD5+ but CD23− and cyclin D1+. Bad markers: del(17p)/TP53, del(11q), unmutated IGHV, ZAP-70, CD38.

13.4 Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN)

The MPN idea. The stem cell carries a constitutively active tyrosine kinase, so it overproduces without waiting for growth factors, but maturation is normal: the blood fills with mature cells, the opposite of AML. Four shared features follow: (1) raised counts of one or more lineages; (2) extramedullary haematopoiesis → hepatosplenomegaly; (3) a late “spent phase” of marrow fibrosis with falling counts; (4) transformation to acute leukaemia (common in CML, uncommon in the rest). High cell turnover adds hyperuricaemia and gout.

Chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML)

CML is an MPN of the pluripotent haematopoietic stem cell defined by the BCR–ABL fusion gene, usually created by the balanced translocation t(9;22)(q34;q11). The shortened chromosome 22 is the Philadelphia (Ph) chromosome.

!t(9;22): ABL joins BCR -> BCR–ABL fusion gene -> *Tyrosine kinase always on -> Growth-factor-independent proliferation -> !Granulocytosis, big spleen *Tyrosine kinase always on -[blocked by]-> ~Imatinib (TKI)

Adults aged 25–60 (peak in the 40s and 50s). BCR–ABL drives growth without blocking maturation, so the blood contains every stage of granulocyte. A later hit that does block maturation produces blast crisis. Onset is insidious: fatigue, weight loss, anorexia and a dragging sensation from a massive spleen; sudden left-upper-quadrant pain means splenic infarction.

  • WBC usually > 100,000/µL
  • Full spectrum of granulocytes; myelocytes and neutrophils dominate (“myelocyte bulge”)
  • Blasts usually < 10% (chronic phase)
  • Basophilia and eosinophilia; platelets often high
  • Mild normocytic anaemia; LAP score low
  • Marrow almost 100% cellular: granulocytic and megakaryocytic hyperplasia, high M:E ratio
  • Small (“dwarf”) megakaryocytes; sea-blue histiocytes (pseudo-Gaucher cells)
  • Spleen massive: red-pulp extramedullary haematopoiesis, infarcts
  • Reticulin fibrosis increases with time

About half of untreated patients pass through an accelerated phase; the rest enter blast crisis abruptly. Imatinib and later BCR–ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitors give lasting remissions in most patients but do not eradicate the stem cell; resistance can arise from ABL kinase mutations (e.g. T315I). Allogeneic stem-cell transplantation can cure.

A leukaemoid reaction is a reactive, non-neoplastic leukocytosis so high (usually > 50,000/µL) or so immature that it mimics leukaemia: severe infection and sepsis, tuberculosis, burns, severe haemolysis or haemorrhage, and marrow infiltration by cancer.

LAP or BCR–ABL? Many teachers still expect the leukocyte alkaline phosphatase (LAP) score (low in CML, high in a leukaemoid reaction). Write it, but add that the definitive test is BCR–ABL by karyotype (Ph chromosome), FISH or PCR; PCR also monitors response to imatinib.

The JAK2 family: PV, ET and primary myelofibrosis

JAK2 is the tyrosine kinase behind the receptors for erythropoietin (EPO), thrombopoietin (TPO) and G-CSF. The V617F mutation leaves it permanently on, so precursors grow without their growth factors; CALR and MPL mutations act on the same pathway.

!JAK2 V617F (or CALR, MPL) -> *JAK–STAT signalling always on -> Growth without EPO or TPO Growth without EPO or TPO -> PV: red cells (+ WBC, platelets) Growth without EPO or TPO -> ET: platelets Growth without EPO or TPO -> PMF: atypical megakaryocytes -> PDGF, TGF-β: marrow fibrosis -> Extramedullary haematopoiesis: huge spleen

One Philadelphia cousin, three JAK2 sisters. CML alone has BCR–ABL. PV makes Packed red cells, ET makes Excess platelets, PMF makes Fibrosis. JAK2: nearly all PV, about half of ET and PMF.

Polycythaemia vera (PV)

An MPN with marked overproduction of red cells, usually with raised granulocytes and platelets too (panmyelosis). Precursors grow without EPO, so serum EPO is low: the key difference from secondary polycythaemia.

  • Hb and haematocrit high (haematocrit may exceed 60%); WBC and platelets raised; basophilia
  • Marrow hypercellular (all three lineages); large, clustered megakaryocytes
  • Moderate splenomegaly (extramedullary haematopoiesis); congested organs
  • Late spent phase: post-polycythaemic myelofibrosis; transformation to AML is uncommon
  • Older adults: plethora, ruddy cyanosis, headache, dizziness, hypertension (hyperviscosity)
  • Itching after a warm bath and peptic ulcer: histamine from basophils
  • Gout from high cell turnover
  • Thrombosis (stroke, MI, DVT, hepatic vein: Budd–Chiari) and, paradoxically, bleeding
  • Phlebotomy with low-dose aspirin: median survival over 10 years

A raised haematocrit has three explanations. Relative: plasma volume is low (dehydration, “stress” polycythaemia); red cell mass is normal. Secondary: EPO is high, from hypoxia (high altitude, COPD, cyanotic heart disease, heavy smoking) or an EPO-secreting tumour (renal cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, cerebellar haemangioblastoma). Primary (PV): EPO is low, JAK2 is mutated, and WBC, platelets and spleen are usually raised too.

Essential thrombocythaemia (ET)

An MPN of megakaryocytes with a persistent platelet count above 450,000/µL. The marrow shows increased, large, hyperlobated megakaryocytes in clusters, with little fibrosis. Often asymptomatic; some have thrombosis, bleeding or erythromelalgia (burning hands and feet from platelet plugs in arterioles, relieved by aspirin). A diagnosis of exclusion: first rule out reactive thrombocytosis (iron deficiency, infection, inflammation, cancer, splenectomy) and other MPNs. Indolent, with long survival.

Primary myelofibrosis (PMF)

An MPN in which obliterative marrow fibrosis develops early and dominates. The fibroblasts are not neoplastic: they respond to PDGF and TGF-β released by neoplastic megakaryocytes. As the marrow scars, haematopoiesis moves to the spleen, liver and nodes.

  • Normocytic anaemia
  • Leukoerythroblastosis: nucleated red cells + immature granulocytes
  • Teardrop cells (dacrocytes): squeezed out of a scarred marrow
  • Large abnormal platelets; basophilia
  • Aspiration gives a dry tap
  • Diffuse reticulin, then collagen, fibrosis; marrow becomes hypocellular
  • Clusters of atypical megakaryocytes with cloud-like nuclei; dilated sinusoids
  • Late new bone formation (osteosclerosis)
Clinical: usually over 60; progressive anaemia and massive splenomegaly (fullness, early satiety, splenic infarcts); fatigue, weight loss, night sweats, gout. Median survival is about 3–5 years and some patients transform to AML. JAK2 inhibitors (ruxolitinib) shrink the spleen and relieve symptoms; only stem-cell transplantation cures.

13.5 Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS)

MDS is a group of clonal stem-cell disorders with dysplastic, ineffective haematopoiesis: the marrow is usually hypercellular, yet the blood shows cytopenias, because many precursors die in the marrow. Blasts are below 20%, and the risk of transformation to AML is high.

Primary MDS affects older adults (median about 70); therapy-related MDS follows alkylating drugs or radiation by 2–8 years and does worse. Mutations hit epigenetic regulators (TET2, DNMT3A), RNA-splicing genes (SF3B1: ring sideroblasts) and TP53 (bad); typical karyotypes are monosomy 5 or 7, del(5q), del(7q), trisomy 8. Patients have weakness, infections and bleeding; about 10–40% progress to AML and median survival is short (a few years at most). The 5q− syndrome of older women does better and responds to lenalidomide.

  • Cytopenias; oval macrocytes
  • Pseudo-Pelger–Huët neutrophils (bilobed), hypogranular neutrophils
  • Giant, abnormal platelets
  • Ring sideroblasts (Prussian blue): iron-laden mitochondria ringing the nucleus
  • Megaloblastoid erythroid precursors, nuclear budding
  • Micromegakaryocytes; blasts < 20%

Leukaemia smears in 10 seconds. (1) Mostly blasts? Acute leukaemia: look for Auer rods (AML) or small agranular blasts with scant cytoplasm (ALL). (2) Every stage of granulocyte plus basophils? CML. (3) Sheets of small mature lymphocytes and smudge cells in an older adult? CLL. (4) Teardrops and nucleated red cells? Marrow fibrosis or infiltration. (5) Left shift with toxic granulation and Döhle bodies? A leukaemoid reaction, not leukaemia.

Leukaemia, mainly ALL, is the commonest childhood cancer in Pakistan, as it is worldwide. A child with fever, pallor, bone pain and bruising needs a blood count and smear review before being treated empirically for typhoid or malaria. Cousin marriage is common, which raises the risk of autosomal recessive marrow-failure syndromes such as Fanconi anaemia, a recognised predisposition to AML.

OSPE Practice Stations
Station 1 · Peripheral smear2 minutes
Task

A 5-year-old: Hb 6.8 g/dL, WBC 58,000/µL, platelets 22,000/µL. Most cells are small blasts with scant agranular cytoplasm, condensed chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli; no Auer rods. (a) Diagnosis? (b) Two tests to confirm the lineage. (c) One favourable genetic finding.

Answer

(a) Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia. (b) TdT positive; flow cytometry CD19/CD10 (B) or CD3/CD7 (T); MPO negative. (c) t(12;21) ETV6–RUNX1 or hyperdiploidy.

Station 2 · Smear + coagulation data2 minutes
Task

An adult with gum bleeding. Platelets 20,000/µL; PT 21 s (control 13); aPTT 52 s (control 32); fibrinogen 80 mg/dL; D-dimer markedly raised. Smear: large cells with dense purple granules, bilobed nuclei and bundles of rod-like inclusions. (a) Diagnosis? (b) What are the inclusions? (c) Interpret the coagulation profile.

Answer

(a) Acute promyelocytic leukaemia, t(15;17). (b) Auer rods (faggot cells). (c) DIC: consumption of platelets and factors (long PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen) with fibrinolysis (high D-dimer).

Station 3 · Peripheral smear2 minutes
Task

A 45-year-old with a massive spleen; WBC 186,000/µL. Granulocytes at every stage, myelocytes and neutrophils most numerous; basophils and eosinophils increased; platelets 510,000/µL. (a) Diagnosis? (b) Confirmatory test. (c) One drug and its target.

Answer

(a) Chronic myeloid leukaemia, chronic phase. (b) Karyotype for the Ph chromosome, or FISH/PCR for BCR–ABL. (c) Imatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor of BCR–ABL.

RAPID REVISION The Night-Before Page

15 one-liners

  1. Acute = blasts that cannot mature; chronic = mature cells that cannot stop.
  2. AML needs ≥ 20% myeloid blasts in marrow or blood.
  3. ALL: children, TdT+, PAS block, MPO−, CNS and testis relapse.
  4. T-ALL: adolescent boy with a mediastinal mass; NOTCH1.
  5. Childhood ALL: t(12;21) and hyperdiploidy good; Ph+ and age under 2 bad.
  6. AML: fine chromatin, 3–5 nucleoli, Auer rods, MPO+.
  7. APL: t(15;17) PML–RARA, faggot cells, DIC; ATRA + arsenic.
  8. Monocytic AML (M4/M5): gums and skin, NSE+.
  9. t(8;21) and inv(16): good-prognosis AML.
  10. CLL: elderly, smudge cells, CD5+ CD23+, proliferation centres.
  11. CLL complications: infections, AIHA, Richter (DLBCL).
  12. CML: t(9;22) BCR–ABL, myelocyte bulge, basophils, huge spleen.
  13. CML vs leukaemoid: low vs high LAP; basophils; Ph chromosome.
  14. PV: JAK2 V617F, low EPO, pruritus, thrombosis.
  15. PMF: teardrops, leukoerythroblastosis, dry tap; MDS: ring sideroblasts.

Memory hooks

  • “3 bleeds, 4–5 chew, 7 has Down”: M3 DIC, M4/M5 gums, M7 Down syndrome
  • One Philadelphia cousin, three JAK2 sisters: CML vs PV, ET, PMF
  • PV packed cells · ET excess platelets · PMF fibrosis
  • CLL accumulates; it does not race (BCL2)
