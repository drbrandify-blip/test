15.1 Normal haemostasis: a recap

Haemostasis keeps blood fluid inside normal vessels yet seals a breach within minutes. Three players act in sequence: the vessel wall, the platelets (primary haemostasis: the plug) and the coagulation cascade (secondary haemostasis: fibrin). Every bleeding disorder fails at one of these steps, and every screening test examines one of them.

What each test measures (normal values vary by laboratory: compare with the control)

  • Platelet count (150,000–450,000/µL): spontaneous bleeding below about 20,000; post-traumatic bleeding at 20,000–50,000
  • Bleeding time (2–9 min): the platelet plug: platelet number and function, vWF, vessel wall
  • PT (about 11–14 s; INR about 1): VII + common pathway (X, V, II, fibrinogen); monitors warfarin
  • aPTT (about 25–40 s): XII, XI, IX, VIII + common pathway; monitors heparin
  • Thrombin time: fibrinogen → fibrin only; long with low fibrinogen (normal 200–400 mg/dL), heparin, FDPs
  • Mixing study: corrects with normal plasma → factor deficiency; fails → inhibitor. D-dimer rises in DIC and thrombosis

Play Tennis outside, Play Table Tennis inside: PT = extrinsic, PTT = intrinsic.

“1972”: vitamin K-dependent factors X, IX, VII, II (plus proteins C and S).

Why do haemophiliacs bleed if TF–VIIa starts clotting? In the body TF–VIIa makes only a small “spark” of thrombin before it is shut off. The fire is kept alight by TF–VIIa activating factor IX and by the IXa–VIIIa complex making Xa on platelets. Without VIII or IX the spark never becomes a fire, though the test-tube PT stays normal.

15.3 Vascular (non-thrombocytopenic) purpuras

Common and usually not serious. The vessel wall or its supporting tissue is weak or inflamed, so blood leaks into skin and mucosa as petechiae and ecchymoses, yet the platelet count and clotting tests are normal.

  • Infections (meningococcaemia, infective endocarditis, rickettsiae) and drug reactions: vasculitis
  • Scurvy: faulty collagen hydroxylation → fragile capillaries; perifollicular haemorrhages, bleeding gums
  • Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (defective collagen); Cushing syndrome, steroids and senile purpura (lost perivascular support)
  • Henoch–Schönlein purpura (IgA vasculitis): palpable purpura on buttocks and extensor legs, abdominal pain, arthralgia, IgA nephritis (Chapters 16, 25)
  • Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia: autosomal dominant (ENG, ACVRL1); thin dilated vessels → epistaxis, GI bleeding
  • Amyloidosis: perivascular amyloid → fragile vessels (periorbital purpura)

Palpable vs flat purpura. Palpable = an inflamed vessel (vasculitis: HSP, drug reaction). Flat with normal tests = a fragile vessel (scurvy, steroids, old age). Flat petechiae with a low platelet count = thrombocytopenia.

15.4 Thrombocytopenia

A platelet count below 150,000/µL, with bleeding from small vessels: petechiae, epistaxis, gum and GI bleeding and, most feared, intracranial haemorrhage. Causes: decreased production (aplastic anaemia, leukaemia, marrow metastases, drugs, alcohol, megaloblastic anaemia, HIV); immune destruction (ITP, drugs such as quinine, sulfonamides and heparin, SLE, CLL, post-transfusion); non-immune consumption (DIC, TTP/HUS, prosthetic valves); sequestration (hypersplenism); and dilution (massive transfusion).

Immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP)

ITP is thrombocytopenia caused by autoantibodies against platelet membrane glycoproteins, most often GpIIb/IIIa or GpIb–IX; splenic macrophages remove the coated platelets. It is primary, or secondary to SLE, HIV or B-cell neoplasms such as CLL.

  • The spleen both makes the antibodies and destroys the platelets, so splenectomy helps most patients with chronic ITP
  • Spleen: normal size; congested sinusoids, prominent germinal centres
  • Marrow: increased megakaryocytes, many large and immature
  • Smear: few but large platelets; red and white cells normal
  • Splenomegaly or lymphadenopathy are not features: look for another cause

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia clots, it does not bleed. About 1–2 weeks after starting heparin, IgG antibodies bind platelet factor 4–heparin complexes and activate platelets: the count falls, but the danger is venous and arterial thrombosis. Stop all heparin and use a non-heparin anticoagulant.

Thrombotic microangiopathies: TTP and HUS

Both give thrombocytopenia + microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) from platelet-rich thrombi in small vessels, which slice red cells into schistocytes. The clotting cascade is not activated, so PT and aPTT are normal: the key difference from DIC.

!ADAMTS13 deficiency (autoantibody, inherited) -> Ultra-large vWF multimers persist -> Platelets aggregate in microvessels -> *Hyaline platelet thrombi *Hyaline platelet thrombi -> Thrombocytopenia *Hyaline platelet thrombi -> MAHA: schistocytes *Hyaline platelet thrombi -> Ischaemia: brain, kidney, heart

TTP: the classic pentad of fever, thrombocytopenia, MAHA, transient neurological deficits and renal failure (all five are uncommon together). Usually adult women with an acquired autoantibody against ADAMTS13, the protease that trims vWF multimers; rarely inherited. Fatal if untreated; plasma exchange (removes antibody, supplies ADAMTS13) saves most patients.

HUS: acute renal failure dominates. Typical: children after bloody diarrhoea from E. coli O157:H7, whose Shiga-like toxin injures endothelium. Atypical: inherited defects of complement regulators (factor H, CD46, factor I), drugs, radiation. ADAMTS13 is normal.

15.5 Defects of platelet function

The count is normal or near normal, but the platelets fail, so the bleeding time is prolonged. Inherited defects hit adhesion (Bernard–Soulier), aggregation (Glanzmann) or secretion (storage-pool disorders). Acquired defects are far commoner: aspirin irreversibly blocks cyclo-oxygenase, so no TxA2 is made for the platelet’s whole life (7–10 days); uraemia impairs adhesion, secretion and aggregation.

Cousin marriage is common in Pakistan, which raises the frequency of autosomal recessive disease. 15.6 Coagulation factor disorders

Haemophilia A and B

Haemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency) is the commonest hereditary cause of serious bleeding. It is X-linked recessive, so males are affected and carrier females are usually well; about 30% have no family history (new mutations), and an intron 22 inversion causes many severe cases. Severity follows the factor level: below 1% severe, 2–5% moderate, 6–50% mild. Haemophilia B (Christmas disease, factor IX) is also X-linked and clinically identical; only a factor assay separates them.

  • Easy bruising; massive bleeding after trauma or surgery (circumcision, tooth extraction)
  • Spontaneous haemarthroses (knees, ankles, elbows) → crippling joint deformity; muscle haematomas
  • Petechiae are characteristically absent
  • aPTT prolonged; PT, BT and platelets normal; low factor assay; mixing corrects
  • Factor VIII concentrate; about 15% of severe cases develop inhibitory antibodies

von Willebrand disease (vWD)

The commonest inherited bleeding disorder (about 1% in some surveys, mostly mild). von Willebrand factor, a large multimer made by endothelium and megakaryocytes, glues platelet GpIb to subendothelial collagen and carries and stabilises factor VIII. So vWD gives mucosal bleeding (epistaxis, menorrhagia, bleeding after dental work) with a long BT, plus a long aPTT when VIII falls. Type 1 (about 70%): dominant, partial deficiency, mild. Type 2 (about 25%): qualitative (2A loses large multimers). Type 3: recessive, near-absent vWF, haemophilia-like. Labs: low vWF antigen and ristocetin cofactor activity. Desmopressin releases stored vWF from endothelial Weibel–Palade bodies (type 1).

Vitamin K deficiency and liver disease

Vitamin K is the cofactor for γ-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX and X (and proteins C and S), which lets them bind calcium and phospholipid; warfarin blocks its recycling. PT rises first (factor VII has the shortest half-life), then aPTT; platelets are normal; injected vitamin K corrects it. Causes: newborns (poor placental transfer, sterile gut, little in breast milk → vitamin K deficiency bleeding, prevented by vitamin K at birth); fat malabsorption (obstructive jaundice, coeliac disease); broad-spectrum antibiotics with poor intake; warfarin or rat-poison overdose.

In liver disease almost every factor falls (VIII, partly endothelial, does not), so PT/INR and aPTT are prolonged; the INR is a sensitive marker of synthetic function. Platelets fall from hypersplenism and alcohol, and activated factors are cleared poorly, so a DIC-like picture can develop.

The vitamin K test separates two jaundiced patients. In obstructive jaundice the liver is healthy but cannot absorb vitamin K: inject it and the PT corrects within a day. In cirrhosis the liver cannot make the factors: the PT stays long. Factor VIII then separates liver disease (normal or high) from DIC (low).

15.7 Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

DIC is an acquired thrombohaemorrhagic disorder, always secondary to another disease, in which widespread activation of coagulation forms microthrombi throughout the microcirculation. Platelets and clotting factors are consumed and fibrinolysis is activated, so the patient both clots and bleeds (“consumption coagulopathy”).

Two triggers

  • Tissue factor enters the blood: obstetric accidents (abruptio placentae, retained dead fetus, septic abortion, amniotic fluid embolism, eclampsia); acute promyelocytic leukaemia; mucin-secreting adenocarcinoma (pancreas, lung, stomach, prostate); trauma, burns, surgery; snake venom
  • Widespread endothelial injury: sepsis (gram-negative endotoxin, meningococcaemia), immune-complex vasculitis, heat stroke, shock

STOMP out clotting: Sepsis · Trauma, burns · Obstetric accidents · Malignancy (APL, adenocarcinoma) · Poisons (snake venom)

!Sepsis, obstetric, cancer, trauma -> Tissue factor release / endothelial injury -> *Widespread coagulation activation *Widespread coagulation activation -> Microthrombi in small vessels -> Ischaemic organ damage, MAHA *Widespread coagulation activation -> Consumption of platelets, fibrinogen, V, VIII -> !Bleeding *Widespread coagulation activation -> Plasmin: fibrinolysis -> FDPs inhibit platelets and thrombin -> !Bleeding
  • Petechiae, ecchymoses; oozing from venepuncture and surgical sites
  • Kidneys: bilateral renal cortical necrosis
  • Adrenals: massive haemorrhage in meningococcaemia (Waterhouse–Friderichsen)
  • Pituitary: postpartum necrosis (Sheehan syndrome)
  • Fibrin microthrombi in arterioles and capillaries: glomeruli, brain, lungs, heart, adrenals, placenta
  • Microinfarcts and haemorrhages around them
  • Smear: schistocytes, few platelets
Clinical: acute DIC (obstetric, sepsis, trauma) is dominated by bleeding, with shock, oliguria, dyspnoea, coma; chronic DIC (cancer) by thrombosis. Labs: platelets ↓; PT, aPTT, TT ↑; fibrinogen ↓; D-dimer ↑↑; schistocytes. Treatment: remove the trigger (deliver the placenta, antibiotics, ATRA in APL); give platelets, fresh frozen plasma, cryoprecipitate.
