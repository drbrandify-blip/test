15.1 Normal haemostasis: a recap

Haemostasis keeps blood fluid inside normal vessels yet seals a breach within minutes. Three players act in sequence: the vessel wall, the platelets (primary haemostasis: the platelet plug) and the coagulation cascade (secondary haemostasis: fibrin). Each bleeding disorder is a failure at one of these steps, and each laboratory test examines one of them.

What each test measures

  • Platelet count: number of platelets
  • Bleeding time (BT): the platelet plug: platelet number and function, vWF, vessel wall
  • Prothrombin time (PT): extrinsic + common pathways: VII, X, V, II, fibrinogen; reported as INR for warfarin
  • aPTT: intrinsic + common pathways: XII, XI, IX, VIII, X, V, II, fibrinogen; monitors heparin
  • Thrombin time (TT): fibrinogen → fibrin only; long with low or abnormal fibrinogen, heparin, FDPs
  • Mixing study: patient + normal plasma. Corrects → factor deficiency; fails → inhibitor

Play Tennis outside, Play Table Tennis inside: PT = extrinsic, PTT = intrinsic. “1972”: vitamin K-dependent factors X, IX, VII, II (plus proteins C and S).

If tissue factor and VIIa start clotting, why do haemophiliacs bleed? In the body, TF–VIIa makes only a small “spark” of thrombin before tissue factor pathway inhibitor shuts it off. The fire is kept alight by TF–VIIa activating factor IX, and by the IXa–VIIIa complex generating factor Xa on the platelet surface. Without VIII or IX the spark never becomes a fire, so the PT (a test-tube shortcut) stays normal while the patient bleeds.

15.3 Vascular (non-thrombocytopenic) purpuras

Common, usually not serious. The vessel wall or its supporting tissue is weak or inflamed, so blood leaks into skin and mucosa as petechiae and ecchymoses, yet the platelet count and clotting tests are normal.

  • Infections: meningococcaemia, infective endocarditis, rickettsiae (vasculitis, sometimes DIC)
  • Drug reactions: immune-complex (hypersensitivity) vasculitis
  • Scurvy (vitamin C deficiency): faulty hydroxylation of collagen → fragile capillaries; perifollicular haemorrhages, corkscrew hairs, bleeding gums
  • Ehlers–Danlos syndrome: defective collagen
  • Cushing syndrome, long-term steroids and senile purpura: loss of perivascular support
  • Henoch–Schönlein purpura (IgA vasculitis): IgA immune complexes in vessels and glomeruli; palpable purpura on buttocks and extensor legs, colicky abdominal pain, arthralgia, IgA nephritis (Chapters 16, 25)
  • Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia: autosomal dominant (ENG, ACVRL1); dilated thin-walled vessels → epistaxis, GI bleeding
  • Amyloidosis: perivascular amyloid → fragile vessels (periorbital purpura)

Palpable vs flat purpura. Palpable purpura means an inflamed vessel (vasculitis: HSP, drug reaction). Flat purpura with normal tests means a fragile vessel (scurvy, steroids, old age). Flat petechiae with a low platelet count mean thrombocytopenia.

15.4 Thrombocytopenia

A platelet count below 150,000/µL. Bleeding is mainly from small vessels: petechiae, epistaxis, gum and GI bleeding, and, most feared, intracranial haemorrhage.

Immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP)

ITP is thrombocytopenia caused by autoantibodies against platelet membrane glycoproteins, most often GpIIb/IIIa or GpIb–IX. Antibody-coated platelets are removed by splenic macrophages. It may be primary, or secondary to SLE, HIV or B-cell neoplasms such as CLL.

  • The spleen both makes the antibodies and destroys the platelets, so splenectomy helps most patients with chronic ITP
  • Spleen: normal size; congested sinusoids, prominent germinal centres
  • Marrow: increased megakaryocytes, many large and immature: production is trying to keep up
  • Smear: few but large platelets; red and white cells normal
  • Splenomegaly or lymphadenopathy are not features: look for another cause

About a fifth of children, usually those without a viral prodrome, go on to chronic ITP.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) clots, it does not bleed. About 1–2 weeks after starting heparin, IgG antibodies bind platelet factor 4–heparin complexes and activate platelets. The count falls, but the danger is venous and arterial thrombosis. Stop all heparin and use a non-heparin anticoagulant; do not give platelets.

Thrombotic microangiopathies: TTP and HUS

Both cause thrombocytopenia + microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) from widespread platelet-rich thrombi in small vessels; red cells are sliced into schistocytes (helmet cells) as they pass the thrombi. The clotting cascade is not activated, so PT and aPTT are normal: the key difference from DIC.

!ADAMTS13 deficiency (autoantibody, inherited) -> Ultra-large vWF multimers persist -> Platelets aggregate in microvessels -> *Hyaline platelet thrombi *Hyaline platelet thrombi -> Thrombocytopenia *Hyaline platelet thrombi -> MAHA: schistocytes *Hyaline platelet thrombi -> Ischaemia: brain, kidney, heart

TTP: classically the pentad of fever, thrombocytopenia, MAHA, transient neurological deficits and renal failure (the full pentad is uncommon). Usually adult women with an acquired autoantibody against ADAMTS13, the protease that trims vWF multimers; rarely inherited. Untreated it is fatal; plasma exchange (removing antibody, supplying ADAMTS13) saves most patients.

HUS: acute renal failure dominates. Typical HUS: children (and the elderly) after bloody diarrhoea from E. coli O157:H7, whose Shiga-like toxin injures endothelium. Atypical HUS: inherited defects of complement regulators (factor H, CD46, factor I), drugs, radiation. ADAMTS13 is normal.

15.5 Defects of platelet function

The count is normal (or near normal) but the platelets do not work, so the bleeding time is prolonged. Inherited defects hit adhesion (Bernard–Soulier), aggregation (Glanzmann) or secretion (storage-pool disorders). Acquired defects are far commoner: aspirin irreversibly blocks cyclo-oxygenase, so no TxA2 is made for the platelet’s whole life (7–10 days); uraemia causes a complex defect of adhesion, secretion and aggregation.

Cousin marriage is common in Pakistan, which raises the frequency of autosomal recessive disease. Rare recessive bleeding disorders, such as Glanzmann thrombasthenia, Bernard–Soulier syndrome, type 3 von Willebrand disease and recessive factor deficiencies (VII, X, XIII), are therefore seen more often than in populations where such marriages are rare. Ask about consanguinity and affected siblings in every child who bleeds.

15.6 Coagulation factor disorders

Haemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency)

The commonest hereditary disease causing serious bleeding. X-linked recessive: affects males; carrier females are usually symptom-free. About 30% have no family history (new mutations); an inversion in intron 22 of the factor VIII gene causes many severe cases. Severity follows the factor level: below 1% severe, 2–5% moderate, 6–50% mild.

  • Easy bruising; massive bleeding after trauma or surgery (circumcision, tooth extraction)
  • Spontaneous haemarthroses (knees, ankles, elbows) → progressive joint deformity
  • Muscle haematomas; intracranial bleeding
  • Petechiae are characteristically absent (platelets are normal)
  • aPTT prolonged; PT, BT and platelet count normal
  • Low factor VIII assay; mixing study corrects
  • Treatment: factor VIII concentrate; about 15% of severe cases develop inhibitory antibodies
  • Haemophilia B (Christmas disease, factor IX): X-linked, clinically identical; told apart only by factor assay

von Willebrand disease (vWD)

The commonest inherited bleeding disorder (about 1% of people in some surveys, mostly mild). von Willebrand factor, a large multimer made by endothelium and megakaryocytes, has two jobs: it glues platelets (GpIb) to subendothelial collagen, and it carries and stabilises factor VIII. So vWD gives a platelet-type bleeding pattern plus, when VIII falls, a long aPTT.

  • Type 1 (about 70%): autosomal dominant, partial quantitative deficiency; mild
  • Type 2 (about 25%): qualitative defect (type 2A loses the large multimers)
  • Type 3: autosomal recessive, near-absent vWF and very low VIII; haemophilia-like haemarthroses
  • Epistaxis, gum bleeding, menorrhagia, bleeding from wounds and after dental work
  • BT prolonged, platelets normal, aPTT normal or prolonged; low vWF antigen and ristocetin cofactor activity
  • Desmopressin releases stored vWF from endothelial Weibel–Palade bodies (type 1); vWF concentrates for severe types

Vitamin K deficiency

Vitamin K is the cofactor for γ-carboxylation of glutamate residues on factors II, VII, IX and X (and proteins C and S); without it they cannot bind calcium and phospholipid. Warfarin blocks the enzyme that recycles vitamin K. PT rises first (factor VII has the shortest half-life), then aPTT; platelets are normal; parenteral vitamin K corrects it.

  • Newborns: little placental transfer, sterile gut, low vitamin K in breast milk → vitamin K deficiency bleeding; prevented by vitamin K at birth
  • Fat malabsorption: obstructive jaundice, coeliac disease, pancreatic insufficiency
  • Broad-spectrum antibiotics + poor diet (gut flora make vitamin K2)
  • Warfarin overdose; rat poison

Liver disease

The liver makes almost every clotting factor (factor VIII also comes from endothelium), so in cirrhosis or acute liver failure the PT (INR) and aPTT are prolonged; the INR is a sensitive marker of liver synthetic function. Platelets fall from hypersplenism and alcohol; fibrinogen may be low or abnormal; activated factors are cleared poorly, so a DIC-like picture can develop.

The vitamin K test separates two jaundiced patients. In obstructive jaundice the liver is healthy but cannot absorb vitamin K: give it by injection and the PT corrects within a day. In cirrhosis the liver cannot make the factors whatever you give: the PT stays long. Factor VIII separates liver disease (VIII normal or high) from DIC (VIII low).

15.7 Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

DIC is an acquired thrombohaemorrhagic disorder, always secondary to another disease, in which widespread activation of coagulation forms microthrombi throughout the microcirculation. Platelets and clotting factors are consumed and fibrinolysis is activated, so the patient both clots and bleeds (“consumption coagulopathy”).

Two triggers

  • Tissue factor enters the blood: obstetric accidents (abruptio placentae, retained dead fetus, septic abortion, amniotic fluid embolism, eclampsia); cancers (acute promyelocytic leukaemia, mucin-secreting adenocarcinoma of pancreas, lung, stomach, prostate); trauma, burns, major surgery; snake venom
  • Widespread endothelial injury: sepsis (gram-negative endotoxin, meningococcaemia), immune-complex vasculitis, heat stroke, burns, shock

STOMP out clotting: Sepsis · Trauma and burns · Obstetric accidents · Malignancy (APL, adenocarcinoma) · Poisons (snake venom)

!Sepsis, obstetric, cancer, trauma -> Tissue factor release / endothelial injury -> *Widespread coagulation activation *Widespread coagulation activation -> Microthrombi in small vessels -> Ischaemic organ damage, MAHA *Widespread coagulation activation -> Consumption of platelets, fibrinogen, V, VIII -> !Bleeding *Widespread coagulation activation -> Plasmin: fibrinolysis -> FDPs inhibit platelets and thrombin -> !Bleeding
  • Petechiae and ecchymoses everywhere; oozing from venepuncture and surgical sites
  • Kidney: bilateral renal cortical necrosis
  • Adrenals: massive haemorrhage in meningococcaemia (Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome)
  • Pituitary: postpartum necrosis (Sheehan syndrome)
  • Fibrin microthrombi in arterioles and capillaries: glomeruli, brain, lungs, heart, adrenals, placenta
  • Microinfarcts and haemorrhages around them
  • Lungs: alveolar capillary thrombi, hyaline membranes
  • Smear: schistocytes, few platelets
Clinical: acute DIC (obstetric, sepsis, trauma) is dominated by bleeding, with shock, oliguria, dyspnoea and coma; chronic DIC (cancer, retained dead fetus) is dominated by thrombosis (e.g. migratory thrombophlebitis). Labs: platelets ↓, PT ↑, aPTT ↑, TT ↑, fibrinogen ↓, D-dimer/FDPs ↑↑, schistocytes. Treatment: remove the trigger (deliver the placenta, antibiotics, ATRA in APL); replace platelets, fresh frozen plasma and cryoprecipitate; heparin only in selected thrombotic forms.

  13. vWD: commonest inherited; long BT ± aPTT; desmopressin.

  15. DIC: low platelets and fibrinogen, long PT/aPTT, high D-dimer.
