27.1 Bone remodelling: the balance that every disease tips

Adult bone is constantly resorbed by osteoclasts (multinucleate, derived from monocyte–macrophage precursors) and rebuilt by osteoblasts (mesenchymal), which lay down osteoid (type I collagen) that is then mineralised with calcium hydroxyapatite. Osteoblasts trapped in matrix become osteocytes. About 10% of the skeleton is remodelled each year.

!Osteoblast / stromal cell -> RANKL -> RANK on osteoclast precursor -> *Mature osteoclast: resorption !Osteoblast / stromal cell -> ~OPG: soluble decoy -[binds, blocks]-> RANKL

One switch explains many diseases. Anything that raises RANKL or lowers OPG makes osteoclasts win: PTH, IL-1, TNF and glucocorticoids raise RANKL; oestrogen and Wnt/β-catenin signalling raise OPG. That is why menopause, steroids, myeloma, metastases and rheumatoid arthritis all erode bone, and why denosumab (anti-RANKL antibody) treats osteoporosis and giant cell tumour.

27.2 Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is an acquired condition of reduced bone mass (increased porosity) that makes bone fragile and prone to fracture. The bone present is normal in composition and mineralisation; there is simply too little of it. WHO definition on DEXA: bone mineral density T-score ≤ –2.5 (osteopenia: –1 to –2.5).

Primary

  • Postmenopausal (oestrogen loss): rapid loss, mainly of trabecular bone
  • Senile (age-related): slower loss of trabecular and cortical bone in both sexes

Secondary

  • Endocrine: hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, Cushing syndrome, hypogonadism, type 1 diabetes
  • Drugs: corticosteroids (commonest secondary cause), heparin, anticonvulsants, alcohol
  • GIT: malnutrition, malabsorption, liver disease, vitamin C/D deficiency
  • Others: immobilisation, multiple myeloma, carcinomatosis, anaemia
~Low peak bone mass (genes, low calcium) -> !Less bone than needed Menopause: oestrogen falls -> IL-1, IL-6, TNF rise -> *RANKL up, OPG down -> Osteoclasts outpace osteoblasts -> !Less bone than needed Ageing -> Fewer, sluggish osteoblasts; less activity -> Osteoclasts outpace osteoblasts !Less bone than needed -> Fragility fractures
  • Whole skeleton affected, but unevenly
  • Postmenopausal: vertebral bodies (cancellous) → crush (compression) fractures, loss of height, kyphosis (dowager’s hump)
  • Senile: thin cortex → femoral neck and Colles fractures
  • Trabeculae thinned, perforated and lose interconnections
  • Cortex thinned by endosteal and subperiosteal resorption; widened Haversian canals (cortex looks cancellous in severe cases)
  • Mineral content normal; no excess osteoid
Clinical: silent until a fracture. X-rays show osteopenia only after 30–40% of bone is lost, so DEXA is used. Serum calcium, phosphate and ALP are normal. Hip fractures bring immobility, pneumonia and pulmonary embolism. Treatment: calcium, vitamin D, exercise, bisphosphonates, denosumab.

27.3 Paget disease of bone (osteitis deformans), in brief

A disorder of excessive but chaotic remodelling that gives bone that is thicker yet weaker. Middle-aged and elderly, commonest in people of British descent. Familial cases often carry SQSTM1 mutations that increase NF-κB activity in osteoclasts; a slow paramyxovirus infection has been suspected but not proven. About 85% have polyostotic disease (skull, pelvis, spine, femur). It runs through three phases: osteolytic (huge osteoclasts with up to 100 nuclei) → mixed osteoclastic–osteoblastic → burnt-out osteosclerotic (thick, dense, disordered bone).

Hallmark microscopy: a mosaic (“jigsaw-puzzle”) pattern of lamellar bone with haphazard cement lines. Labs: very high ALP, raised urinary hydroxyproline, normal calcium and phosphate.

Clinical: bone pain, enlarging skull (hats no longer fit), deafness from nerve compression, bowed tibia, chalk-stick fractures, secondary osteoarthritis, high-output heart failure. Osteosarcoma develops in about 1%.

27.4 Rickets and osteomalacia

Rickets (children) and osteomalacia (adults) are disorders of defective mineralisation of osteoid, usually from vitamin D deficiency or abnormal vitamin D metabolism. In children the growth plates are also affected, which produces the deformities of rickets.

!Skin: 7-dehydrocholesterol + UVB -> Vitamin D3 -> Liver: 25-hydroxylation -> Kidney: 1α-hydroxylase -> *1,25(OH)2D: active hormone Diet: fish, fortified food -> Vitamin D3 *1,25(OH)2D: active hormone -> Gut absorbs Ca and PO4; bone mineralised

Causes (follow the pathway)

  • Too little made or eaten: poor sunlight, covered clothing, dark skin, poor diet
  • Malabsorption: coeliac disease, biliary obstruction, pancreatic insufficiency
  • Liver disease (25-hydroxylation); anticonvulsants (faster breakdown)
  • Renal disease (1α-hydroxylation): renal osteodystrophy; vitamin D-dependent rickets
  • Phosphate wasting: X-linked hypophosphataemic rickets (PHEX → high FGF23)

Why the bone softens. Low vitamin D → less gut calcium → low serum calcium → secondary hyperparathyroidism. PTH restores calcium by resorbing bone but makes the kidney waste phosphate. With a low calcium × phosphate product, new osteoid cannot be mineralised. In a child the cartilage of the growth plate also fails to calcify, so it piles up in irregular masses.

  • Skull: craniotabes (soft, “ping-pong” occiput), frontal bossing, squared head, delayed fontanelle closure and dentition
  • Chest: rachitic rosary (overgrown cartilage at costochondral junctions), pigeon breast, Harrison sulcus
  • Limbs: widened wrists and ankles; bowed legs once walking; lumbar lordosis
  • Growth plate: overgrown, irregular cartilage projecting into marrow; disorderly replacement by osteoid
  • Osteoid deposited on unmineralised cartilage remnants; overgrowth of capillaries and fibroblasts
  • Adults: excess unmineralised osteoid seams around trabeculae (seen in undecalcified sections)
Osteomalacia: diffuse bone pain, proximal myopathy (waddling gait), fractures of vertebrae and femoral neck, and Looser zones: narrow radiolucent bands at right angles to the cortex (pubic rami, ribs, scapula). Labs: low/normal calcium, low phosphate, high ALP, high PTH, low 25-hydroxyvitamin D.

Vitamin D deficiency is very common in Pakistan despite abundant sunshine: covered clothing, indoor lives and poor diets keep skin synthesis and intake low. Rickets is still seen in children, and osteomalacia in women, particularly after repeated pregnancies and long breastfeeding. Bone pain with a raised ALP in a young woman should prompt a vitamin D level before any other diagnosis.

27.5 Osteomyelitis

Osteomyelitis is inflammation of bone and marrow, almost always due to infection. It may be acute or chronic. Organisms reach bone by the blood (commonest), by spread from a contiguous focus, or by direct implantation (compound fracture, surgery).

Tuberculous osteomyelitis and Pott disease

Complicates about 1–3% of tuberculosis, usually by blood spread in childhood. Favourite sites: spine (thoracic and lumbar vertebrae), knees and hips. It is more destructive and harder to control than pyogenic infection. Pott disease: caseating infection crosses the intervertebral discs to involve several vertebrae, causing collapse, angular kyphosis (gibbus), cord compression (paraplegia) and a cold psoas abscess tracking to the groin. Micro: caseating epithelioid granulomas with Langhans giant cells.

Pakistan is among the top five high-TB-burden countries, with an incidence of roughly 266 per 100,000. Back pain with fever, weight loss and a gibbus in a young adult is spinal TB until proven otherwise; GeneXpert on aspirated pus confirms it and detects rifampicin resistance.

27.6 Bone tumours

Three facts frame every bone tumour question. (1) Metastases are far commoner than primary bone tumours, and among primaries myeloma is commonest (Chapter 21). (2) Benign tumours outnumber malignant ones, especially under 40; in the elderly a bone tumour is more likely malignant. (3) Age + site + radiograph narrow the diagnosis before the biopsy: many tumours arise around the knee, where the growth plates are busiest.

Classification by the matrix the tumour makes (benign | malignant). Bone-forming: osteoid osteoma, osteoblastoma | osteosarcoma. Cartilage-forming: osteochondroma, enchondroma, chondroblastoma | chondrosarcoma. Uncertain origin: giant cell tumour (locally aggressive), aneurysmal bone cyst | Ewing sarcoma. Haematopoietic: myeloma, lymphoma.

Bone-forming tumours

Osteoid osteoma: < 2 cm, in the cortex of femur or tibia; teens and twenties, males. Severe pain, worse at night and relieved by aspirin/NSAIDs because the tumour makes PGE₂. X-ray: small lucent nidus in dense reactive sclerosis. Treated by radiofrequency ablation.

Osteoblastoma: same histology but > 2 cm, in the posterior elements of vertebrae; dull ache not relieved by aspirin; little sclerosis. Micro (both): interlacing trabeculae of woven bone rimmed by plump osteoblasts in loose vascular stroma.

Osteosarcoma

Osteosarcoma is a malignant mesenchymal tumour in which the cancer cells produce bone matrix (osteoid). It is the commonest primary malignant tumour of bone after myeloma and lymphoma.

  • Age: bimodal. About 75% under 20 years (growth spurt); a second peak in the elderly with Paget disease, bone infarcts or prior radiation. Males slightly more.
  • Site: metaphysis of long bones; about 50% around the knee (distal femur, proximal tibia), then proximal humerus.
  • X-ray: large destructive mixed lytic–blastic mass with infiltrative margins; tumour lifts the periosteum to form Codman triangle; spicules give a sunburst pattern.
  • Labs: raised ALP.

RB loss: children with hereditary retinoblastoma have a vastly increased risk. TP53: Li–Fraumeni syndrome; mutated in many sporadic tumours. CDKN2A (p16, p14ARF) inactivation. MDM2 and CDK4 amplification, typical of low-grade (parosteal) tumours. Most tumours are sporadic.

  • Bulky, gritty, grey-white tumour with haemorrhage and cystic change
  • Destroys the cortex and forms a soft-tissue mass; spreads along the medullary canal
  • Rarely crosses the growth plate or enters the joint
  • Pleomorphic cells: large hyperchromatic nuclei, bizarre tumour giant cells, many and atypical mitoses
  • Neoplastic bone: coarse or fine lace-like osteoid made directly by malignant cells (the diagnostic feature)
  • Malignant cartilage may be present; vascular invasion, necrosis
Behaviour: painful, enlarging mass; sometimes a pathological fracture. Spreads by blood, chiefly to the lungs: 10–20% have pulmonary metastases at diagnosis. With neoadjuvant chemotherapy and surgery, 5-year survival is about 60–70% without overt metastases.

Cartilage-forming tumours

Osteochondroma (exostosis)

A benign cartilage-capped outgrowth attached to the underlying bone by a bony stalk: the commonest benign bone tumour. About 85% are solitary; the rest occur in multiple hereditary exostoses (autosomal dominant, germline EXT1 or EXT2 loss with a second hit; these genes make the enzymes for heparan sulfate). Late adolescence and early adulthood; men about 3 times more often. Arise from the metaphysis near the growth plate of long bones, especially the knee, and point away from the joint. They stop growing when the plate closes. Malignant change to chondrosarcoma is rare, commoner in the hereditary form.

  • Sessile or pedunculated, 1–20 cm
  • Bluish-white cartilage cap over a bony stalk
  • Cortex and medulla continuous with those of the host bone (X-ray key)
  • Cap of benign hyaline cartilage covered by perichondrium
  • Deep cartilage undergoes enchondral ossification, forming the bony head and stalk
  • Marrow space of the stalk merges with host marrow

Enchondroma (chondroma): benign hyaline cartilage nodule inside the medullary cavity, usually of the small tubular bones of the hands and feet; 20–50 years; often an incidental finding or a pathological fracture. Gross: grey-blue, translucent, < 3 cm; micro: well-circumscribed lobules of hyaline cartilage with bland chondrocytes. X-ray: lucency with ring-like calcification and intact cortex. IDH1/IDH2 mutations. Multiple: Ollier disease; with soft-tissue haemangiomas: Maffucci syndrome; both carry a risk of chondrosarcoma.

Chondroblastoma: rare benign cartilage tumour of the epiphysis in adolescents, while the growth plate is still open; sheets of chondroblasts with “chicken-wire” calcification.

Chondrosarcoma: malignant tumour that makes cartilage (not osteoid). Patients ≥ 40 years, men more; axial skeleton: pelvis, shoulder girdle, ribs (rare in hands and feet, unlike enchondroma). Conventional type about 90%; may arise from enchondroma or osteochondroma. Gross: large, lobulated, grey-white, translucent, glistening, myxoid or gelatinous with spotty calcification. Micro: malignant cartilage permeating marrow and trapping native trabeculae. Grade decides prognosis: grade 1 rarely metastasises; grade 3 often spreads to the lungs.

Ewing sarcoma

A malignant small round cell tumour of bone and soft tissue: the second commonest bone sarcoma in children; about 80% of patients are under 20. Arises in the medulla of the diaphysis of long bones (femur) and in the pelvis. Fever, raised ESR, anaemia and a warm, tender swelling mimic osteomyelitis. X-ray: lytic, permeative lesion with onion-skin layers of periosteal new bone.

t(11;22)(q24;q12) in about 85%: EWSR1–FLI1 fusion, an aberrant transcription factor. Rest: EWSR1–ERG and others. IHC: CD99 membrane positivity, FLI1.

  • Soft, tan-white tumour filling the medulla, breaking through cortex into soft tissue
  • Haemorrhage and necrosis
  • Sheets of uniform small round cells, slightly larger than lymphocytes, scant stroma
  • Scant clear cytoplasm rich in glycogen (PAS+)
  • Homer–Wright rosettes (neural differentiation)
  • Chemotherapy + surgery/radiation: 5-year survival about 75%; chemotherapy-induced necrosis predicts outcome

Giant cell tumour of bone (osteoclastoma)

A benign but locally aggressive tumour of adults aged 20–40 years. The neoplastic cells are mononuclear osteoblast precursors that express high levels of RANKL; they recruit and activate the non-neoplastic osteoclast-type giant cells that destroy bone. Most carry a mutation in the histone gene H3F3A. It is the classic epiphyseal tumour of adults (after plate closure), around the knee and distal radius, extending to the subchondral plate (arthritis-like pain).

X-ray: large, purely lytic, eccentric lesion expanding into a thin shell of reactive bone: “soap-bubble”. Behaviour: recurs after curettage in up to 40–60%; rare lung “metastases”. Denosumab shrinks it.

  • Large, red-brown, haemorrhagic, often cystic
  • Thin bony shell; may abut articular cartilage
  • Sheets of uniform oval mononuclear cells (the tumour) with mitoses
  • Evenly scattered osteoclast-type giant cells with up to 100 or more nuclei, nuclei resembling those of the stromal cells
  • Haemorrhage, haemosiderin, reactive bone

Metastatic tumours

The commonest malignancy of bone. Adults: prostate, breast, kidney, lung, thyroid; children: neuroblastoma, Wilms tumour. They seek red marrow: vertebrae, pelvis, ribs, skull, proximal femur. Most are lytic (tumour PTHrP and cytokines raise RANKL); prostate is classically blastic; breast often mixed. Expect pain, fractures, hypercalcaemia, multiple lesions.
