28.1 Osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis (OA), or degenerative joint disease, is the commonest joint disease. Its essential feature is degeneration of articular cartilage; the structural and functional failure of the joint follows. Synovial inflammation, if any, is secondary and mild.

  • Primary (idiopathic): appears with ageing, often in a few joints, without obvious cause. Weight-bearing joints and hands: knees, hips, lower lumbar and cervical spine, DIP and PIP joints, first carpometacarpal and first MTP joints.
  • Secondary: at any age in a previously damaged joint: trauma, developmental deformity, obesity, diabetes, haemochromatosis, ochronosis, previous inflammatory arthritis.
  • Clinical: after 50; deep aching pain worse with use, short morning stiffness (< 30 min), crepitus, limited movement. Heberden nodes (DIP) and Bouchard nodes (PIP) are osteophytes, prominent in women. No systemic features.
~Age, load, obesity, genes -> Chondrocyte injury -> Early: chondrocytes proliferate, proteoglycan falls Early: chondrocytes proliferate, proteoglycan falls -> IL-1, TNF, nitric oxide -> *MMPs degrade matrix -> Fibrillation, cartilage loss -> !Bone-on-bone: eburnation, osteophytes
  • Cartilage soft, granular, fibrillated, then lost in patches
  • Exposed subchondral bone polished like ivory: eburnation
  • Osteophytes: mushroom-shaped bony spurs at the joint margins
  • Loose bodies (“joint mice”): dislodged cartilage and bone fragments
  • Early: clusters (clones) of proliferating chondrocytes, loss of proteoglycan
  • Fibrillation: vertical and horizontal clefts in the matrix
  • Thickened sclerotic subchondral bone; subchondral cysts with fibrous walls (synovial fluid forced through cracks)
  • Synovium: mild, secondary congestion and fibrosis

28.2 Rheumatoid arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic systemic autoimmune disease that mainly attacks joints, producing a non-suppurative proliferative and inflammatory synovitis that often progresses to destruction of articular cartilage and ankylosis. It affects about 1% of people, women 3–5 times more than men, usually in middle age.

~HLA-DRB1 shared epitope, PTPN22 -> !Loss of tolerance to citrullinated proteins ~Smoking, infection: citrullination -> !Loss of tolerance to citrullinated proteins !Loss of tolerance to citrullinated proteins -> CD4 TH1 + TH17 cells; anti-CCP, RF -> *TNF, IL-1, IL-6, IL-17 -> Synovitis + pannus: MMPs -> Cartilage destroyed *TNF, IL-1, IL-6, IL-17 -> RANKL: osteoclasts -> Bone erosions

Why anti-TNF works. In RA the inflamed synovium itself becomes the destroyer. TNF and IL-1 from macrophages make synovial cells release metalloproteinases that digest cartilage, while RANKL on activated T cells and synovial fibroblasts recruits osteoclasts that erode bone at the joint margin. Block TNF and both arms slow down, which is why TNF antagonists transformed the disease.

  • Symmetric polyarthritis: small joints of hands and feet first (MCP, PIP), then wrists, ankles, elbows, knees; DIP usually spared; cervical spine (atlanto-axial subluxation)
  • Synovium swollen, thick, with villous projections
  • Pannus: a mass of inflamed synovium, granulation tissue and fibroblasts that creeps over and erodes the cartilage
  • Late: fibrous then bony ankylosis; ulnar deviation of fingers, radial deviation of wrist, swan-neck and boutonnière deformities
  • Synovial cell hyperplasia
  • Dense infiltrate of CD4+ T cells, B cells, plasma cells and macrophages, often forming lymphoid follicles
  • Increased vascularity (angiogenesis)
  • Fibrin on the synovial surface; neutrophils in the fluid
  • Osteoclastic activity in underlying bone: marginal erosions, subchondral cysts, periarticular osteoporosis
Rheumatoid nodule (about a quarter of patients, usually severe, RF-positive disease): firm, non-tender subcutaneous nodule over pressure points (extensor forearm, elbow, occiput). Micro: central fibrinoid necrosis surrounded by a palisade of epithelioid macrophages and an outer rim of lymphocytes, plasma cells and granulation tissue.

Extra-articular disease

  • Lung: interstitial fibrosis, pleuritis, nodules (Caplan syndrome with pneumoconiosis)
  • Pericarditis, vasculitis (skin ulcers, neuropathy), scleritis, Sjögren syndrome
  • Felty syndrome: RA + splenomegaly + neutropenia
  • Anaemia of chronic disease; secondary (AA) amyloidosis
Anti-CCP (ACPA) about 70% of patients; highly specific; may appear years before symptoms.
Rheumatoid factor IgM (or IgA) autoantibody against the Fc of IgG; about 80%, but not specific.
X-ray effusion, juxta-articular osteopenia, marginal erosions, joint-space narrowing.

28.3 Seronegative spondyloarthropathies and infectious arthritis

Spondyloarthropathies are RF-negative arthritides in which inflammation starts at the entheses (where ligaments and tendons attach to bone) rather than in the synovium. They involve the sacroiliac joints and spine, are linked to HLA-B27, and are often triggered by infection.

Suppurative (septic) arthritis

Bacteria reach the joint by blood (commonest), from adjacent osteomyelitis or by direct inoculation. A medical emergency: enzymes from neutrophils destroy cartilage within days.

  • Organisms: H. influenzae under 2 years; S. aureus in older children and adults; gonococcus in adolescents and young adults (complement C5–C9 deficiency predisposes to disseminated disease); Salmonella in sickle cell disease
  • Usually one joint, the knee commonest: sudden hot, swollen, painful joint, fever, leukocytosis
  • Joint aspiration is diagnostic: purulent fluid, organisms

Tuberculous arthritis

A chronic, progressive, monoarticular arthritis, from adjacent tuberculous osteomyelitis or blood spread. Weight-bearing joints: hips, knees, ankles; spine as Pott disease (Chapter 27).

  • Insidious pain, swelling, low-grade fever, weight loss
  • Micro: synovium with caseating granulomas; a pannus of granulation tissue destroys cartilage and bone
  • Heals with fibrous ankylosis and obliteration of the joint space

Synovial fluid at a glance. Normal: clear, viscous, WBC < 200/µL. Non-inflammatory (OA): clear yellow, WBC < 2,000/µL. Inflammatory (RA, gout, pseudogout): cloudy, low viscosity, WBC 2,000–50,000/µL, mostly neutrophils; look for crystals. Septic: turbid or purulent, WBC usually > 50,000/µL with > 75% neutrophils, low glucose, Gram stain and culture positive.

28.4 Gout and pseudogout

Gout is a disease of recurrent attacks of acute arthritis caused by monosodium urate (MSU) crystals deposited in and around joints, leading in time to chronic gouty arthritis and to masses of urate called tophi. Hyperuricaemia (plasma urate > 6.8 mg/dL) is essential but not sufficient: most hyperuricaemic people never develop gout.

Causes of hyperuricaemia

  • Primary gout (about 90%): usually reduced renal excretion of urate; less often overproduction. Rare enzyme defects: partial HGPRT deficiency (complete = Lesch–Nyhan syndrome), PRPP synthetase overactivity
  • Secondary (about 10%): high cell turnover (leukaemia, myeloproliferative disease, chemotherapy: tumour lysis), chronic renal disease, drugs (thiazides, low-dose aspirin), alcohol, lead, von Gierke disease
  • Risk of attacks: age (men after 30, women after menopause), alcohol, obesity, purine-rich meals, genes
!Hyperuricaemia -> MSU crystals form in cool joints -> Phagocytosed by macrophages -> *NLRP3 inflammasome: caspase-1 -> IL-1β released -> Neutrophils flood the joint -> !Acute gouty arthritis !Acute gouty arthritis -[repeated attacks]-> Tophi, chronic arthritis
  • Acute: red, hot, exquisitely painful joint, classically the first MTP joint (podagra): about half of first attacks, most patients eventually
  • Chronic tophaceous: chalky urate crust on cartilage, thick fibrotic synovium, pannus, juxta-articular punched-out erosions, ankylosis; on average about 12 years after the first attack
  • Tophi in ear helix, olecranon bursa, Achilles tendon, fingers; may ulcerate and discharge chalky material
  • Kidney: urate in medullary interstitium (gouty nephropathy), uric acid stones
  • Acute: dense neutrophilic infiltrate in synovium and fluid; long, slender, needle-shaped crystals, many inside neutrophils
  • Tophus (pathognomonic): amorphous mass of urate crystals surrounded by macrophages, lymphocytes and foreign-body giant cells
  • Polarised light: crystals are strongly negatively birefringent (yellow when parallel to the compensator axis)
  • Fix in alcohol: urate dissolves in aqueous formalin
Natural history: asymptomatic hyperuricaemia → acute attacks (sudden, often at night, after alcohol or a feast) → symptom-free intercritical periods → chronic tophaceous gout. Treat attacks with NSAIDs, colchicine or steroids; prevent with xanthine oxidase inhibitors (allopurinol, febuxostat) or uricosurics.

Pseudogout (calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate, CPPD, crystal deposition disease) usually begins after 50 years. Crystals form first in articular cartilage, menisci and discs (chondrocalcinosis) when pyrophosphate accumulates in the matrix; they later shed into the joint and provoke inflammation. It is sporadic (age), hereditary (ANKH mutation) or secondary to joint damage, hyperparathyroidism, haemochromatosis, hypomagnesaemia, hypothyroidism or diabetes. On H&E the deposits are oval blue-purple aggregates.

28.5 Skeletal muscle: neurogenic or myopathic?

A muscle biopsy answers one question first: is the nerve or the muscle fibre diseased? Normal muscle shows a checkerboard mix of type 1 (slow, oxidative) and type 2 (fast, glycolytic) fibres, because one motor neuron supplies fibres scattered through the fascicle.

Why denervation makes groups. When a motor neuron dies, its scattered fibres shrink. Surviving neighbouring axons sprout and adopt them, converting them to their own fibre type, so the checkerboard becomes patches of one type (type grouping). If that adopting neuron later dies too, the whole patch shrinks together: grouped atrophy. Disuse and steroids cause a different, non-specific pattern: type 2 fibre atrophy.

Pakistan and Afghanistan remain the last countries with endemic wild poliovirus type 1. Polio destroys anterior horn motor neurons, so the paralysed muscle shows classic neurogenic atrophy. Every missed oral polio vaccine dose keeps this textbook picture alive in Pakistani wards.

28.6 Muscle diseases

Muscular dystrophies: Duchenne and Becker

Muscular dystrophies are inherited diseases of progressive muscle fibre degeneration with replacement by fibrous and fatty tissue. Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is the commonest and most severe: X-linked, about 1 in 3,500 live male births. The DMD gene at Xp21 is one of the largest human genes, so new mutations are frequent; deletions account for about two-thirds.

!DMD gene mutation (Xp21) -> No dystrophin (DMD) or faulty dystrophin (BMD) -> Sarcolemma tears on contraction -> Ca influx, fibre necrosis -> *Regeneration exhausted -> !Fibrosis + fat: weakness

Frame decides fate. Dystrophin anchors actin to the dystroglycan–sarcoglycan complex and the basement membrane, like a shock absorber. An out-of-frame deletion scrambles the code beyond it, so no dystrophin is made: Duchenne. An in-frame deletion yields a shorter but partly working dystrophin: Becker, milder and later.

  • Marked variation in fibre size: small and hypertrophic rounded fibres, fibre splitting
  • Necrosis, myophagocytosis and basophilic regenerating fibres; internal nuclei
  • Endomysial fibrosis; late, extensive fatty replacement
  • Immunostain: dystrophin absent (DMD) or reduced/patchy (BMD)

Myotonic dystrophy

The commonest muscular dystrophy of adults; autosomal dominant. A CTG trinucleotide repeat expansion in the 3′ untranslated region of the DMPK gene (chromosome 19q) makes a toxic RNA that traps splicing regulators, so many proteins, including a muscle chloride channel, are mis-spliced. Repeats lengthen in successive generations: anticipation (congenital form when transmitted by the mother). Myotonia: sustained contraction with slow relaxation, “cannot let go of a handshake”. Weakness is distal and facial (“hatchet face”, ptosis), with cataracts, frontal balding, testicular atrophy, cardiac conduction defects and insulin resistance. Biopsy: chains of internal nuclei, ring fibres, sarcoplasmic masses, type 1 fibre atrophy; little necrosis.

Myasthenia gravis (in brief)

Autoimmune disease of the neuromuscular junction: antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor (about 85%; others against MuSK or LRP4) block and internalise receptors and fix complement. The thymus is abnormal in most: thymic hyperplasia or thymoma. Fatigable weakness that worsens with use and recovers with rest; ptosis and diplopia first, then bulbar and limb muscles. Improves with anticholinesterases; thymectomy and immunosuppression help. Muscle biopsy is usually unremarkable.

Myasthenia vs Lambert–Eaton. Lambert–Eaton syndrome has antibodies to presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels, is often paraneoplastic (small cell lung carcinoma), spares the eyes, has autonomic symptoms and shows strength that improves with repeated effort (incremental response), the opposite of myasthenia gravis.
