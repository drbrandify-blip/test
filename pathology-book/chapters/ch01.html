General pathology taught the processes (cell injury, inflammation, repair, haemodynamics, immunity, neoplasia); special (systemic) pathology applies them organ by organ. Every disease here, and every long answer you write, follows one frame: definition → aetiology → pathogenesis → morphology (gross, microscopy) → clinical features and tests → complications and prognosis.

1.1 Oral premalignant lesions

Most of the mouth is lined by non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium. Chronic irritation by tobacco, areca nut or alcohol makes it thicken and keratinise, which looks white; or it becomes thin, dysplastic and vascular, which looks red. The colour is a rough guide to the risk.

Leukoplakia is a white patch or plaque of the oral mucosa that cannot be scraped off and cannot be characterised clinically or microscopically as any other disease (a clinical label, not a diagnosis). Erythroplakia is a red, velvety, often eroded area, flat or slightly depressed, that cannot be explained by any other cause. A mixed lesion is speckled leukoplakia (erythroleukoplakia).

White: watch. Red: real danger.

Both are premalignant until a biopsy proves otherwise. The usual march: hyperkeratosis → dysplasia → carcinoma in situ → invasive SCC.

White, but not leukoplakia: candidiasis (scrapes off), lichen planus (lacy striae), frictional keratosis, and oral hairy leukoplakia: EBV-driven corrugated plaques on the lateral tongue in HIV; not premalignant.

Oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF)

A chronic, progressive scarring disease of the oral mucosa, seen almost entirely in South Asia, caused by chewing areca nut (supari) in paan, gutka and paan masala. It is a recognised premalignant condition.

  • Clinical: burning with spicy food, blanched marble-white mucosa, vertical fibrous bands, progressive trismus
  • Microscopy: atrophic (sometimes dysplastic) epithelium over dense, hyalinised, poorly vascular collagen reaching muscle

1.2 Oral squamous cell carcinoma

About 95% of oral cancers are squamous cell carcinomas (SCC). Two separate roads lead to them, and the road decides the site, the patient and the prognosis.

Road 1: carcinogen-driven (oral cavity)

  • Tobacco smoked or chewed (naswar, gutka), betel quid with areca, alcohol
  • Field cancerisation: the whole exposed mucosa is mutated, so second primaries are common
  • TP53 (commonest), loss of CDKN2A (p16), NOTCH1
  • South Asia: buccal mucosa, gingivobuccal sulcus (where the quid rests); West: tongue, floor of mouth

Road 2: HPV-driven (oropharynx)

  • HPV-16; tonsillar crypts and base of tongue
  • E7 inactivates RB, so cells over-express p16: the surrogate immunostain
  • Younger patients, often non-smokers; non-keratinising
  • Better prognosis
  • Early: raised, firm, pearly plaque or verrucous patch
  • Later: ulcer with raised, rolled, indurated edges or a fungating mass, fixed to deeper tissue
  • Nests and cords of atypical squamous cells invading the stroma
  • Well differentiated: keratin pearls, single-cell keratinisation, intercellular bridges
Spread: local, then cervical lymph nodes (often at diagnosis); lungs, liver and bone late. Early lesions do well, advanced ones poorly. Verrucous carcinoma: warty, well differentiated, pushing border, rarely metastasises.

Oral cavity cancer is one of the most common cancers in Pakistan, at or near the top in men in the Karachi cancer registry. The drivers are naswar, gutka and mainpuri (smokeless tobacco), paan with supari and smoking; about 19% of adults use tobacco in some form (GATS 2014). Areca nut also causes OSMF, so a young chewer with trismus already carries a premalignant mucosa.

Salivary gland tumours

The smaller the gland, the more likely the tumour is malignant. About 65–80% of tumours arise in the parotid, but only 15–30% of these are malignant; about 40% of submandibular, half of minor-gland and 70–90% of sublingual tumours are malignant. A parotid lump with facial palsy, pain or fixation is malignant until proven otherwise.

Pleomorphic adenoma (benign mixed tumour)

The commonest salivary tumour (about 60% of parotid tumours). “Mixed” because neoplastic epithelial and myoepithelial cells lie in a myxoid, hyaline or chondroid stroma that the myoepithelial cells themselves make. Driven by PLAG1 or HMGA2 rearrangement.

  • Rounded, well-demarcated, rarely over 6 cm
  • Capsule incomplete: tongue-like protrusions into the gland
  • Grey-white, myxoid, with bluish translucent chondroid areas
  • Ducts, acini, cords and sheets of epithelial and myoepithelial cells
  • Myxoid and chondroid matrix; cartilage, rarely bone
  • No atypia
Clinical: painless, slow, mobile lump at the angle of the jaw. Enucleation leaves protrusions behind (recurrence up to about 25%; about 4% after parotidectomy). Carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma: about 2% if present < 5 years, about 10% if > 15 years; highly aggressive.

Warthin tumour (papillary cystadenoma lymphomatosum)

Second commonest benign tumour, practically confined to the parotid; 5th–7th decades; smokers have about eight times the risk; about 10% bilateral. Gross: encapsulated, 2–5 cm, pale grey with slit-like cysts of mucoid fluid. Microscopy: cysts and papillae lined by a double layer of oncocytic cells (granular pink cytoplasm) over a dense lymphoid stroma with germinal centres.

1.4 Oesophagus: obstruction and bleeding

Read the dysphagia. A mechanical block (stricture, carcinoma) narrows the lumen gradually, so solids stick first and liquids follow later. A motility disorder (achalasia) fails to propel anything, so solids and liquids are difficult from the start and undigested food is regurgitated.

Achalasia

Achalasia is a motility disorder with a triad: incomplete relaxation of the lower oesophageal sphincter (LES), increased LES tone and aperistalsis of the oesophageal body.

!Loss of inhibitory myenteric neurons -> Less NO and VIP at LES -> LES fails to relax, tone rises -> *Food stagnates, oesophagus dilates -> Regurgitation, aspiration, SCC risk
  • Primary (most): degeneration of inhibitory ganglion cells; autoimmune or post-viral damage suspected
  • Secondary: Chagas disease (T. cruzi), diabetic neuropathy, amyloid, sarcoidosis, malignancy (“pseudo-achalasia”)
  • Morphology: dilated body above a narrow LES; few or no myenteric ganglion cells; stasis oesophagitis, candidiasis
  • Diagnosis: barium “bird-beak”; manometry. Complications: aspiration pneumonia, diverticula, SCC

Hiatal hernia

The diaphragmatic crura separate and part of the stomach rises into the thorax; often silent.

  • Sliding (axial), about 95%: GE junction and cardia slide up as a bell-shaped pouch; GERD, ulcers, bleeding
  • Paraoesophageal (rolling): GE junction stays put; fundus rolls up beside the oesophagus; risk of strangulation

Mallory–Weiss tears

Severe retching, classically in acute alcohol intoxication, raises gastric pressure before the oesophagus relaxes, and the wall tears. The tears are longitudinal, linear, mucosal and cross the GE junction. They cause haematemesis but usually heal by themselves. A transmural rupture (Boerhaave syndrome) is rare and catastrophic (mediastinitis).

Oesophageal varices

!Cirrhosis (HCV in Pakistan) -> Portal hypertension -> Left gastric vein collaterals -> *Dilated submucosal veins -> !Rupture: massive haematemesis
  • Develop in about half of patients with cirrhosis; schistosomiasis is a major cause worldwide
  • Morphology: dilated, tortuous veins in the submucosa of the distal oesophagus and proximal stomach; collapse after death, so easily missed at autopsy
  • Rupture: sudden massive haematemesis without warning; death from shock or hepatic coma; rebleeding common (see Chapter 6)

1.5 Oesophagitis, GERD and Barrett oesophagus

Reflux oesophagitis (gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, GERD)

The commonest cause of oesophagitis: refluxed acid, pepsin and bile injure the squamous lining when the LES fails. LES tone falls with alcohol, tobacco, CNS depressants and fatty meals; reflux rises with obesity, pregnancy, hiatal hernia and delayed gastric emptying.

  • Often only redness; may look normal
  • Severe: erosions, ulcers, stricture
  • Intraepithelial eosinophils; neutrophils if severe
  • Basal zone hyperplasia > 20% of thickness
  • Elongated papillae reaching the upper third
Clinical: heartburn, sour regurgitation, dysphagia; chest pain may mimic angina. Complications: ulcer and bleeding, stricture, Barrett oesophagus.

  • Herpes simplex: punched-out ulcers; inclusions and multinucleated squamous cells at the edge
  • CMV: shallow ulcers; inclusions in endothelial and stromal cells of the base
  • Chemical: lye, very hot drinks, lodged pills (doxycycline, bisphosphonates, iron, NSAIDs)

Barrett oesophagus

Barrett oesophagus is a complication of chronic GERD in which distal oesophageal squamous mucosa is replaced by intestinal-type columnar epithelium with goblet cells (intestinal metaplasia). It develops in about 10% of people with symptomatic GERD, mostly white men aged 40–60, and is the main precursor of oesophageal adenocarcinoma. The metaplasia is an adaptation (columnar cells resist acid better), but the clone can acquire TP53 mutations and progress; most patients never do.

Smoke, Spirits, Scalding tea: middle third. Acid (Barrett), Adiposity: lower third.

Both present late: progressive dysphagia (solids, then liquids), odynophagia, marked weight loss, haematemesis, hoarseness, cough on swallowing (fistula). Overall survival is poor; mucosal tumours do well.

An “oesophageal cancer belt” runs through Iran and Afghanistan into Balochistan, and in Pakistan squamous cell carcinoma predominates. Very hot tea, naswar, smoking and nutritional deficiencies are the local risk factors: a middle-aged patient from Quetta with progressive dysphagia should be scoped early.
