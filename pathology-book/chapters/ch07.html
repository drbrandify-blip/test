7.1 Gallstones (cholelithiasis)

Bile is a clever solvent. Water-insoluble cholesterol is kept in solution only because bile salts and lecithin wrap it into micelles and vesicles. Tip the balance (too much cholesterol, too little bile salt) and cholesterol crystallises. Pigment stones follow the same logic with insoluble unconjugated bilirubin. About 10–20% of adults in high-income countries carry gallstones, most without knowing it.

Risk factors for cholesterol stones

  • Female sex hormones: female sex, pregnancy, oral contraceptives
  • Obesity, insulin resistance, rapid weight loss
  • Advancing age; Native and Mexican Americans, northern Europeans
  • Stasis: pregnancy, parenteral nutrition, spinal cord injury
  • Hyperlipidaemia, clofibrate; bile acid defects; ABCG8 variants

The 4 Fs: Female, Fat, Fertile, Forty

Some teachers add Fair and Flatulent (6 Fs). Flatulent dyspepsia is a symptom, not a cause; in an answer, list the 4 Fs and then the risk factors alongside.

Clinical features and complications of gallstones

About 70–80% of people with stones remain asymptomatic for life. Pain begins when a stone obstructs the outlet: biliary colic, a severe, steady right-upper-quadrant or epigastric pain, often after a fatty meal, radiating to the right shoulder.

  • Acute and chronic cholecystitis; empyema, gangrene, perforation
  • Choledocholithiasis → obstructive jaundice, ascending cholangitis
  • Acute pancreatitis (stone at the ampulla)
  • Mucocele (hydrops): obstructed gallbladder fills with clear mucus
  • Gallstone ileus: a large stone erodes into the bowel (cholecystoenteric fistula) and impacts at the ileocaecal valve
  • Carcinoma of the gallbladder (long-term risk)

7.2 Cholecystitis

Acute calculous cholecystitis

Acute inflammation of a gallbladder that contains stones, triggered in about 90% of cases by obstruction of the neck or cystic duct. It is the commonest complication of gallstones and the commonest reason for emergency cholecystectomy.

Chemical first, bacterial later. Trapped bile is the first irritant: mucosal phospholipases turn lecithin into toxic lysolecithin, the mucus barrier is stripped and prostaglandins inflame the wall. The distended gallbladder squeezes its own blood supply, and only then do gut bacteria (E. coli, enterococci) invade. Early attacks may be sterile; neglected ones become gangrenous.

Acute acalculous cholecystitis

About 5–12% of gallbladders removed for acute cholecystitis contain no stones. It strikes critically ill patients: major non-biliary surgery, severe trauma, burns, sepsis, prolonged parenteral nutrition, the postpartum state. The mechanism is ischaemia (the cystic artery is an end artery) plus stasis. Gangrene and perforation are commoner than in calculous disease, and the diagnosis is easily missed in a sedated patient.

Chronic cholecystitis

May follow repeated acute attacks but often arises without them; stones are present in over 90%. Bacteria grow in only about a third, so supersaturated bile itself seems to drive the inflammation.

Clinical: acute attacks give progressive RUQ pain, fever, vomiting, leukocytosis and a positive Murphy sign (jaundice suggests a duct stone); chronic disease gives recurrent colic or nothing. Variants: porcelain gallbladder (dystrophic calcification of the wall; raised cancer risk) · xanthogranulomatous cholecystitis (nodular wall packed with lipid-laden macrophages; mimics carcinoma) · hydrops (atrophic, obstructed gallbladder filled with clear secretions).

7.3 Carcinoma of the gallbladder

The commonest malignancy of the extrahepatic biliary tract, typically in older women. The key risk factor is gallstones, present in the great majority (up to about 95%): years of stone-induced inflammation drive metaplasia, dysplasia and carcinoma. Porcelain gallbladder and chronic biliary infection add risk.

  • Commonest in the fundus and neck
  • Infiltrating (commoner): diffuse, firm, grey-white thickening of the wall
  • Exophytic: cauliflower-like mass growing into the lumen
  • Adenocarcinoma in most (papillary to poorly differentiated glands in desmoplastic stroma); few adenosquamous or squamous
  • Direct invasion of the liver, cystic duct and portal nodes is usual by diagnosis
Clinical: insidious, mimicking gallstone disease (RUQ pain, jaundice, anorexia). Prognosis is poor; the lucky few are found incidentally at cholecystectomy.

Gallbladder carcinoma is relatively common in Pakistan and northern India, as part of a regional belt of high incidence. It is strongly gallstone-associated and more frequent in women. Because early cancers are often found unexpectedly, every gallbladder removed for “stones” should be sent for histopathology.

7.4 Acute pancreatitis

Acute pancreatitis is a reversible inflammatory disorder of the pancreas whose severity ranges from interstitial oedema with focal fat necrosis to widespread haemorrhagic parenchymal necrosis. Its basis is autodigestion of the gland by inappropriately activated pancreatic enzymes.

Biliary tract disease (gallstones) and alcohol together cause about 80% of cases; about 5% of people with gallstones develop pancreatitis, and 10–20% of cases are idiopathic. Robbins groups the rest as metabolic (hypertriglyceridaemia, hypercalcaemia, drugs), genetic (PRSS1 cationic trypsinogen, gain of function; SPINK1 trypsin inhibitor, loss of function; CFTR), mechanical (trauma, ERCP, pancreas divisum, periampullary tumour, Ascaris), vascular (shock, atheroembolism, vasculitis) and infectious (mumps, coxsackievirus).

Trypsin is the master switch. The pancreas normally protects itself by making enzymes as inactive zymogens, packaging them in granules away from lysosomes, and carrying a trypsin inhibitor (SPINK1). Once trypsinogen is activated inside the gland, trypsin activates everything else: prophospholipase, proelastase, kallikrein, the clotting and complement cascades. A single misplaced activation therefore becomes a chain reaction. This is also why a gain-of-function PRSS1 mutation (trypsin that resists inactivation) or loss of SPINK1 causes hereditary pancreatitis.

Alcohol contracts the sphincter of Oddi, is toxic to acinar cells and forms protein plugs in small ducts. Duct obstruction acts through oedema, enzyme leak and ischaemia.

  • Mild (interstitial): oedematous gland with focal fat necrosis
  • Severe (necrotising/haemorrhagic): red-black haemorrhage mixed with chalky yellow-white foci of fat necrosis
  • Fat necrosis also in omentum, mesentery, even subcutaneous fat
  • Peritoneum: turbid, brown-tinged fluid with fat globules
  • Microvascular leakage → interstitial oedema
  • Enzymatic fat necrosis: ghost outlines of fat cells with basophilic calcium soap deposits (saponification)
  • Acute inflammatory reaction (neutrophils) around necrosis
  • Proteolytic destruction of acini, ducts and islets; necrosis of vessel walls → haemorrhage

Clinical features and laboratory findings

  • Severe, constant upper abdominal pain radiating to the back, often after a heavy meal or drinking bout
  • Severe cases: acute abdomen, shock (fluid sequestration, haemorrhage, cytokines, endotoxaemia); flank (Grey Turner) or periumbilical (Cullen) bruising
  • Serum amylase rises within 24 h and falls in a few days; lipase rises a little later, stays high longer and is more specific
  • Hypocalcaemia (calcium trapped in fat necrosis), hyperglycaemia, leukocytosis, raised bilirubin
  • Diagnosis: typical pain plus amylase or lipase > 3 × upper limit, or contrast CT showing a swollen, necrotic gland

Complications

  • Systemic: shock, DIC, ARDS, acute tubular necrosis, SIRS
  • Local: infected necrosis and pancreatic abscess (gram-negative bacteria), pseudocyst, duodenal obstruction
  • Mild attacks settle within days; severe necrotising disease still carries a significant mortality

Pancreatic pseudocyst

A collection of necrotic, haemorrhagic, enzyme-rich fluid walled off by granulation and fibrous tissue without an epithelial lining (hence “pseudo”), usually in the lesser sac after acute (especially alcoholic) pancreatitis or trauma. Pseudocysts make up about three-quarters of pancreatic cysts. They may resolve, or become infected, bleed, rupture or compress neighbouring organs.

7.5 Chronic pancreatitis

Chronic pancreatitis is long-standing inflammation with irreversible destruction of the exocrine parenchyma, fibrosis and, in late stages, loss of endocrine tissue. Irreversibility is what separates it from acute pancreatitis.

  • Long-term alcohol abuse: commonest; middle-aged men
  • Long-standing duct obstruction: stones, tumours, pancreas divisum
  • Hereditary: PRSS1, SPINK1; CFTR mutations
  • Tropical pancreatitis (Asia, Africa; some SPINK1): young, large ductal stones
  • Autoimmune (IgG4-related) pancreatitis
  • A sizeable fraction are idiopathic
!Alcohol, obstruction, genes -> Repeated acinar injury -> *Stellate cells activated by TGF-β, PDGF -> !Fibrosis, loss of acini -> ~Late: islets lost, diabetes !Alcohol, obstruction, genes -> Protein plugs in ducts -> Calcified stones, dilated ducts
  • Gland hard, shrunken, nodular
  • Dilated ducts with visible calcified concretions
  • Pseudocysts may be attached
  • Parenchymal fibrosis; acini reduced in number and size
  • Relative sparing of islets, which cluster and may look enlarged within the scar
  • Variably dilated ducts with eosinophilic protein plugs or stones; ductal epithelium atrophic, hyperplastic or squamous
  • Chronic inflammatory cells around lobules and ducts
Autoimmune (IgG4) pancreatitis: duct-centred lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, storiform fibrosis, venulitis; steroid-responsive; can mimic carcinoma.

Clinical features and complications

  • Recurrent or persistent abdominal pain; some are silent
  • Exocrine insufficiency → malabsorption, steatorrhoea, weight loss, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency
  • Diabetes mellitus (late, once islets are destroyed)
  • Pseudocysts (about 10%); raised risk of carcinoma
  • Calcification on X-ray or CT is characteristic; amylase may be normal in a “burnt-out” gland

Chronic pancreatitis slide in 10 seconds: (1) Lobules are shrunken and separated by broad bands of pink fibrous tissue. (2) Acini are scanty; what survives are islets of Langerhans, pale islands stranded in the scar. (3) Ducts are dilated, some with pink inspissated plugs. (4) Lymphocytes and plasma cells sprinkle the fibrosis. No neutrophil-rich necrosis: that would be acute.

7.6 Carcinoma of the pancreas

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is silent until advanced, and fewer than about one in ten patients survive five years. It arises from precursor lesions: usually microscopic pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN), less often cystic precursors (IPMN, mucinous cystic neoplasm).

The “big four” genes: KRAS (activating mutation, > 90%; the commonest oncogene) · CDKN2A/p16 (lost in most) · TP53 · SMAD4, accumulating stepwise from low-grade PanIN to invasive carcinoma. Inherited risk: BRCA2, PRSS1, CDKN2A (familial melanoma), Peutz–Jeghers. Strongest environmental risk: smoking; also chronic pancreatitis, diabetes, obesity.

  • Site: about 60% head, 15% body, 5% tail, 20% diffuse
  • Gross: hard, stellate, grey-white, poorly defined mass
  • Micro: infiltrating glands, intense desmoplasia, perineural and vascular invasion
  • Head tumours: block the distal bile duct → painless progressive jaundice, weight loss
  • Body/tail tumours: silent; late back pain and liver metastases
  • Trousseau sign: migratory thrombophlebitis from tumour procoagulants
  • CA19-9: raised in many; not specific, not for screening; used to follow treatment

Courvoisier law. In a jaundiced patient, a palpable, non-tender gallbladder is unlikely to be due to stones, because a stone-bearing gallbladder is chronically inflamed, fibrotic and cannot distend. It points to malignant obstruction of the distal bile duct, classically carcinoma of the head of the pancreas. Do not confuse it with the Trousseau sign of malignancy (migratory thrombophlebitis).
