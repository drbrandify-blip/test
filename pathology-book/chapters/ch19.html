19.1 Prostate: zones and prostatitis

The adult prostate (about 20 g) has a peripheral zone (about 70% of glands, posterior, against the rectum), a central zone, a periurethral transition zone and anterior fibromuscular stroma. Glands have two cell layers: a flat basal layer and tall secretory cells. Hence: hyperplasia starts in the transition zone and squeezes the urethra early; carcinoma starts in the peripheral zone, is felt on rectal examination and obstructs late.

19.2 Benign prostatic hyperplasia (nodular hyperplasia)

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a non-neoplastic proliferation of stromal and glandular elements forming nodules in the transition zone, enlarging the gland and obstructing urine flow. It is present in about 20% of men at 40 years, 70% at 60 and 90% by 80. It is not premalignant.

!Testosterone -> *5α-reductase type 2 (stromal cells) -> DHT (10× more potent) -> Androgen receptors: stroma and epithelium -> Growth factors: FGF, TGF-β -> !Nodules of stroma and glands Ageing: reduced apoptosis -> !Nodules of stroma and glands

Why the drugs work. BPH needs dihydrotestosterone, not testosterone itself; men castrated before puberty never develop it. Finasteride blocks 5α-reductase and shrinks the gland over months. Part of the obstruction is smooth-muscle tone in the stroma and capsule, so α1-blockers (tamsulosin) relieve symptoms within days.

  • Enlarged, 60–100 g or more; firm, rubbery
  • Well-circumscribed nodules bulging from the cut surface in the transition (periurethral) zone
  • Glandular nodules: yellow-pink, soft, small cysts with milky fluid; stromal nodules: grey-white, firm
  • Urethra compressed to a slit; a median lobe may project into the bladder
  • Nodules of hyperplastic glands and fibromuscular stroma
  • Glands lined by tall columnar cells with a preserved basal cell layer (two layers)
  • Papillary infoldings into dilated lumens
  • Lumens contain corpora amylacea (laminated concretions)
  • Foci of infarction with squamous metaplasia
Clinical: lower urinary tract symptoms (hesitancy, poor stream, intermittency, dribbling, frequency, nocturia, urgency), acute retention. Complications: bladder hypertrophy and trabeculation, diverticula, residual urine → UTI and stones, bilateral hydronephrosis and renal failure. PSA may be mildly raised.

BPH slide in 10 seconds: nodules of glands of varying size with papillary infoldings, lined by two layers of cells, many containing pink, laminated corpora amylacea, set in plenty of fibromuscular stroma. No crowding, no prominent nucleoli, no single-layered glands invading between others.

19.3 Carcinoma of the prostate

One of the commonest cancers of older men and a leading cause of male cancer death in the West; a disease of men over 50, with small latent cancers common at autopsy.

Risk factors

  • Age; race (highest in men of African ancestry)
  • Family history; germline BRCA2, HOXB13
  • Androgens: does not develop in men castrated before puberty
  • Diet and environment: risk rises in Asian migrants to the West
  • TMPRSS2–ETS (ERG) fusion: an androgen-driven promoter switches on an ETS transcription factor (about half of cases)
  • PTEN loss → PI3K/AKT activation
  • GSTP1 silenced by promoter hypermethylation (very common, early)
  • AR amplification or mutation in castration-resistant disease
  • Precursor: high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (HGPIN): atypical cells with prominent nucleoli in glands that keep a basal layer
  • About 70% arise in the peripheral zone, classically posteriorly: palpable as a hard, irregular nodule on rectal examination
  • Firm, gritty, grey-white to yellow, poorly defined
  • Local spread to seminal vesicles, bladder base (ureteric obstruction); rectum rarely (Denonvilliers fascia)
  • Small, crowded, back-to-back glands infiltrating stroma
  • Glands lined by a single layer of cuboidal cells: no basal cell layer
  • Enlarged nuclei with prominent nucleoli
  • Perineural invasion; intraluminal crystalloids, blue mucin
  • IHC: AMACR positive; basal markers (p63, high-molecular-weight keratin) negative; PSA/NKX3.1 confirm prostatic origin of metastases

Gleason grading uses gland architecture at low power, not nuclear atypia; patterns 1–2 are no longer diagnosed, so the lowest practical score is 6. Grade group, stage and PSA are the main prognostic factors.

Spread and clinical picture

  • Most are silent (peripheral, away from the urethra): found by PSA, rectal examination or metastases
  • Lymphatics: obturator and pelvic, then para-aortic nodes
  • Blood: bone, especially the axial skeleton (lumbar spine, pelvis, femur), via the vertebral venous plexus
  • Bone metastases are typically osteoblastic (sclerotic): back pain, raised alkaline phosphatase
  • Treatment: surgery, radiotherapy, androgen deprivation; active surveillance for small low-grade cancers

Prostate-specific antigen is a serine protease (kallikrein) of prostatic epithelium that liquefies semen. It is organ-specific, not cancer-specific.

  • Traditional cut-off 4 ng/mL; 20–40% of organ-confined cancers lie below it
  • Also raised in BPH, prostatitis, infarction, after catheterisation or biopsy
  • Refinements: PSA density (per gram of gland), velocity, age-specific ranges, free-to-total ratio (lower free fraction in cancer)
  • Best use: monitoring recurrence after treatment

19.4 Testis: congenital and inflammatory disease

Cryptorchidism

Cryptorchidism is failure of the testis to descend into the scrotum. It affects about 1% of boys at 1 year, is usually unilateral (bilateral in about a quarter) and the testis most often lies in the inguinal canal. Descent has a transabdominal phase and an androgen-dependent inguinoscrotal phase.

  • Germ cell development arrests; damage visible from about 2 years
  • Seminiferous tubules shrink with thick, hyalinised basement membranes, ending as hyaline cords
  • Leydig cells spared, so they look prominent
  • Testis small and firm; the descended one may also be abnormal

Complications

  • Infertility, especially if bilateral
  • Germ cell tumour: risk raised 3–5-fold, also in the descended testis
  • Inguinal hernia, torsion, trauma against the pubis
  • Orchiopexy before about 2 years reduces atrophy and infertility, but not all of the cancer risk

Testicular atrophy: causes

  • Atherosclerosis (old age), end-stage orchitis, cryptorchidism
  • Hypopituitarism, cachexia, irradiation, oestrogen/antiandrogen therapy, cirrhosis
  • Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY): primary failure

Torsion: twisting of the spermatic cord blocks the thin-walled veins first while arteries still pump blood in, so the testis becomes engorged and undergoes haemorrhagic (venous) infarction. Neonatal torsion occurs around birth (extravaginal). Adult torsion occurs in adolescents with a bilateral “bell-clapper” deformity (intravaginal): sudden severe pain, often after exertion or in sleep, absent cremasteric reflex. A surgical emergency: untwisted within about 6 hours, the testis usually survives; the other side is fixed too.

Spermatocele: epididymal cyst with sperm.

19.5 Testicular tumours

A firm, painless testicular enlargement is a tumour until proved otherwise. Testicular tumours peak at 15–34 years, where they are the commonest cancer of men. About 95% are germ cell tumours (GCTs): in adults almost all are malignant. Sex cord–stromal tumours are usually benign.

Classification (WHO, simplified)

  • GCTs from germ cell neoplasia in situ (GCNIS): seminoma; non-seminomatous: embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac tumour, choriocarcinoma, teratoma (postpubertal); mixed GCT
  • GCTs not from GCNIS: spermatocytic tumour (old men), prepubertal teratoma and yolk sac tumour (children)
  • Sex cord–stromal: Leydig cell, Sertoli cell tumours
  • Lymphoma: the commonest testicular tumour in men over 60 (diffuse large B-cell)

Risk factors

  • Cryptorchidism; androgen insensitivity, gonadal dysgenesis
  • Family history; tumour in the other testis

GCNIS and i(12p). Germ cell neoplasia in situ (formerly intratubular germ cell neoplasia) is a row of large atypical germ cells with clear cytoplasm and big nuclei along the basement membrane of seminiferous tubules; about half progress to invasive GCT within 5 years. GCNIS and its tumours share an isochromosome 12p, i(12p), found in virtually all postpubertal GCTs, and express the pluripotency factors OCT3/4 and NANOG. Seminomas often carry KIT mutations.

Seminoma

The commonest GCT (about half of all), peaking in the 30s; it never occurs in infants. It is the testicular counterpart of the ovarian dysgerminoma.

  • Bulky, well-demarcated, soft
  • Homogeneous grey-white to cream, lobulated cut surface (“potato-like”)
  • No haemorrhage or necrosis (if present, suspect a non-seminoma component)
  • Tunica albuginea usually intact
  • Sheets of uniform large cells divided into lobules by delicate fibrous septa with lymphocytes
  • Round to polygonal cells with clear, glycogen-rich cytoplasm, distinct membranes, central nucleus with prominent nucleoli (“fried-egg” cells)
  • Granulomas in some
  • About 15% contain syncytiotrophoblasts (mild hCG rise)
  • IHC: OCT3/4, KIT (CD117), PLAP, D2-40 positive

Seminoma slide in 10 seconds: sheets of large clear cells with central nuclei and big nucleoli, like fried eggs, chopped into lobules by thin fibrous septa packed with lymphocytes. Two-component pattern (tumour cells + lymphocytes) = seminoma. Ask: radiosensitive; AFP normal; spreads to para-aortic nodes.

Yolk → AFP · Chorio → hCG · Load → LDH

AFP comes from yolk sac (fetal) elements; hCG from syncytiotrophoblast, sky-high in choriocarcinoma; LDH tracks tumour Load. A pure seminoma never raises AFP.

  • AFP: yolk sac elements; never raised in pure seminoma, so a raised AFP means a non-seminoma component
  • hCG: syncytiotrophoblast; very high in choriocarcinoma, mild in some seminomas
  • LDH: tumour bulk; prognosis
  • Uses: diagnosis, staging, response and early detection of relapse

Clinical rules: remove by radical inguinal orchiectomy; scrotal biopsy is avoided because it opens a lymphatic route through scrotal skin. Spread follows the testicular vessels to para-aortic nodes (not inguinal). Overall cure exceeds 90%.

Leydig cell tumour: golden-brown, homogeneous; polygonal cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and rod-shaped Reinke crystalloids; androgens → precocious puberty in boys, oestrogens → gynaecomastia in men; about 10% malignant. Sertoli cell tumour: grey-white, tubule-forming, usually benign and hormonally silent.
