8.1 Defences of the lung and classification of pneumonia

Pneumonia is any infection of the lung parenchyma. The lung is exposed to microbes with every breath and to oral flora with every unnoticed aspiration in sleep, yet it stays sterile below the larynx because its defences are layered. Pneumonia means one or more layers have failed, and which layer fails predicts which organism invades.

Defences

  • Nose and upper airway filter large particles; cough and glottic closure
  • Mucociliary escalator sweeps trapped particles upwards
  • Alveolar macrophages, surfactant, IgA, complement, neutrophil recruitment
  • Adaptive immunity: antibody (opsonises capsulated bacteria), T cells (intracellular microbes)

What breaks them

  • Lost cough reflex: coma, anaesthesia, alcohol, drugs, neuromuscular disease → aspiration
  • Damaged cilia: cigarette smoke, viral infection (influenza), hot or corrosive gases, immotile cilia
  • Retained secretions: cystic fibrosis, bronchial obstruction (tumour)
  • Impaired macrophages: alcohol, smoke, hypoxia; pulmonary oedema

The defect predicts the bug. Weak antibody or no spleen (sickle cell disease, splenectomy) invites capsulated bacteria such as pneumococcus and H. influenzae. Defective neutrophils or complement invite pyogenic bacteria and Aspergillus. Weak T-cell immunity (HIV, transplant) invites intracellular organisms: mycobacteria, Pneumocystis, CMV and herpesviruses. A preceding influenza infection strips the cilia and opens the door to S. aureus and pneumococcus.

Pathologists also classify by anatomical pattern: lobar pneumonia (consolidation of a large part or all of a lobe), bronchopneumonia (patchy consolidation centred on bronchioles) and interstitial pneumonia (inflammation of alveolar walls, typical of atypical and viral infections).

8.2 Lobar pneumonia and bronchopneumonia

Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus), a gram-positive lancet-shaped diplococcus, is the commonest cause of community-acquired pneumonia and of lobar pneumonia. Sputum Gram stain shows neutrophils with diplococci; blood cultures are positive in a fraction of cases. Pneumococcal vaccines protect high-risk groups. Klebsiella pneumoniae, the commonest gram-negative cause, strikes debilitated and malnourished people and chronic alcoholics, with thick, gelatinous (“red-currant jelly”) sputum from its viscid capsule.

Why lobar pneumonia fills a whole lobe. Pneumococci multiply in the alveoli and provoke a flood of protein-rich oedema fluid. Bacteria ride this fluid from alveolus to alveolus through the pores of Kohn, so the infection spreads like a stain until it reaches the pleura of the fissure, which stops it. Bronchopneumonia starts instead in many separate bronchioles, so it produces many separate patches.

  • Pleura: a fibrinous pleuritis overlies the affected lobe in the early stages (pleuritic pain, friction rub). It may resolve or organise into fibrous thickening and adhesions.
  • If resolution fails, fibroblasts grow into the exudate and organise it into solid fibrous tissue.

Bronchopneumonia

Patchy foci of acute suppurative inflammation, centred on bronchioles, that may be confined to one lobe but are usually multilobar, bilateral and basal (secretions gravitate to the lower lobes). Typical patients are infants, the elderly, the bedridden and the terminally ill; organisms include staphylococci, streptococci, H. influenzae, Pseudomonas and coliforms.

Lobar pneumonia slide in 10 seconds: (1) Alveolar walls are intact and thin; the lung architecture is preserved. (2) Every alveolus is stuffed with exudate: neutrophils and pink fibrin strands (plus red cells in red hepatisation, few red cells in grey). (3) All fields look alike, because the whole lobe is at one stage. (4) The pleura may carry a fibrin layer. Intact walls separate it from an abscess, where the walls are destroyed.

8.3 Atypical, hospital-acquired and aspiration pneumonia; lung abscess

Atypical (interstitial) pneumonia

Called “atypical” because sputum is scanty, chest signs of consolidation are absent, the white count is only modestly raised and, histologically, the alveoli contain no exudate. Mycoplasma pneumoniae is the commonest cause in children and young adults; viruses (influenza, RSV, adenovirus, SARS-CoV-2) are the others. Viruses damage the respiratory epithelium and cilia, which also explains secondary bacterial pneumonia after influenza.

  • Patchy or lobar; areas are red-blue, congested and subcrepitant
  • Pleuritis is unusual
  • Interstitial inflammation: alveolar septa widened, oedematous, infiltrated by lymphocytes, macrophages, plasma cells
  • Alveolar spaces largely free of cellular exudate
  • Severe cases: hyaline membranes (diffuse alveolar damage)

Hospital-acquired pneumonia

Pneumonia starting 48 hours or more after admission, common in ICU and in ventilated patients, the immunosuppressed and those on prolonged antibiotics. Gram-negative rods and MRSA dominate; mortality is high.

Aspiration pneumonia

Debilitated or unconscious patients (stroke, alcohol intoxication, seizures, repeated vomiting) aspirate gastric contents and oral flora. Injury is partly chemical (acid) and partly bacterial (anaerobes). It is often necrotising, frequently fatal, and the commonest route to lung abscess.

Pneumonia in the immunocompromised

  • Pneumocystis jirovecii: HIV with CD4 < 200/µL; alveoli filled with foamy, frothy pink exudate; cup-shaped cysts on silver (GMS) stain
  • CMV: enlarged cells with owl-eye intranuclear inclusions
  • Invasive aspergillosis: neutropenia; septate hyphae branching at acute angles invade vessels → haemorrhagic infarcts
  • Mycobacteria (typical and atypical) and the usual bacteria

Lung abscess

Lung abscess is a localised area of suppurative necrosis of lung parenchyma that forms one or more cavities. Anaerobes are found in almost all, usually mixed with aerobes.

Routes

  • Aspiration of infected material (commonest): usually single, right-sided (right main bronchus is wider and more vertical)
  • Post-pneumonic: S. aureus, Klebsiella, type 3 pneumococcus
  • Septic emboli (right-sided endocarditis, IV drug use): multiple
  • Behind an obstructing tumour: always exclude carcinoma in an older smoker
  • Penetrating trauma; spread from a subphrenic abscess

Morphology and course

  • A few mm to 5–6 cm; cavity of suppurative debris; air–fluid level if it drains into a bronchus
  • Micro: suppurative destruction of lung tissue; with time, a fibrous wall
  • Copious, foul-smelling purulent sputum, fever, weight loss, clubbing
  • Complications: empyema, haemorrhage, brain abscess or meningitis, secondary (AA) amyloidosis

8.4 Tuberculosis: the organism and pathogenesis

Tuberculosis is a chronic communicable granulomatous disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It usually involves the lungs but can affect any organ, and its hallmark lesion is the caseating granuloma.

M. tuberculosis is a slender, aerobic, non-motile rod with a waxy, mycolic acid-rich wall that holds carbol fuchsin against acid-alcohol: it is acid-fast. It grows slowly (weeks in culture). Spread is by droplet nuclei coughed out by a patient with open (cavitary) disease. M. bovis in unpasteurised milk causes oropharyngeal and intestinal TB. Infection (bacilli present, usually latent and symptomless) must be distinguished from disease (clinically active TB); most infected people never develop disease.

!Inhaled droplet nuclei -> Alveolar macrophages engulf bacilli -> Phagolysosome maturation blocked -> Bacilli multiply; bacteraemia (first 3 weeks) Bacilli multiply; bacteraemia (first 3 weeks) -> Antigen to hilar nodes; IL-12 -> *Th1 CD4 cells make IFN-γ *Th1 CD4 cells make IFN-γ -> Activated macrophages kill (NO, ROS) *Th1 CD4 cells make IFN-γ -> TNF: epithelioid cells, giant cells -> !Granuloma with caseation
  1. Entry: alveolar macrophages take up bacilli through mannose and complement receptors.
  2. Replication: the bacillus blocks phagolysosome maturation and multiplies unchecked for about 3 weeks; bacteraemia seeds many organs silently.
  3. Th1 response: dendritic cells carry antigen to hilar nodes; IL-12 drives CD4+ Th1 cells that secrete IFN-γ.
  4. Macrophage activation: IFN-γ enables phagolysosome fusion, nitric oxide, reactive oxygen species and autophagy: bacilli are killed or contained.
  5. Granuloma and damage: TNF and chemokines recruit monocytes that become epithelioid cells and fuse into Langhans giant cells; type IV hypersensitivity produces caseous necrosis.

Immunity and hypersensitivity are the same response. The Th1/IFN-γ reaction that lets macrophages kill bacilli also kills host tissue: caseation, cavitation and fibrosis are all products of it. Hence: (1) a positive tuberculin test shows T-cell sensitisation (past or present infection, or BCG), not active disease; (2) defects in IL-12/IFN-γ receptor genes or anti-TNF drugs (infliximab) cause disseminated or reactivated mycobacterial disease; (3) in advanced HIV, granulomas are poorly formed, bacilli abound, cavitation is rare and the tuberculin test is often falsely negative.

  • Caseous necrosis: soft, dry, crumbly, yellow-white, cheese-like material
  • Healing: grey-white fibrosis and chalky calcification
  • Centre: caseation, an amorphous, granular, pink area with no cell outlines
  • Epithelioid histiocytes: pale-pink cytoplasm, elongated slipper-shaped nuclei
  • Langhans giant cells: nuclei arranged in a horseshoe at the periphery
  • Cuff of lymphocytes, then fibroblasts and collagen
  • ZN stain: red acid-fast bacilli, most often in or near the caseous centre

8.5 Primary and secondary tuberculosis

!Inhaled M. tuberculosis (first exposure) -> *Primary TB: Ghon complex *Primary TB: Ghon complex -[immunity contains, ~95%]-> Fibrosis, calcification (Ranke complex) Fibrosis, calcification (Ranke complex) -> ~Latent infection: viable dormant bacilli ~Latent infection: viable dormant bacilli -[immunity falls]-> *Secondary TB: apical lesion Exogenous reinfection -> *Secondary TB: apical lesion *Primary TB: Ghon complex -[weak immunity]-> !Progressive primary TB !Progressive primary TB -> Miliary TB, TB meningitis *Secondary TB: apical lesion -> Cavitation, bronchogenic spread *Secondary TB: apical lesion -> Miliary or isolated-organ TB

Primary tuberculosis

The form that develops in a previously unexposed, unsensitised person. Inhaled bacilli lodge in the subpleural air spaces of the lower part of the upper lobe or upper part of the lower lobe, near the interlobar fissure. A Ghon focus (1–1.5 cm, grey-white, caseating) forms; bacilli drain to the hilar lymph nodes, which also caseate. Focus + nodes = Ghon complex. In about 95% of people, cell-mediated immunity controls the infection: the complex fibroses and calcifies (Ranke complex), but viable bacilli may persist for decades.

Progressive primary TB occurs when immunity is weak (HIV, malnutrition, infancy). It mimics bacterial pneumonia: consolidation of lower and middle lobes, hilar lymphadenopathy, pleural effusion, little cavitation, and lymphohaematogenous spread to meninges and the whole body (miliary TB).

Secondary (post-primary, reactivation) tuberculosis

Disease in a previously sensitised host, usually reactivation of a dormant focus when resistance falls, sometimes exogenous reinfection in high-prevalence areas. It is classically localised to the apex of one or both upper lobes, probably because of the high oxygen tension there. Because the host is already sensitised, the tissue reaction is prompt and intense: the lesion is walled off (so nodes are less involved) but caseation and cavitation are frequent. A cavity that erodes into a bronchus discharges bacilli into the sputum, making the patient infectious.

  • Early: firm, grey-white to yellow focus < 2 cm within 1–2 cm of the apical pleura, central caseation, peripheral fibrosis
  • Healing: fibrocalcific scar that puckers the pleura; pleural adhesions
  • Progressive: caseation expands and erodes a bronchus → ragged cavity lined by caseous material, poorly walled off by fibrous tissue (fibrocavitary TB)
  • Coalescent tubercles with central caseation, epithelioid cells, Langhans giant cells, lymphocytes
  • Fibrous wall around older lesions
  • Acid-fast bacilli on ZN, abundant in cavity walls
Miliary TB: bacilli reach the blood (lymphatics → thoracic duct → right heart → lungs, or pulmonary veins → systemic arteries) and seed countless small (about 2 mm) yellow-white foci resembling millet seeds in the lungs or in liver, spleen, marrow, adrenals, meninges, kidneys, fallopian tubes and epididymis.

Spread of secondary TB

  • Bronchogenic: tuberculous bronchopneumonia, endobronchial and laryngeal TB
  • Swallowed sputum: intestinal TB (ileocaecal)
  • Pleura: effusion, empyema, fibrous obliteration
  • Blood: miliary TB; isolated-organ TB: meninges, kidneys, adrenals (Addison disease), bone (Pott disease of the spine), fallopian tubes, epididymis
  • Lymphadenitis, commonest extrapulmonary form, usually cervical (“scrofula”)

Clinical features

  • Insidious low-grade evening fever, night sweats, anorexia, weight loss, malaise
  • Cough, purulent sputum, haemoptysis, pleuritic pain
  • Massive haemoptysis may come from a Rasmussen aneurysm (pulmonary artery branch in a cavity wall)
  • Long-standing disease: secondary (AA) amyloidosis, cor pulmonale

Drug resistance: rifampicin resistance = rpoB mutations (detected by GeneXpert); isoniazid resistance = katG or inhA. MDR-TB resists at least isoniazid and rifampicin. Rifampicin resistance is a good surrogate for MDR-TB.

Pakistan is among the top five high-TB-burden countries, with an incidence of roughly 266 per 100,000 population. Drug-resistant TB is a growing concern, and GeneXpert MTB/RIF is now widely used. BCG at birth protects children mainly against miliary TB and TB meningitis.

  1. The defect predicts the bug: antibody/spleen → capsulated; T cell → intracellular.
  2. Commonest CAP and lobar pneumonia organism: S. pneumoniae.
  3. Klebsiella: alcoholics, red-currant jelly sputum.
  4. Lobar stages: congestion → red → grey hepatisation → resolution.
  5. Grey hepatisation: red cells lysed; fibrin and neutrophils persist.
  6. Bronchopneumonia: patchy, basal, bilateral; extremes of age.
  7. Atypical pneumonia: interstitial mononuclear infiltrate; Mycoplasma.
  8. HAP: ≥ 48 h after admission; gram-negatives, MRSA.
  9. Lung abscess: aspiration, anaerobes, right side, air–fluid level.
  10. TB survives by blocking phagolysosome maturation.
  11. Th1 → IFN-γ → macrophage activation; TNF keeps granulomas.
  12. Primary TB: Ghon focus + hilar nodes; heals as Ranke complex.
  13. Secondary TB: apex, caseation, cavity, infectious.
  14. Miliary TB: about 2 mm millet-seed foci by haematogenous spread.
  15. GeneXpert: TB DNA + rifampicin resistance (rpoB) in 2 hours.

Memory hooks

  • “Congested Red Grey Rose”: Congestion, Red, Grey, Resolution
  • Ghon goes low, reactivation goes high: primary subpleural mid-lung; secondary apical
  • “Immunity bites back”: the Th1 response that kills TB also caseates the lung
  • Tubercle = C-E-L-L: Caseation, Epithelioid cells, Langhans cells, Lymphocytes
