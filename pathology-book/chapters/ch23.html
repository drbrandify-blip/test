23.1 Thyroid function: reading the profile

Hypothalamic TRH drives pituitary TSH, and TSH drives follicular cells to iodinate thyroglobulin and release T4 (mostly) and T3 (the active hormone, largely made from T4 in the tissues). Free hormone feeds back on the pituitary, which detects even small changes; hence serum TSH is the most sensitive single screening test.

Opposite directions = the gland; same direction = the pituitary. In primary thyroid disease the pituitary is healthy and argues back: a failing gland (low T4) pushes TSH up, an overactive gland (high T4) shuts TSH down. When TSH and T4 move together, the fault lies upstream in the pituitary or hypothalamus (central disease).

Normal values (lab ranges vary): TSH about 0.4–4.0 mIU/L; free T4 about 0.8–1.8 ng/dL. Radioiodine uptake: diffusely high in Graves; a single hot nodule in toxic adenoma; low in thyroiditis or exogenous thyroxine.

Thyrotoxicosis is the hypermetabolic state caused by raised circulating free T3 and T4. Hyperthyroidism is thyrotoxicosis caused by an overactive gland, its commonest cause. Leakage of stored hormone (thyroiditis) or swallowed thyroxine causes thyrotoxicosis without hyperthyroidism.

Causes of thyrotoxicosis

  • With hyperthyroidism (RAIU high): Graves (commonest), toxic multinodular goitre, toxic adenoma; rarely TSH-secreting adenoma
  • Without (RAIU low): granulomatous and painless thyroiditis, struma ovarii, factitious thyroxine

Causes of hypothyroidism

  • Primary: iodine deficiency (commonest worldwide), Hashimoto (commonest where iodine is adequate), thyroidectomy, radioiodine, lithium, amiodarone, congenital defects
  • Central: pituitary tumour, Sheehan syndrome, hypothalamic lesions

Cretinism (hypothyroidism from infancy): impaired brain and skeletal growth, short stature, coarse face, protruding tongue, umbilical hernia; maternal iodine deficiency is the classic cause. Myxoedema (older children, adults): physical and mental slowing with mucopolysaccharide-rich oedema.

23.2 Autoimmune thyroid disease: Graves and Hashimoto

Graves disease

Graves disease is an autoimmune disorder in which IgG autoantibodies stimulate the TSH receptor, producing a triad: thyrotoxicosis with diffuse goitre, infiltrative ophthalmopathy and, in a minority, localised infiltrative dermopathy (pretibial myxoedema). It is the commonest cause of endogenous hyperthyroidism; peak 20–40 years; women up to 10 times more often. Links: HLA-DR3, CTLA4, PTPN22.

!Loss of tolerance to TSH receptor -> *TSI: stimulating anti-TSHR IgG -> TSH receptor switched on (cAMP) -> Hormone release: thyrotoxicosis TSH receptor switched on (cAMP) -> Follicular hyperplasia: diffuse goitre !Loss of tolerance to TSH receptor -> T cells attack orbital fibroblasts -> GAG, fat, lymphocytes in orbit -> *Exophthalmos
  • Symmetrically, diffusely enlarged; capsule intact
  • Soft; cut surface beefy, meaty red (little colloid)
  • Tall, crowded columnar cells heaped into small papillae without fibrovascular cores
  • Pale colloid with scalloped margins
  • Lymphoid aggregates with germinal centres
Labs: low TSH, high free T4/T3, TSI positive, diffusely high radioiodine uptake. Pre-operative iodine makes the epithelium involute and colloid accumulate. Patients are prone to other autoimmune diseases (SLE, pernicious anaemia, type 1 diabetes).

Hashimoto thyroiditis (chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis)

The commonest cause of hypothyroidism where dietary iodine is sufficient; peak 45–65 years, women 10–20 times more often. Tolerance to thyroid peroxidase (TPO) and thyroglobulin breaks down and the gland is slowly destroyed.

!CTLA4, PTPN22, HLA susceptibility -> Loss of tolerance to TPO and Tg Loss of tolerance to TPO and Tg -> CD8 T cells kill follicular cells -> *Follicle destruction Loss of tolerance to TPO and Tg -> Th1 cells: IFN-γ activates macrophages -> *Follicle destruction Loss of tolerance to TPO and Tg -> Anti-TPO, anti-Tg antibodies (ADCC) -> *Follicle destruction *Follicle destruction -> !Atrophy, fibrosis: hypothyroidism
  • Diffusely enlarged, symmetrical; capsule intact
  • Cut surface pale, tan-grey, firm, slightly friable
  • Dense lymphocytes and plasma cells forming lymphoid follicles with germinal centres
  • Small, atrophic follicles lined by Hürthle cells: abundant granular eosinophilic cytoplasm (packed mitochondria)
  • Interstitial fibrosis; fibrous and IgG4-related variants

Clinical: painless goitre with gradual hypothyroidism, sometimes preceded by transient thyrotoxicosis (hashitoxicosis) as follicles break open. Anti-TPO and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies are present. Other autoimmune diseases are common, and the risk of extranodal marginal zone B-cell lymphoma of the thyroid is increased.

23.3 Goitre: diffuse and multinodular

A simple (non-toxic) goitre is the gland’s answer to impaired hormone synthesis, most often dietary iodine deficiency: less hormone, more TSH, and TSH makes follicular cells grow. The patient usually stays euthyroid. Endemic goitre affects more than 10% of a population in iodine-poor mountains (Himalayas, Andes); sporadic goitre affects young women and follows goitrogens (cabbage, cauliflower, cassava) or inherited enzyme defects (dyshormonogenetic goitre).

  • Multilobulated, asymmetrically enlarged; the largest goitres (over 2 kg); may extend behind the sternum (plunging goitre)
  • Nodules of brown gelatinous colloid with haemorrhage, fibrosis, calcification, cysts
  • Colloid-rich follicles lined by flat inactive epithelium, alternating with hyperplastic areas
  • Haemorrhage, haemosiderin, scarring, calcification
  • Nodules lack the complete capsule of an adenoma
Clinical: mass effects (airway obstruction, dysphagia, venous compression), cosmetic deformity; an autonomous nodule may cause thyrotoxicosis without eye signs (toxic multinodular goitre, Plummer syndrome). Malignancy is uncommon (under 5%), but a dominant nodule must be aspirated.

Iodine-deficiency goitre was historically endemic in the northern areas of Pakistan (Gilgit-Baltistan, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Azad Kashmir). Universal salt iodisation has improved coverage, but large multinodular goitres remain a common surgical specimen.

23.4 Thyroid nodules and neoplasms

Most solitary nodules are benign: follicular adenomas or non-neoplastic lumps (dominant goitre nodule, cyst). FNAC is the first investigation. Clues to malignancy: solitary nodule, young or male patient, past neck irradiation, cold nodule on scan, hardness, fixity, hoarseness or a neck node. A hot nodule is almost always benign.

Follicular adenoma

A benign, usually solitary neoplasm of follicular epithelium. Toxic (hot) adenomas carry gain-of-function TSHR or GNAS mutations (TSH-independent cAMP signalling); non-functioning ones may carry RAS, PIK3CA or PAX8–PPARG changes.

  • Solitary, spherical, encapsulated nodule (about 3 cm) compressing the surrounding gland
  • Grey-white to red-brown; haemorrhage, cystic change
  • Uniform colloid-filled follicles like normal thyroid; Hürthle cell variant
  • Focal endocrine atypia is not malignancy
  • Intact capsule, no capsular or vascular invasion

Look at the edge, not the centre. Benign and malignant follicular cells look the same, so a needle in the middle of the nodule cannot decide. Only the capsule and its vessels reveal invasion, so the whole nodule must be excised and its capsule sampled generously.

Thyroid carcinomas: the big four

Papillary > 85%, follicular 5–15%, medullary about 5%, anaplastic < 5%. Women predominate in early and middle adult life. Ionising radiation in the first two decades is the major risk factor (papillary); iodine deficiency favours follicular carcinoma.

Papillary: MAPK pathway activated by RET/PTC rearrangements (radiation), NTRK1 fusions or BRAF V600E (one-third to one-half; more aggressive). Follicular: RAS mutations, PI3K/AKT activation (PTEN, PIK3CA), PAX8–PPARG fusion t(2;3). Anaplastic: dedifferentiation with TP53 loss. Medullary: activating RET mutation, germline in MEN2 and familial cases.

Papillary carcinoma

Usually 25–50 years; the cancer that follows childhood neck irradiation. It may present first as an enlarged cervical lymph node.

  • Solitary or multifocal; circumscribed or infiltrative
  • Granular, firm, gritty (calcification); sometimes cystic
  • Branching papillae with fibrovascular cores, lined by cuboidal cells
  • Nuclei make the diagnosis, even without papillae: clear, empty “Orphan Annie eye” nuclei, pseudo-inclusions, grooves, overlapping
  • Psammoma bodies: concentric laminated calcifications
  • Lymphatic invasion common
Variants: follicular (encapsulated non-invasive forms are now NIFTP, low risk), tall cell (older, aggressive), diffuse sclerosing (young), microcarcinoma (< 1 cm). Prognosis: 10-year survival over 95%; nodal spread hardly alters it; worse with older age, extrathyroidal extension and lung metastases.

Papillary = the “P” cancer

Popular (commonest) · Past radiation · Papillae with cores · Pale “Orphan Annie” nuclei · Psammoma bodies · Palpable neck nodes · Prognosis excellent

Follicular carcinoma

Women aged 40–60, commoner in iodine-deficient regions. A single nodule, minimally invasive (well circumscribed) or widely invasive; small uniform follicles with colloid; nuclei lack papillary features; diagnosis rests on capsular or vascular invasion. Spreads by blood to lungs and bone; metastases take up radioiodine and thyroglobulin is the follow-up marker.

Medullary carcinoma

A neuroendocrine tumour of parafollicular C cells secreting calcitonin (marker), often CEA, sometimes VIP (diarrhoea) or ACTH. About 70% sporadic (adults, solitary) and 30% familial (MEN2A, MEN2B, familial MTC: younger, bilateral, multicentric); RET carriers have prophylactic thyroidectomy (MEN table, Chapter 24).

  • Solitary or bilateral nodules; firm, pale grey-tan, infiltrative
  • Polygonal to spindle cells in nests and trabeculae
  • Stromal amyloid from altered calcitonin (Congo red positive)
  • Background C-cell hyperplasia in familial cases

Anaplastic carcinoma

Elderly patients with a rapidly enlarging bulky neck mass compressing trachea and oesophagus; about half have a past or coexisting differentiated carcinoma. Pleomorphic giant cells, spindle cells (sarcoma-like) or both; cytokeratin positive. Mortality is close to 100%, usually within a year.

Papillary carcinoma slide in 10 seconds: finger-like papillae with thin fibrovascular cores; crowded, overlapping, clear “empty” nuclei (grooves and pink pseudo-inclusions at high power); purple laminated psammoma bodies. Graves papillae lack cores, and their nuclei are dark and round.

23.5 Parathyroid glands

Low free calcium, sensed by the calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR), releases PTH. PTH raises calcium and lowers phosphate: more renal calcium reabsorption, more phosphate excretion, more vitamin D activation (1α-hydroxylation) and more osteoclast activity (indirectly, via RANKL on osteoblasts).

Primary hyperparathyroidism

Autonomous PTH overproduction: adenoma 85–95%, primary hyperplasia 5–10% (sporadic or MEN1/MEN2A), carcinoma about 1%. Usually women over 50. It is the commonest cause of asymptomatic hypercalcaemia; malignancy is the commonest cause of symptomatic hypercalcaemia. Genes: MEN1 and cyclin D1 (CCND1) overexpression (sometimes by inversion next to the PTH gene promoter).

  • Solitary, well-circumscribed, soft, tan to red-brown nodule, 0.5–5 g, thin capsule
  • Other glands normal or shrunken
  • Uniform polygonal chief cells, few oxyphil cells; little or no fat
  • Rim of compressed normal parathyroid outside the capsule
Hyperplasia: all four glands enlarged. Carcinoma: diagnosed only by invasion or metastasis.

Effects

  • Bone: osteoporosis; osteitis fibrosa cystica (osteoclastic resorption, peritrabecular fibrosis, cysts); brown tumours (osteoclasts, giant cells, haemorrhage, haemosiderin) that mimic neoplasm
  • Kidney: nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis, polyuria
  • Metastatic calcification: stomach, lungs, kidneys, vessels
  • Gut, mind: constipation, peptic ulcer, pancreatitis, gallstones; depression, lethargy, weakness

“Painful bones, renal stones, abdominal groans, psychic moans”

The four faces of hypercalcaemia. Today most patients are found earlier, on a routine calcium.

Labs: calcium high, PTH high or inappropriately normal, phosphate low, urinary calcium high; alkaline phosphatase high with bone disease.

Who is wrong: the gland or the body? In primary disease the gland is wrong: it makes PTH despite high calcium. In secondary disease the gland is right: failing kidneys retain phosphate and cannot activate vitamin D, calcium falls, and all four glands enlarge to correct it (renal osteodystrophy; vascular calcification may cause calciphylaxis). In tertiary disease the gland has been driven so long that it no longer switches off.

Hypercalcaemia: check PTH first. High PTH = parathyroid cause. Suppressed PTH points to malignancy (PTH-related protein from squamous carcinomas, osteolytic metastases, myeloma), vitamin D excess or sarcoidosis. Medullary carcinoma secretes calcitonin but does not cause hypocalcaemia.

Hypoparathyroidism

Causes

  • Surgical: glands removed or devascularised at thyroidectomy (commonest)
  • Congenital absence: DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion), with thymic aplasia and heart defects
  • Autoimmune: APS-1 (AIRE) with mucocutaneous candidiasis and Addison disease
  • Autosomal dominant: gain-of-function CaSR mutation

Features of hypocalcaemia

  • Tetany: circumoral tingling, carpopedal spasm, laryngospasm, seizures
  • Chvostek sign (tap facial nerve → twitch); Trousseau sign (BP cuff → carpal spasm)
  • Psychosis, basal ganglia calcification, cataracts, prolonged QT, dental defects

Pseudohypoparathyroidism: end-organ resistance to PTH (GNAS defects), so low calcium and high phosphate coexist with high PTH; often short stature and short fourth and fifth metacarpals.

Patient A: TSH 26 mIU/L (0.4–4), free T4 low, anti-TPO strongly positive, firm goitre. Patient B: TSH < 0.01 mIU/L, free T4 high, painful thyroid, ESR 85 mm/h, radioiodine uptake very low. Patient C: calcium 11.6 mg/dL, phosphate low, PTH raised, renal stones. Interpret each and name the likely disease.

Answer

A: primary hypothyroidism; Hashimoto thyroiditis. B: thyrotoxicosis without hyperthyroidism (hormone leak); subacute granulomatous (de Quervain) thyroiditis. C: primary hyperparathyroidism, most likely a parathyroid adenoma.

RAPID REVISION The Night-Before Page

15 one-liners

  1. TSH is the best screening test; TSH and T4 move oppositely in primary disease.
  2. Thyrotoxicosis with low radioiodine uptake = thyroiditis or exogenous hormone.
  3. Graves: stimulating anti-TSH-receptor IgG; goitre, eyes, shins.
  4. Graves gland: beefy red; tall cells, scalloped colloid, papillae without cores.
  5. Hashimoto: anti-TPO, lymphoid follicles, Hürthle cells; hypothyroid.
  6. Hashimoto raises the risk of marginal zone B-cell lymphoma.
  7. De Quervain: painful, post-viral, giant cells engulf colloid, self-limited.
  8. Riedel: woody fibrosis beyond the capsule; IgG4-related.
  9. Goitre: iodine lack → TSH → hyperplasia → colloid → multinodular.
  10. Hot nodule: almost always benign; cold, solitary, young, male, irradiated: suspect.
  11. Follicular carcinoma = capsular or vascular invasion; spreads by blood.
  12. Papillary: clear grooved nuclei, psammoma, nodes; RET/PTC, BRAF.
  13. Medullary: C cells, calcitonin, amyloid stroma, RET, MEN2.
  14. Anaplastic: elderly, giant and spindle cells, TP53, death within a year.
  15. Primary HPT: adenoma 85–95%; high Ca, high PTH, low phosphate.

Memory hooks

  • Opposite = gland, same = pituitary: reading TSH with T4
  • P cancer: popular, past radiation, pale nuclei, psammoma, palpable nodes, prognosis good
  • Look at the edge: follicular carcinoma is a diagnosis of the capsule
  • Bones, stones, groans, moans: hypercalcaemia of primary HPT
