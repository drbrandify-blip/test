4.1 Polyps: what they are and how to classify them

A polyp is any mass that projects above the mucosal surface into the lumen. It may be sessile (broad-based, no stalk) or pedunculated (on a stalk). “Polyp” describes a shape, not a disease: the pathologist must decide whether it is reactive, hamartomatous or neoplastic, because only the neoplastic ones are on the road to cancer.

Hyperplastic polyps

Common in the 6th–7th decades; small (< 5 mm), often multiple, mostly in the left colon and rectum. Delayed shedding of surface cells makes them crowd and fold, so the glands look serrated (saw-tooth, star-shaped), but only in the upper crypt. No dysplasia and no meaningful malignant potential; the look-alike to exclude is the sessile serrated lesion.

Inflammatory polyps

In solitary rectal ulcer syndrome a poorly relaxing anorectal sphincter makes the rectal mucosa prolapse, ulcerate and heal repeatedly, producing a polyp of inflamed, regenerating mucosa with fibromuscular hyperplasia of the lamina propria. IBD pseudopolyps: see Chapter 3.

Hamartomatous polyps

A hamartoma is a disorganised overgrowth of tissues native to the site. Sporadic juvenile polyps are harmless, but the polyposis syndromes that produce hamartomas carry a real cancer risk.

4.2 Adenomas: the true precursors

Adenoma is a benign neoplastic polyp of glandular epithelium whose defining feature is epithelial dysplasia (intraepithelial neoplasia). Colonic adenomas are the precursor of most colorectal adenocarcinomas; about 30% of adults in Western countries have at least one by the age of 60.

Why dysplasia starts at the surface. In normal crypts, cells divide at the base and mature as they climb, stopping division before they reach the surface. In an adenoma the cells fail to mature as they rise, so the surface is lined by cells that still look like crypt-base stem cells: tall, crowded, with enlarged, elongated, hyperchromatic, stratified (“pencil”) nuclei and little mucin. That failed maturation is what the pathologist calls dysplasia.

Features that predict malignant change

  • Size: the most important. Cancer is very rare in adenomas < 1 cm; nearly 40% of those > 4 cm contain carcinoma
  • High-grade dysplasia
  • Villous architecture (largely a reflection of size)
  • Number of adenomas (multiple = higher risk)

Clinical features

  • Most are silent, found at screening colonoscopy
  • Occult bleeding → iron-deficiency anaemia
  • Rarely, a large villous adenoma secretes mucus rich in protein and potassium → hypoproteinaemia, hypokalaemia

Sessile serrated lesion (sessile serrated adenoma)

Looks like a hyperplastic polyp but behaves like an adenoma. Mostly in the right colon, flat and easily missed, and usually without classic cytological dysplasia. The clue is architectural: serration runs down to the crypt base, where crypts dilate and grow sideways (boot- or L-shaped). Malignant potential is similar to adenomas, via BRAF mutation and DNA methylation.

Intramucosal carcinoma and pseudo-invasion. Dysplastic cells confined to the lamina propria or muscularis mucosae (intramucosal carcinoma) almost never metastasise, because the mucosa has few lymphatics; invasion counts only once the submucosa is reached. Glands pushed into the stalk of a twisted polyp, with haemorrhage and haemosiderin, are pseudo-invasion, not carcinoma.

4.3 Familial polyposis and cancer syndromes

Two syndromes illustrate the two molecular routes to cancer: FAP (loss of a tumour suppressor, APC) and Lynch syndrome (loss of DNA mismatch repair).

Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) and its variants

Autosomal dominant germline mutation of APC (chromosome 5q21). Hundreds to thousands of adenomas carpet the colon from adolescence; at least 100 are needed for a diagnosis of classic FAP. Each polyp looks like an ordinary tubular adenoma, but with so many, colorectal adenocarcinoma develops in 100% of untreated patients, often before 30 years. Hence prophylactic colectomy. Other lesions: gastric and duodenal (ampullary) adenomas, fundic gland polyps and congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium (CHRPE).

Lynch syndrome (hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer, HNPCC)

Autosomal dominant germline mutation of a DNA mismatch-repair gene: most often MSH2 or MLH1, less often MSH6 or PMS2. It accounts for about 2–4% of colorectal cancers. Patients do not get hundreds of polyps (“non-polyposis”); instead, their few adenomas progress to cancer unusually fast. Cancers appear at a younger age than sporadic cancer, favour the right colon and are often mucinous. Extracolonic cancers: endometrium (commonest), ovary, stomach, small bowel, hepatobiliary tract, ureter and renal pelvis, brain and skin (sebaceous tumours).

3 relatives with a Lynch-type cancer, 2 successive generations, 1 diagnosed before 50 (FAP excluded). And: Lynch hits the Lining of the uterus.

4.4 How colorectal cancer develops

The APC/β-catenin pathway (adenoma–carcinoma sequence)

About 80% of sporadic cancers follow this chromosomal-instability route. It starts with loss of APC and proceeds by the stepwise addition of mutations, which we can see under the microscope as normal mucosa → adenoma → carcinoma.

APC (5q21): gatekeeper in the β-catenin destruction complex. KRAS: mutated in < 10% of adenomas under 1 cm but about half of larger ones; keeps growth signals on. SMAD2/4 (18q): carry growth-inhibitory TGF-β signals. TP53 (17p): lost in 70–80% of cancers but rarely in adenomas, so it marks invasion.

The mismatch-repair (MSI) and serrated pathways

Mismatch-repair enzymes proofread DNA after replication. When they fail, errors accumulate fastest in microsatellites (short tandem repeats such as CACACA), which lengthen or shorten: microsatellite instability (MSI). Genes that contain such repeats in their coding sequence, such as TGFBR2 (TGF-β receptor) and BAX (pro-apoptotic), are then knocked out. The defect is germline in Lynch syndrome, but in sporadic cancers it arises from methylation of the MLH1 promoter, usually in a sessile serrated lesion carrying a BRAF mutation (the CpG island methylator phenotype, CIMP).

4.5 Colorectal adenocarcinoma

Adenocarcinoma is the commonest malignancy of the gastrointestinal tract. Incidence peaks at 60–70 years; fewer than 20% of cases occur before 50. A cancer in a young patient should make you think of FAP, Lynch syndrome or long-standing colitis.

Risk factors: diet low in fibre and high in refined carbohydrate, fat and red or processed meat; obesity, physical inactivity, smoking; adenomas, inherited syndromes and inflammatory bowel disease (see Chapter 3). Protective: aspirin and other NSAIDs, which inhibit COX-2, an enzyme over-expressed in most adenomas and cancers.

Morphology

Tumours occur along the whole colon. The shape depends on the site: the wide caecum and ascending colon carry liquid stool, so tumours there grow outwards into the lumen; the narrow distal colon carries solid stool, so an encircling tumour soon obstructs.

  • Proximal (right): polypoid, exophytic, fungating masses along one wall; ulcerate and bleed; rarely obstruct
  • Distal (left): annular, encircling growth with heaped-up edges: “napkin-ring” constriction (“apple-core” on barium enema)
  • Cut surface grey-white, firm; ulcerated centre
  • Tall columnar cells resembling adenoma epithelium, forming irregular, back-to-back glands
  • Invasion through the muscularis mucosae into the wall with a strong desmoplastic stroma (makes the tumour firm)
  • Gland lumens contain necrotic “dirty necrosis”
  • Variants: mucinous (> 50% extracellular mucin), signet-ring cell: both poor prognosis

Two bedside rules. (1) An older man or post-menopausal woman with iron-deficiency anaemia has GI cancer until proven otherwise. (2) Streptococcus gallolyticus (S. bovis) bacteraemia or endocarditis is linked to colorectal cancer: do a colonoscopy.

Ileocaecal tuberculosis is common in Pakistan and can present as a right iliac fossa mass with weight loss and anaemia, mimicking caecal carcinoma (and Crohn disease). Biopsy settles it: caseating granulomas in TB, invasive dysplastic glands in carcinoma.

Spread, staging and prognosis

Routes of spread

  • Direct: through the wall into pericolic fat, bladder, uterus, vagina
  • Lymphatic: regional (pericolic, mesenteric) nodes
  • Haematogenous: portal vein → liver (commonest distant site); then lungs and bone. Low rectal tumours drain to systemic veins and can reach the lungs directly
  • Transcoelomic: peritoneal seeding

Prognostic factors

  • The two most important: depth of invasion and lymph node metastasis
  • Distant metastasis
  • Poor grade, mucinous or signet-ring type; lymphovascular invasion; involved margins
  • MSI-high tumours do better stage for stage
  • Most stage I patients are cured; few with distant metastases survive 5 years
