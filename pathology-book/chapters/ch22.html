22.1 Where breast lesions arise; inflammatory disorders

The breast is a branching duct system that ends in the terminal duct lobular unit (TDLU). Each duct and lobule has two cell layers: inner luminal epithelial cells and outer myoepithelial cells on a basement membrane. Rule of thumb: benign and in situ lesions keep the myoepithelial layer; invasive carcinoma loses it. Knowing where along the tree a lesion arises predicts what it looks like and how it presents.

Triple assessment of every breast lump: (1) clinical examination, (2) imaging (ultrasound in young women, mammography in older women) and (3) tissue sampling (FNAC or core needle biopsy). A lump is labelled benign only when all three agree.

Three benign lesions that look malignant: fat necrosis (hard mass + calcification), duct ectasia (nipple retraction) and sclerosing adenosis / radial scar (distorted glands on mammogram and slide). Every hard lump with skin or nipple retraction needs tissue diagnosis.

22.2 Benign epithelial lesions (fibrocystic change)

These are very common changes in women of reproductive age, often felt as premenstrual lumpiness and pain. Robbins groups them by the risk of later invasive carcinoma, which is the only thing that matters clinically. The risk applies to both breasts: these lesions are markers of risk, not direct precursors.

Non-proliferative changes

  • Cysts: dilated lobules; large ones are bluish (“blue-dome cysts”) with turbid fluid; lined by flattened or apocrine metaplastic cells (eosinophilic, granular cytoplasm); calcification common
  • Fibrosis: ruptured cysts provoke inflammation and scarring
  • Adenosis: more acini per lobule, may calcify

Proliferative changes

  • Epithelial hyperplasia: more than two cell layers filling ducts, with irregular peripheral slit-like spaces and mixed, streaming cells
  • Sclerosing adenosis: crowded acini compressed and distorted by stroma; myoepithelium kept; mimics invasive carcinoma
  • Radial scar (complex sclerosing lesion): stellate lesion with entrapped glands in a central scar
  • Atypical ductal hyperplasia: monomorphic cells partly filling ducts, resembling but not amounting to DCIS. Atypical lobular hyperplasia: LCIS-like cells filling less than half of a lobule

22.3 Fibroepithelial tumours and intraductal papilloma

Fibroadenoma and phyllodes tumour are biphasic: both stroma and epithelium proliferate, but the stroma is the neoplastic part. Both often carry MED12 mutations in their stromal cells. What separates them is how cellular and how aggressive that stroma is.

Fibroadenoma

The commonest benign tumour of the female breast, typically in women aged 20–35. It is hormone-responsive: it may enlarge in pregnancy and lactation, and regresses and calcifies after menopause. Multiple fibroadenomas occur in transplant patients on cyclosporine.

  • Well-circumscribed, freely mobile (“breast mouse”), rubbery nodule, usually a few cm
  • Grey-white cut surface that bulges, with slit-like spaces
  • Loose, delicate, cellular stroma enclosing glands lined by epithelial + myoepithelial cells
  • Intracanalicular pattern: stroma compresses ducts into curved slits; pericanalicular: ducts keep round lumens
  • Sharp border; stroma bland, few mitoses
Not premalignant. A simple fibroadenoma adds no risk; a “complex” one (cysts, sclerosing adenosis, calcification) carries the small risk of proliferative disease.

Phyllodes tumour

Also arises from intralobular stroma, but is much less common, occurs about 10–20 years later (peak in the sixth decade), grows larger and faster, and has more cellular stroma that overgrows the epithelium and pushes it into leaf-like (Greek phyllon) projections. Graded benign, borderline or malignant by stromal cellularity, atypia, mitoses, overgrowth and an infiltrative border. Most behave benignly but recur locally unless widely excised; malignant ones metastasise by blood (lymph-node spread is rare).

Intraductal papilloma

A benign papillary growth in a duct. The common solitary large-duct papilloma sits in a lactiferous sinus beneath the nipple and presents with serous or bloody nipple discharge, sometimes a small subareolar lump. Each papilla has a fibrovascular core covered by two cell layers: luminal epithelium and myoepithelium. Papillary carcinoma lacks the myoepithelial layer. Multiple small-duct papillomas lie deeper; a papilloma with atypia raises the risk.

22.4 Carcinoma of the breast: risk and pathogenesis

The commonest cancer of women worldwide (excluding skin) and a leading cause of cancer death in women. In the USA the lifetime risk is about 1 in 8. Almost all are adenocarcinomas arising in the TDLU.

Breast cancer is the commonest cancer in Pakistani women, and Pakistan has one of the highest breast cancer rates in Asia; the lifetime risk is often quoted as about 1 in 9 women. Many women present late, in stage III–IV, because of stigma, shyness and the absence of screening. Awareness campaigns such as Pink Ribbon Pakistan teach breast self-awareness and early consultation for any lump.

Risk factors (Robbins)

  • Female sex and age (rare before 25)
  • Family history in first-degree relatives; germline mutations
  • Long oestrogen exposure: early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, late first full-term pregnancy
  • Postmenopausal hormone therapy (oestrogen + progestin); obesity after menopause; alcohol
  • Atypical hyperplasia; previous breast or endometrial cancer
  • Chest radiation when young (e.g. for Hodgkin lymphoma); dense breasts
  • Protective: breastfeeding, early first pregnancy, exercise

Hereditary breast cancer

A minority (roughly one in ten) arise from an inherited high-penetrance mutation; suspect it with early onset, bilateral disease, male breast cancer, or ovarian cancer in the family.

One morphology, three diseases. Breast carcinomas that look alike under the microscope follow three different molecular roads, and the road decides behaviour and treatment. Robbins groups them by two questions: does the tumour express oestrogen receptor (ER)? and is HER2 (ERBB2) amplified? Oestrogen drives the growth of ER-positive cells, which is why hormone exposure is a risk factor and why blocking oestrogen treats them.

22.5 Carcinoma in situ and invasive carcinoma

In situ carcinoma is confined by the basement membrane, with the myoepithelial layer still present, so it cannot metastasise. The names “ductal” and “lobular” describe the appearance of the cells, not the site: both arise in the TDLU.

DCIS patterns. Comedo DCIS: high-grade cells around central necrosis that calcifies; cheesy necrotic plugs squeeze out of cut ducts like comedones. Non-comedo DCIS: cribriform (evenly spaced, round “cookie-cutter” spaces), solid, papillary or micropapillary; usually lower grade.

Paget disease of the nipple: DCIS (usually high grade, often HER2-positive) spreads up the lactiferous ducts into the nipple epidermis without crossing the basement membrane. The nipple shows a unilateral, red, scaly, crusted eruption that is easily mistaken for eczema. Paget cells are large, with pale abundant cytoplasm, lying singly or in groups in the epidermis. About half the patients have an underlying invasive carcinoma, usually with a palpable mass.

Invasive carcinoma of no special type (NST; invasive ductal carcinoma)

The commonest type, about 70–80%. About half arise in the upper outer quadrant. It presents as a hard, painless lump; tethering to Cooper ligaments causes skin dimpling, involvement of ducts causes nipple retraction, and blocked dermal lymphatics cause peau d’orange (orange-peel skin).

  • Hard (scirrhous), irregular, stellate mass with infiltrating “crab-like” edges
  • Grey-white, gritty cut surface with chalky yellow-white streaks (elastosis); grates like an unripe pear
  • May tether skin, retract nipple, fix to pectoral fascia
  • Cords, nests, glands and sheets of malignant ductal cells infiltrating a dense desmoplastic stroma
  • No myoepithelial cells around the invasive nests
  • Lymphovascular invasion; accompanying DCIS
  • Nottingham grade 1–3: tubule formation + nuclear pleomorphism + mitotic count

Invasive lobular carcinoma

  • The second commonest type (roughly 10–15%); more often bilateral and multicentric
  • Discohesive cells infiltrating in single file (“Indian file”) or in targetoid rings around ducts; signet-ring cells; little desmoplasia
  • Hallmark: loss of E-cadherin (CDH1); almost all ER-positive
  • May form only a vague thickening, hard to feel or see on mammography
  • Unusual metastatic sites: peritoneum and retroperitoneum, leptomeninges, GI tract, ovaries and uterus, bone marrow

Prognostic factors

  • Distant metastasis (stage IV): cure unlikely
  • Axillary lymph node status: the most important prognostic factor in the absence of distant spread; assessed by sentinel lymph node biopsy
  • Tumour size; locally advanced disease (skin, chest wall); inflammatory carcinoma

  • Proliferation (mitoses, Ki-67), lymphovascular invasion, molecular subtype

Predictive factors (guide therapy)

Spread

A 48-year-old woman has a 4 cm hard, painless lump in the upper outer quadrant with dimpled skin and two firm axillary nodes. Core biopsy: invasive carcinoma NST, grade 3. Immunohistochemistry: ER 0%, PR 0%, HER2 3+, Ki-67 60%.

Reasoning: T2 N1 → stage II. ER/PR negative with HER2 overexpression = HER2-enriched subtype. She needs chemotherapy with trastuzumab; endocrine therapy would be useless. Her nodes, not the tumour size, carry most prognostic weight.

22.7 The male breast

Gynaecomastia

Enlargement of the male breast from an imbalance of oestrogen (stimulates) over androgen (inhibits). Causes: puberty and old age (physiological), Klinefelter syndrome, cirrhosis (the liver fails to clear oestrogen), drugs (spironolactone, digoxin, cimetidine, anabolic steroids, alcohol, marijuana), and hormone-secreting tumours (testicular Leydig cell tumour, hCG-secreting tumours).

Morphology: button-like subareolar enlargement; dense collagenous stroma around ducts with micropapillary epithelial hyperplasia; lobules are rarely formed.

Carcinoma of the male breast

  • Rare: less than 1% of breast cancers; older men
  • Risk: BRCA2, first-degree relatives, Klinefelter, oestrogen excess, testicular failure, chest radiation
  • Subareolar mass or nipple discharge; because there is little breast tissue it invades skin and chest wall early
  • Mostly ER-positive NST carcinoma; stage for stage, outlook is similar to women, but many present late

Lab slides in 10 seconds. Fibroadenoma: a sharply bordered nodule of loose pale stroma with slit-like (or round) ducts lined by two cell layers: “ducts squeezed by stroma”. Carcinoma (NST): no border; irregular nests and cords of pleomorphic cells scattered through dense pink fibrous stroma, single cells invading fat, and no myoepithelial rim. Lobular carcinoma: small bland cells marching in single file.
