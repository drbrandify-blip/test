26.1 Ischaemic heart disease: the big picture

Ischaemic heart disease (IHD) is a group of related syndromes caused by myocardial ischaemia: an imbalance between the heart’s demand for oxygenated blood and its supply. In more than 90% of cases the cause is reduced coronary flow from atherosclerosis; IHD is therefore often called coronary artery disease.

Ischaemia cuts oxygen and also nutrient delivery and waste removal, so it is worse than hypoxia alone. Other causes: emboli, vasospasm, vasculitis; raised demand (tachycardia, LVH); poor oxygen carriage (anaemia, CO, shock).

Four mechanisms, one disease

  • Fixed stenosis. Symptoms on exertion when ≥ 70% of the lumen is narrowed (“critical stenosis”); at rest when ≥ 90%.
  • Acute plaque change. Rupture or fissuring, erosion or ulceration, or haemorrhage into the plaque exposes thrombogenic material.
  • Thrombosis on the disrupted plaque: partial and transient, or total and persistent.
  • Vasoconstriction (platelet thromboxane, endothelial dysfunction) narrows the lumen further.
!Atherosclerotic plaque -> Fixed stenosis ≥ 70% -> *Stable angina on exertion !Atherosclerotic plaque -> Acute plaque change -> Platelets + thrombus Platelets + thrombus -[partial or transient]-> *Unstable angina, NSTEMI, sudden death Platelets + thrombus -[total and persistent]-> *Transmural MI (STEMI)

The dangerous plaque is not the biggest one. Most plaques that rupture and cause MI were only mildly to moderately stenotic beforehand. What makes a plaque vulnerable is a large soft lipid core, a thin fibrous cap and many macrophages digesting collagen with metalloproteinases. This is why a man with a “normal” exercise test can still infarct (see Chapter 25 for plaque structure).

The IHD syndromes

  • Stable angina: fixed stenosis; exertional pain relieved by rest or nitroglycerin
  • Prinzmetal (variant) angina: coronary vasospasm; pain at rest, ST elevation
  • Unstable (crescendo) angina: plaque disruption + non-occlusive thrombus; heralds MI
  • Myocardial infarction: occlusive thrombus → myocyte death (26.2)
  • Chronic IHD: repeated infarcts → progressive heart failure
  • Sudden cardiac death: lethal arrhythmia within about 1 hour of symptoms

Acute coronary syndrome = unstable angina, acute MI and sudden cardiac death: all three begin with acute plaque change. IHD is a leading cause of death in Pakistan, where South Asians develop it younger.

26.2 Myocardial infarction

Myocardial infarction (MI) is death (necrosis) of cardiac muscle due to prolonged severe ischaemia, usually from an occlusive thrombus on a disrupted coronary atherosclerotic plaque. Risk rises with age, male sex (women catch up after menopause), hypertension, smoking, diabetes and dyslipidaemia.

Pathogenesis: a race against time

Within seconds of occlusion aerobic metabolism stops, anaerobic glycolysis takes over and ATP falls; contractility is lost within 2 minutes (so even brief ischaemia can cause heart failure). Lactic acid accumulates. Severe ischaemia for 20–40 minutes causes irreversible injury; microvascular injury follows after about an hour, and the infarct reaches its full extent in 3–6 hours.

Why the subendocardium dies first. It is the last zone reached by blood from the epicardial coronary arteries and it sits under the highest intramural pressure. Necrosis therefore starts there and spreads outwards as a wavefront towards the epicardium over 3–6 hours. This is the scientific basis of “time is muscle”: reperfusion in the first hours salvages the outer myocardium.

Patterns of infarction

  • Transmural (STEMI): full or nearly full wall thickness in one coronary territory; occlusive thrombus.
  • Subendocardial (NSTEMI): inner third to half of the wall; transient or partial thrombus, or shock (circumferential necrosis).
  • Microscopic: small-vessel occlusion (vasculitis, emboli).

Reperfusion changes the picture. Opening the artery (thrombolysis, PCI) saves muscle but the injured zone becomes haemorrhagic (damaged vessels leak) and shows contraction band necrosis: calcium floods into dying cells and the myofibrils hypercontract into dense eosinophilic bands. Oxygen free radicals add reperfusion injury. Salvaged muscle may stay non-contractile for days: stunned myocardium.

Clinical features and laboratory diagnosis

Severe crushing substernal pain radiating to the left arm, jaw or epigastrium, lasting more than 30 minutes and not relieved by nitroglycerin; sweating, nausea, dyspnoea. About 10–15% are “silent”, especially in diabetics (autonomic neuropathy) and the elderly. Diagnosis rests on symptoms, ECG (ST elevation, Q waves) and cardiac biomarkers leaked from dead myocytes.

Who ruptures? Age > 60, anterior or lateral wall, women, first MI (no old scar to buttress the wall) and no LVH. A ventricular aneurysm, unlike rupture, is late and rarely ruptures because its wall is tough scar.

26.3 Hypertensive heart disease and cor pulmonale

Both are pressure-overload diseases. A ventricle pumping against high resistance adds sarcomeres in parallel: myocytes get thicker, the wall thickens and the cavity stays normal or small. This is concentric hypertrophy, in contrast to the eccentric hypertrophy (wall thickening plus dilatation) of volume overload such as regurgitant valves.

Systemic (left-sided) hypertensive heart disease

Minimal criteria: (1) left ventricular hypertrophy, usually concentric, without other cardiovascular disease to explain it, and (2) a history or pathological evidence of hypertension.

  • LV wall > 2.0 cm thick; heart may exceed 500 g
  • Cavity normal or small (concentric); left atrium enlarged
  • Broad myocytes with enlarged, hyperchromatic, rectangular (“boxcar”) nuclei
  • Interstitial fibrosis; hyaline arteriolosclerosis
!Sustained hypertension -> LV pressure overload -> *Concentric LVH -> Stiff wall: diastolic dysfunction -> Left atrial dilatation, AF *Concentric LVH -> Demand exceeds supply -> !Heart failure, IHD, sudden death

Cor pulmonale (pulmonary hypertensive heart disease)

Cor pulmonale is right ventricular hypertrophy and/or dilatation caused by pulmonary hypertension arising from disorders of the lung parenchyma or pulmonary vasculature. RV changes due to left-sided heart disease or congenital shunts are excluded by definition.

  • Acute: massive pulmonary embolism → sudden RV dilatation without hypertrophy; may cause death.
  • Chronic: COPD (commonest), interstitial fibrosis, recurrent small emboli, primary pulmonary hypertension, sleep apnoea, kyphoscoliosis.
  • Chronic morphology: RV wall ≥ 1.0 cm (normal 0.3–0.5 cm), may approach LV thickness.
  • Right heart failure: raised JVP, nutmeg liver, ankle oedema.

26.4 Rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease

Rheumatic fever (RF) is an acute, immunologically mediated, multisystem inflammatory disease that follows group A β-haemolytic streptococcal pharyngitis after an interval of a few weeks. Chronic rheumatic heart disease (RHD) is its main sequel: deforming fibrotic valvular disease, especially mitral stenosis.

!Group A strep pharyngitis -> Antibodies + CD4 T cells to M protein -> *Molecular mimicry: cross-react with heart -> Acute RF: pancarditis, arthritis -> Repeated attacks, healing by fibrosis -> !Chronic RHD: fish-mouth mitral valve

Why the heart? Antibodies and CD4+ T cells against streptococcal M protein cross-react with cardiac self-antigens (molecular mimicry); T-cell cytokines activate macrophages, hence the Aschoff body. Only about 3% of untreated patients develop RF (genetic susceptibility). Skin infection does not cause RF (it can cause PSGN, Chapter 16).

Major criteria: Joints (migratory polyarthritis of large joints) · ♥ Carditis · Nodules (subcutaneous) · Erythema marginatum · Sydenham chorea

Jones criteria: evidence of recent group A strep infection (raised ASO titre, positive throat culture or rapid antigen) plus two major, or one major and two minor criteria. Minor: fever, arthralgia, raised ESR/CRP, prolonged PR interval on ECG.

  • Pancarditis: all three layers
  • Pericarditis: fibrinous or serofibrinous (“bread-and-butter”); resolves
  • Myocarditis: scattered Aschoff bodies; may cause dilatation and failure (the cause of death in the acute stage)
  • Valvulitis: fibrinoid necrosis along lines of closure → tiny 1–2 mm warty vegetations (verrucae); mitral and aortic
  • MacCallum plaques: subendocardial thickenings in the left atrium
  • Focus of T lymphocytes, occasional plasma cells and plump activated macrophages
  • Anitschkow cells: macrophages with abundant cytoplasm and a central nucleus whose chromatin forms a slender wavy ribbon (“caterpillar cells”); some fuse into multinucleate Aschoff giant cells
  • Early lesions: central fibrinoid necrosis
  • Later replaced by fibrous scar
Chronic RHD: mitral valve involved in almost all cases (mitral alone 65–70%, mitral + aortic about 25%). Leaflets thickened and fibrotic, commissures fused, chordae tendineae short, thick and fused → a rigid funnel with a “fish-mouth” or “buttonhole” orifice; calcification and neovascularisation of leaflets. Aschoff bodies are rarely found now. The left atrium dilates and may hold a mural thrombus; lungs show chronic venous congestion; the RV hypertrophies.

Clinical: acute RF peaks at 5–15 years: fever and migratory polyarthritis (one large joint after another), carditis with friction rub, murmurs, arrhythmia. Arthritis heals without deformity. Chronic RHD appears decades later with mitral stenosis and its consequences: atrial fibrillation, left atrial thrombus and embolic stroke, pulmonary hypertension, right heart failure and infective endocarditis on the scarred valve.

Rheumatic fever and RHD remain common in Pakistan, unlike high-income countries where they have almost vanished. The classic local presentation is a young woman with mitral stenosis, often first unmasked in pregnancy, when the rise in blood volume and heart rate tips her into pulmonary oedema. Prompt penicillin for streptococcal sore throat and secondary prophylaxis prevent most of this disease.

26.5 Infective endocarditis and other valve disease

Infective endocarditis (IE) is microbial infection of the heart valves or mural endocardium that leads to formation of vegetations of thrombotic debris and organisms, often with destruction of the underlying cardiac tissue.

Organisms and predispositions

  • Viridans streptococci 50–60%: dental work, damaged valves
  • S. aureus 10–20%: the major organism in IV drug users, in whom the tricuspid valve is typical
  • Coagulase-negative staphylococci (S. epidermidis): prosthetic valves
  • Enterococci, HACEK group (oral commensals); about 10% culture-negative
  • Risk: RHD, MVP, calcific or bicuspid valves, prosthetic valves, catheters, immunosuppression, diabetes

Morphology

Friable, bulky, potentially destructive vegetations, single or multiple, mostly on aortic and mitral valves. They may perforate cusps, rupture chordae or erode into the myocardium as a ring abscess. Micro: fibrin, neutrophils and colonies of bacteria over a necrotic valve.

Calcific aortic stenosis

The commonest valve abnormality: age-related “wear and tear” presenting in the 70s–80s, or in the 50s–60s on a congenital bicuspid valve. Valve cells turn osteoblast-like and calcify the cusps.

  • Gross: heaped-up calcified masses on the outflow surfaces of the cusps, bulging into the sinuses of Valsalva; no commissural fusion and a normal mitral valve (rheumatic AS: fused commissures + mitral disease)
  • Consequence: concentric LVH → angina, syncope, heart failure; prognosis poor once symptoms start unless the valve is replaced

Mitral valve prolapse (myxomatous mitral valve)

Leaflets are floppy and balloon back into the left atrium in systole. A few per cent of adults, more women; also in Marfan syndrome.

  • Gross: enlarged, redundant, hooded leaflets; elongated, thinned chordae
  • Micro: thinned fibrosa; myxomatous degeneration: spongiosa expanded by mucoid (proteoglycan) material
  • Usually silent (mid-systolic click); few develop IE, mitral regurgitation, emboli or arrhythmias

Cardiomyopathy is heart disease resulting from a primary abnormality of the myocardium, not explained by coronary, valvular, hypertensive or congenital disease. Many are genetic. They are grouped by function into three patterns.

How HCM obstructs itself and kills. The bulging septum narrows the outflow tract; in systole the anterior mitral leaflet is drawn towards it (systolic anterior motion), so obstruction is dynamic and worsens with dehydration, standing or exercise. The thick, stiff ventricle fills poorly (diastolic failure), and the disarrayed, scarred myocardium is a substrate for ventricular fibrillation. Screen first-degree relatives.

Myocarditis (brief)

Inflammation of the myocardium with myocyte injury. Viruses are the commonest cause (Coxsackie A and B, other enteroviruses); others: Trypanosoma cruzi (Chagas), diphtheria toxin, Lyme disease, drug hypersensitivity (eosinophils), giant cell myocarditis, SLE, transplant rejection.

Micro: interstitial lymphocytic infiltrate with focal myocyte necrosis. It may heal, cause sudden death, or progress to dilated cardiomyopathy.

  2. Acute coronary syndromes start with acute plaque change + thrombus.
  3. Myocyte injury irreversible after 20–40 min; infarct complete in 3–6 h.
  4. LAD 40–50%, RCA 30–40%, LCX 15–20% of infarcts.
  5. Neutrophils 1–3 d; macrophages 3–7 d; granulation 1–2 wk; scar by 2 months.
  6. TTC: viable muscle brick-red, infarct pale.
  7. Troponin: up 2–4 h, peak ~48 h, lasts 7–10 d; CK-MB gone by 72 h.
  8. Rupture at 3–7 d; pericarditis 2–3 d; Dressler weeks; aneurysm late.
  9. Hypertensive heart: concentric LVH, wall > 2 cm, boxcar nuclei.
  10. Cor pulmonale: RVH from lung disease; acute = massive PE.
  11. RF: strep throat → M-protein mimicry → Aschoff bodies, pancarditis.
  12. Chronic RHD: mitral almost always; fish-mouth stenosis, LA thrombus.
  13. Acute IE: S. aureus, normal valve; subacute: viridans strep, damaged valve.
  14. Calcific AS: outflow-side calcium, no commissural fusion; MVP: myxomatous.
  15. DCM systolic (EF < 40%); HCM β-MHC disarray; RCM amyloid.
