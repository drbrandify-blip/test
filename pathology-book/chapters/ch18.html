18.1 Renal cell carcinoma and benign renal tumours

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is a malignant tumour of renal tubular epithelium, arising in the cortex. It forms about 3% of adult cancers, peaks in the sixth and seventh decades and is about twice as common in men.

Risk factors

  • Tobacco (the most important), obesity, hypertension
  • Asbestos, petroleum products, heavy metals; unopposed oestrogen
  • End-stage kidneys with acquired cystic disease (Chapter 17), tuberous sclerosis

Familial RCC (a few per cent)

  • von Hippel–Lindau: bilateral clear cell RCC, cerebellar and retinal haemangioblastomas
  • Hereditary papillary RCC (MET); Birt–Hogg–Dubé (FLCN)

!VHL loss on 3p (both alleles) -> HIF-1α not tagged for degradation -> *Hypoxia programme switched on *Hypoxia programme switched on -> VEGF, PDGF: rich vasculature *Hypoxia programme switched on -> TGF-α, GLUT1: growth, glycolysis *Hypoxia programme switched on -> Erythropoietin: polycythaemia

One gene explains the whole picture. The VHL protein is part of a ubiquitin ligase that destroys hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF). Without it, the tumour cell behaves as if permanently starved of oxygen: it builds new blood vessels (hence haemorrhage and early spread into veins), switches to glycolysis and stores glycogen and lipid (dissolved in processing, leaving clear cytoplasm and a yellow cut surface), and may secrete erythropoietin. Anti-VEGF drugs and mTOR inhibitors are used against advanced disease.

Clear cell carcinoma: morphology

  • Solitary, spherical, 3–15 cm, anywhere in the cortex; well demarcated, bulging
  • Variegated cut surface: golden-yellow (lipid) with grey-white, red-brown haemorrhagic, necrotic and cystic areas
  • May grow as a solid column up the renal vein into the IVC, even the right heart
  • Sheets, nests or tubules of round to polygonal cells with abundant clear cytoplasm (glycogen, lipid) and distinct membranes
  • Small round nuclei; nucleolar prominence sets the WHO/ISUP grade (1–4)
  • Rich, delicate branching vascular network between nests
  • Sarcomatoid (spindle) change = high grade, poor prognosis

Clinical features and spread

  • Classic triad of haematuria, flank pain, palpable mass in only about 10%; haematuria is the most reliable sign
  • “The internist’s tumour”: fever, weight loss, paraneoplastic syndromes
  • About a quarter have metastases at presentation: lungs (cannon-ball deposits, over half), bones (lytic, about a third), nodes, liver, adrenals, brain
  • Left tumour in the renal vein may cause a left varicocele
  • 5-year survival about 70% (up to 95% without metastases)
  • Polycythaemia: erythropoietin
  • Hypercalcaemia: PTH-related peptide
  • Hypertension: renin
  • Cushing syndrome: ACTH-like hormone
  • Feminisation or masculinisation: gonadotrophins
  • Eosinophilia, leukaemoid reaction, amyloidosis

RCC slide in 10 seconds: nests and sheets of cells with water-clear cytoplasm and sharp cell borders, each nest wrapped by thin-walled capillaries, with fresh haemorrhage around. Small round dark nuclei. Look-alikes: adrenal cortex (finely vacuolated, not empty), clear cell areas of other tumours. Ask about VHL/3p and polycythaemia.

18.2 Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma)

The commonest primary renal tumour of childhood, peaking at 2–5 years; about 95% occur before 10 years and 5–10% are bilateral. It arises from persistent embryonic kidney (metanephric blastema); nephrogenic rests in the adjacent kidney are its precursor lesions.

  • Large, solitary, well-circumscribed mass; 5–10% bilateral or multicentric
  • Soft, homogeneous, tan-grey (“fish-flesh”) cut surface with haemorrhage, cysts and necrosis
  • Triphasic: (1) blastema: sheets of small blue cells; (2) epithelial: abortive tubules and glomeruli; (3) stroma: fibrocytic or myxoid, sometimes skeletal muscle
  • Anaplasia (about 5%): huge hyperchromatic nuclei, abnormal mitoses; TP53 mutation, chemoresistance
Clinical: a large abdominal mass found by the mother while bathing the child, sometimes with haematuria, pain or hypertension; spreads to the lungs. With surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy, long-term survival is about 90%; anaplasia is the main adverse histological feature.

18.3 Urothelial tumours of the bladder

About 90% of bladder cancers are urothelial (transitional cell) carcinomas; squamous cell carcinoma (3–7% in the West, far more where schistosomiasis is endemic) and adenocarcinoma make up the rest. Men are affected about three times as often, mostly at 50–80 years. The same tumour arises, less often, in the renal pelvis and ureter.

Risk factors

  • Cigarette smoking: the most important; raises risk 3- to 7-fold
  • Arylamines (2-naphthylamine, benzidine): aniline dye, rubber, leather and textile workers; latency 15–40 years
  • Long-term cyclophosphamide (acrolein; also haemorrhagic cystitis)
  • Pelvic radiation; arsenic; analgesic (phenacetin) abuse for renal pelvis tumours
  • Schistosoma haematobium → mainly squamous carcinoma

Tobacco is the bladder’s carcinogen. In the Global Adult Tobacco Survey (2014), about 19% of Pakistani adults used tobacco in some form (men about 32%, women about 6%). Aromatic amines from smoke are excreted and concentrated in urine, which then bathes the urothelium for hours, so stopping smoking is the single most effective prevention.

Clinical: painless haematuria, often the only symptom; CIS causes frequency, urgency and dysuria. A tumour over a ureteric orifice causes hydronephrosis. The whole urothelium is exposed to the same urinary carcinogens (field effect), so tumours are multifocal and recur at new sites. Diagnosis: cystoscopy and biopsy; urine cytology is sensitive for high-grade tumours and CIS. Treatment: transurethral resection; intravesical BCG for CIS and high-risk superficial tumours; radical cystectomy for muscle invasion.

Squamous vs urothelial. Schistosoma haematobium eggs in the bladder wall cause chronic inflammation → squamous metaplasia → dysplasia → keratinising SCC, usually invasive at diagnosis (Egypt, Sudan). Long-standing stones and catheters do the same. Adenocarcinoma arises at the dome from urachal remnants or in bladder exstrophy.

Transitional cell carcinoma slide in 10 seconds: finger-like papillae with thin fibrovascular cores covered by thick, many-layered urothelium. Then grade: orderly cells with uniform nuclei = low grade; crowded, discohesive cells with large dark pleomorphic nuclei and mitoses at all levels = high grade. Always check the base for invasion of muscle.

18.4 Urolithiasis

Urolithiasis is the formation of calculi anywhere in the urinary collecting system, most often in the kidney. It is common, commoner in men, peaks at 20–30 years and runs in families.

Morphology: unilateral in about 80%; usually in the renal pelvis, calyces and bladder. Most are small (2–3 mm), smooth or jagged; a staghorn calculus is a branching cast of the pelvicalyceal system.

Clinical: silent, or renal colic (severe loin pain radiating to the groin) as a stone passes down the ureter, with haematuria. Complications: obstruction and hydronephrosis, infection (pyelonephritis, pyonephrosis), ulceration and, in the bladder, squamous metaplasia.

Small stones hurt; big stones hide. A small stone can enter the ureter, where it obstructs and triggers violent peristalsis: colic. A large staghorn cannot leave the pelvis, so it may stay silent for years, announcing itself only by haematuria, infection or slow destruction of the kidney.

Pakistan lies in the Afro-Asian “stone belt”: hot summers, outdoor labour and low fluid intake keep urine concentrated, so urolithiasis is very common. Calcium oxalate stones predominate, and bladder stones in children are still seen in poor rural areas. The cheapest prevention is drinking enough water to pass at least 2 litres of urine a day.

18.5 Hydronephrosis

Hydronephrosis is dilatation of the renal pelvis and calyces, with progressive atrophy of the parenchyma, caused by obstruction to the outflow of urine.

Causes

  • Congenital: pelviureteric junction obstruction, posterior urethral valves, urethral stricture, severe reflux
  • Stones, sloughed papillae, blood clot
  • Tumours: BPH, carcinoma of prostate, bladder or cervix; retroperitoneal lymphoma
  • Inflammation, retroperitoneal fibrosis; neurogenic bladder; pregnancy (mild)
  • Obstruction below the bladder neck → bilateral; above it → unilateral

Pathogenesis

Filtration continues after obstruction; filtrate leaks back into the interstitium and the raised pelvic pressure is transmitted to the collecting ducts. This compresses the medullary vessels, causing venous stasis and ischaemic atrophy, first of the papillae and then of the cortex. Tubular function (concentration) fails first; GFR falls later.

  • Pelvis and calyces dilated; papillae flattened, then cupped
  • Parenchyma compressed to a thin rim; late, a large thin-walled cystic sac
  • Dilated, then atrophic tubules; interstitial fibrosis and inflammation
  • Glomeruli preserved until late
Clinical: acute obstruction is painful; unilateral obstruction may be silent while the other kidney copes. Partial bilateral obstruction causes polyuria (concentrating defect), distal tubular acidosis, salt wasting and hypertension; complete bilateral obstruction causes anuria, and relief is followed by a post-obstructive diuresis.
