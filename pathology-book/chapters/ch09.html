9.1 Obstructive vs restrictive lung disease

Obstructive diseases raise resistance to airflow anywhere from trachea to respiratory bronchiole, so air is trapped and expiration is slow. Restrictive diseases reduce expansion of the lung, either because the parenchyma is stiff (interstitial fibrosis) or because the chest wall cannot move; lung volumes shrink but the air that is inhaled comes out fast.

9.2 Emphysema

COPD is persistent, poorly reversible airflow limitation caused mainly by smoking; its two components, emphysema and chronic bronchitis, usually coexist. Only a minority of smokers develop significant COPD, so genes and other exposures matter.

Emphysema is irreversible enlargement of the airspaces distal to the terminal bronchiole, accompanied by destruction of their walls without significant fibrosis. It is a morphological definition; chronic bronchitis is a clinical one.

  • Centriacinar: respiratory bronchioles enlarged, distal alveoli spared; upper lobes (apical segments); black pigment in walls; heavy smokers, often with chronic bronchitis. More than 95% of clinically significant emphysema.
  • Panacinar: whole acinus enlarged uniformly; lower zones, worst at the bases; α1-antitrypsin deficiency.
  • Distal acinar (paraseptal): next to pleura and septa, upper lung, near scars; forms bullae → spontaneous pneumothorax in young adults.
  • Irregular (airspace enlargement with fibrosis): around old scars; common but clinically insignificant.

Pathogenesis

!Tobacco or biomass smoke -> Neutrophils, macrophages, CD8 T cells -> *Unopposed proteases: elastase, MMPs !Tobacco or biomass smoke -> Oxidants (ROS) -> Antiproteases inactivated -> *Unopposed proteases: elastase, MMPs α1-antitrypsin deficiency (PiZZ) -> *Unopposed proteases: elastase, MMPs *Unopposed proteases: elastase, MMPs -> Alveolar walls destroyed -> !Emphysema: recoil lost Neutrophils, macrophages, CD8 T cells -> Mucus hypersecretion, bronchiolitis -> !Chronic bronchitis
  • Inflammation: neutrophils, macrophages, CD4/CD8 T cells; LTB4, IL-8, TNF.
  • Protease–antiprotease imbalance: neutrophil elastase and macrophage MMPs digest elastic tissue faster than antiproteases neutralise them.
  • Oxidant–antioxidant imbalance: smoke free radicals injure cells and inactivate α1-antitrypsin. Infection drives exacerbations.

α1-antitrypsin (gene SERPINA1, chromosome 14) is the main serum inhibitor of neutrophil elastase. PiZZ homozygotes have very low levels and develop panacinar emphysema early, much earlier if they smoke, plus liver disease (PAS-positive globules in hepatocytes).

Why emphysema obstructs although the airways are open. Small airways have no cartilage; alveolar walls hold them open like guy-ropes on a tent. Destroy the walls and the lung loses elastic recoil, and bronchioles collapse during expiration, trapping air. The lungs hyperinflate and the patient breathes out through pursed lips to splint the airways.

  • Advanced panacinar: pale, voluminous lungs that cover the heart when the chest is opened
  • Centriacinar: changes worse in the upper two-thirds
  • Bullae (large subpleural blebs) in distal acinar and irregular types
  • Thinned, destroyed alveolar walls; adjacent alveoli fuse into large airspaces
  • Free-floating fragments of septa; reduced capillary bed
  • Bronchioles deformed and narrowed; bronchiolar inflammation and submucosal fibrosis
Clinical: dyspnoea is the first symptom, insidious and steadily progressive; cough and wheeze; weight loss; barrel chest; sits hunched forward, breathing through pursed lips. Death from respiratory acidosis and coma, right heart failure (cor pulmonale) or pneumothorax.

Not true emphysema: compensatory hyperinflation (after lobectomy), obstructive overinflation (ball-valve tumour or foreign body), bullous emphysema (bullae > 1 cm), interstitial (mediastinal) emphysema (air in stroma after an alveolar tear).

9.3 Chronic bronchitis

Chronic bronchitis is defined clinically: a persistent productive cough for at least 3 consecutive months in at least 2 consecutive years, in the absence of another identifiable cause.

The defining abnormality is mucus hypersecretion, starting in large airways. Tobacco smoke, polluted air and dusts (grain, cotton, silica) cause hypertrophy of submucosal mucous glands and more goblet cells in small airways. Inflammation is by macrophages, neutrophils and CD8 T cells, not eosinophils (unlike asthma). Infection maintains it and triggers exacerbations.

  • Mucosa of large airways hyperaemic, oedematous, coated with mucopurulent secretions
  • Small bronchi and bronchioles filled with similar secretions
  • Enlarged mucous glands: Reid index (gland layer ÷ wall between epithelium and cartilage) above the normal 0.4
  • Goblet cell metaplasia, mucus plugs, bronchiolar inflammation and fibrosis (small airway disease)
  • Squamous metaplasia and dysplasia of bronchial epithelium
Clinical: years of cough and sputum, then dyspnoea, hypoxaemia, hypercapnia, cyanosis, recurrent infections and cor pulmonale.

9.4 Asthma

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways causing recurrent episodes of wheezing, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough, particularly at night or early morning. Its hallmarks are intermittent, reversible airway obstruction, chronic bronchial inflammation with eosinophils, smooth muscle hypertrophy and hyperreactivity, and excess mucus. Status asthmaticus is a severe attack unresponsive to therapy that can be fatal.

  • No alveolar wall destruction (unlike emphysema)
Clinical: attacks of wheeze, dyspnoea and cough with prolonged expiration lasting hours, near-normal between attacks; eosinophilia; raised IgE (atopic); obstruction reversible with bronchodilator.

9.5 Bronchiectasis

Bronchiectasis is permanent dilatation of bronchi and bronchioles caused by destruction of their muscle and elastic supporting tissue, resulting from or associated with chronic necrotising infection. It is not a primary disease but the end result of persistent infection or obstruction.

Causes

  • Hereditary: cystic fibrosis, primary ciliary dyskinesia (Kartagener), immunodeficiency, sequestration
  • Post-infectious: necrotising pneumonia (TB, S. aureus, H. influenzae, Pseudomonas), adenovirus, influenza, HIV, Aspergillus (ABPA in asthmatics)
  • Obstruction: tumour, foreign body, mucus impaction (one segment)
  • Other: RA, SLE, IBD, lung transplant rejection; many idiopathic

Kartagener syndrome: defective ciliary dynein arms (autosomal recessive) → situs inversus, chronic sinusitis, bronchiectasis; men infertile (immotile sperm).

  • Usually lower lobes, bilateral, most vertical airways; localised to one segment if due to tumour or foreign body
  • Bronchi dilated up to four times normal; can be followed out to the pleura (normally impossible)
  • Cylindrical, fusiform or saccular; cut surface: cyst-like spaces full of mucopus
  • Active disease: intense acute and chronic inflammation of bronchial walls; ulceration of mucosa
  • Remaining epithelium pseudostratified or squamous metaplastic
  • Destruction of muscle and elastic tissue; necrosis may form an abscess
  • Chronic: peribronchial fibrosis, obliteration of bronchioles
Clinical: persistent cough with copious, foul, mucopurulent sputum, haemoptysis, clubbing, flares with infections. Complications: cor pulmonale, brain abscess, AA amyloidosis.

9.6 Interstitial lung disease: IPF and the pneumoconioses

Chronic interstitial lung diseases scar the alveolar walls, not the airways. The lung becomes stiff (more work to breathe) and thick walls block gas exchange (early hypoxaemia): restrictive spirometry, low DLCO, nodular or reticular shadows, and finally a honeycomb lung with cor pulmonale. Groups: fibrosing (IPF, NSIP, organising pneumonia, connective tissue disease, pneumoconioses, bleomycin, amiodarone, radiation), granulomatous (sarcoidosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis), eosinophilic, smoking-related and others.

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)

A progressive, patchy, bilateral interstitial fibrosis of unknown cause, in older adults (more often men and smokers). Its histological and radiological pattern is usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP); UIP is required for the diagnosis but can also be seen in asbestosis, connective tissue disease and chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis.

IPF is failed repair, not runaway inflammation. Repeated injury to alveolar epithelium (smoking, reflux, environment) in a susceptible lung is repaired badly: injured cells release TGF-β, driving fibroblastic foci that lay down collagen. Susceptibility: short telomeres (TERT, TERC) and a MUC5B promoter variant. Hence steroids fail; antifibrotics (pirfenidone, nintedanib) only slow it.

  • Pleura cobblestoned by retracting scars along septa
  • Firm, rubbery white fibrosis, worst in the lower lobes, subpleural regions and along interlobular septa
  • Cystic honeycombing late
  • Patchy interstitial fibrosis alternating with near-normal lung
  • Fibroblastic foci (early) next to dense collagen (old): temporal heterogeneity, the hallmark of UIP
  • Honeycomb fibrosis: cystic spaces lined by bronchiolar epithelium or type II pneumocytes
  • Mild interstitial inflammation; pulmonary hypertensive vascular changes
Clinical: exertional dyspnoea, dry cough, “velcro” crackles, clubbing; mean survival about 3 years or less; transplant is the only cure. NSIP: uniform (same-age) fibrosis, better prognosis.

Pneumoconioses

Lung reactions to inhaled mineral dusts at work. Harm depends on particle size (1–5 µm particles lodge at distal airway bifurcations), shape, solubility and reactivity. Macrophages engulf the dust; silica and asbestos activate the NLRP3 inflammasome (IL-1) and growth factors (TGF-β, PDGF) drive fibrosis. Smoking worsens every mineral dust, asbestos most.

Why amphiboles are worse. Curly serpentine chrysotile (most used) is cleared more easily; straight, stiff amphiboles (crocidolite, amosite) align with airflow, penetrate deep to the pleura and persist, so they cause most mesotheliomas. Asbestos is both tumour initiator and promoter, and carries tobacco carcinogens on its surface.

Silicosis is a documented hazard of stone-crushing workers in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Punjab; screen them for silicotuberculosis. About 19% of adults use tobacco (men about 32%), and biomass fuel smoke in rural kitchens causes COPD in women who never smoked.

9.7 Granulomatous ILD and cor pulmonale

Sarcoidosis

A multisystem disease of unknown cause with non-caseating granulomas, usually presenting as bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy or lung shadows on X-ray, before age 40; commoner in non-smokers. A diagnosis of exclusion (TB, fungi, berylliosis). Pathogenesis: an exaggerated CD4+ Th1 response (IL-2, IFN-γ) to an unknown antigen in a predisposed host (HLA); lavage shows a high CD4:CD8 ratio, yet skin tests show anergy; polyclonal hypergammaglobulinaemia.

  • Lungs (about 90%): interstitial granulomas along bronchovascular bundles, septa, pleura; 5–15% fibrose
  • Hilar/paratracheal nodes (75–90%): firm, rubbery, non-matted
  • Skin (about a quarter): erythema nodosum, nodules
  • Eyes: uveitis; lacrimal and salivary glands (Mikulicz syndrome); spleen, liver, heart
  • Well-formed, tightly packed non-necrotising epithelioid granulomas with Langhans or foreign-body giant cells
  • Thin rim of lymphocytes; later concentric fibrosis and hyaline scars
  • Schaumann bodies (laminated calcium–protein concretions) and asteroid bodies (stellate inclusions in giant cells): typical but not specific
Labs and course: hypercalcaemia (macrophages make calcitriol), raised serum ACE. About 65–70% recover; about 20% keep lung or visual damage; 10–15% die of pulmonary fibrosis and cor pulmonale.

Sarcoidosis vs tuberculosis. Sarcoid: non-caseating, tight “naked” granulomas, no AFB, discrete nodes, tuberculin negative. TB: caseation, matted nodes, sinuses, AFB on ZN, tuberculin positive. In Pakistan always exclude TB before calling a granuloma “sarcoid”.

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (extrinsic allergic alveolitis)

An immunologically mediated, mainly interstitial disease caused by intense or prolonged inhalation of organic antigens. Unlike asthma, the damage is alveolar (restrictive). Examples: farmer’s lung (actinomycetes in mouldy hay), pigeon breeder’s lung, humidifier lung, bagassosis (sugarcane). Immune basis: precipitating antibodies and complement in vessel walls (type III) plus granulomas (type IV).

Micro: patchy bronchiolocentric interstitial lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages; loose non-necrotising granulomas in about two-thirds. Acute attacks (fever, cough, dyspnoea 4–6 h after exposure) resolve if exposure stops; chronic disease leaves irreversible upper-lobe fibrosis.

Cor pulmonale

Cor pulmonale is right ventricular hypertrophy and dilatation, often with failure, caused by pulmonary hypertension from disease of the lung, pulmonary vessels or chest wall (left heart and congenital disease excluded).

!COPD, ILD, chest wall disease, sleep apnoea -> Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction -> *Pulmonary hypertension Capillary bed lost (emphysema, fibrosis) -> *Pulmonary hypertension *Pulmonary hypertension -> RV pressure overload -> RV hypertrophy (wall > 0.5 cm) -> !Right heart failure

Acute: massive pulmonary embolism (RV dilates, no hypertrophy). Chronic: RV wall thickens from 0.3–0.5 cm to 1.0 cm or more; pulmonary arteries show medial hypertrophy and intimal fibrosis. COPD is the commonest cause; raised JVP, hepatomegaly, pitting oedema.

  3. Centriacinar: smokers, upper lobes; panacinar: α1-AT, lower lobes.
  4. Paraseptal emphysema: bullae, spontaneous pneumothorax.
  5. COPD = proteases + oxidants beat antiproteases.
  6. Chronic bronchitis: cough 3 months × 2 years; Reid index > 0.4.
  7. Pink puffer = emphysema; blue bloater = chronic bronchitis.
  8. Atopic asthma: Th2, IgE, eosinophils; early and late phases.
  9. Asthma morphology: mucus plugs, Curschmann, Charcot–Leyden, thick BM.
  10. Bronchiectasis: obstruction + infection; bronchi traced to pleura.
  11. Kartagener: dynein defect, situs inversus, sinusitis, bronchiectasis.
  12. IPF = UIP: patchy fibrosis, fibroblastic foci, honeycombing.
  13. Silica: upper zone, whorled nodules, eggshell nodes, TB risk.
  14. Asbestos: lower lobes, ferruginous bodies, plaques, mesothelioma.
  15. Sarcoid: non-caseating granulomas, BHL, high calcium, anergy.
