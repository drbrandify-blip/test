9.1 Obstructive vs restrictive lung disease

Obstructive diseases raise resistance to airflow anywhere from trachea to respiratory bronchiole, so air is trapped and expiration is slow. Restrictive diseases reduce expansion of the lung, either because the parenchyma is stiff (interstitial fibrosis) or because the chest wall cannot move; lung volumes shrink but the air that is inhaled comes out fast.

9.2 Emphysema

COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) is the umbrella term for persistent, poorly reversible airflow limitation caused mainly by smoking; its two components, emphysema and chronic bronchitis, usually coexist in the same patient. Only a minority of smokers develop clinically significant COPD, so genes and other exposures matter.

Emphysema is irreversible enlargement of the airspaces distal to the terminal bronchiole, accompanied by destruction of their walls without significant fibrosis. It is a morphological definition; chronic bronchitis is a clinical one.

  • Centriacinar (centrilobular): the respiratory bronchioles (central part of the acinus) are enlarged; distal alveoli are spared. Upper lobes, especially apical segments; walls often contain black pigment. Seen in heavy smokers, often with chronic bronchitis; more than 95% of clinically significant emphysema is of this type.
  • Panacinar (panlobular): the acinus is enlarged uniformly from respiratory bronchiole to terminal alveoli. Lower zones and anterior margins, worst at the bases; associated with α1-antitrypsin deficiency.
  • Distal acinar (paraseptal): distal acinus involved, next to the pleura and interlobular septa, in the upper half of the lung, often near scars. Confluent spaces form bullae and underlie many cases of spontaneous pneumothorax in young adults.
  • Airspace enlargement with fibrosis (irregular): around scars from old inflammation; common at autopsy but clinically insignificant.

Pathogenesis

  • Inflammation: smoke recruits neutrophils, macrophages and CD4/CD8 T cells; mediators include LTB4, IL-8 and TNF.
  • Protease–antiprotease imbalance: neutrophil elastase and macrophage matrix metalloproteinases digest elastic tissue faster than antiproteases can neutralise them.
  • Oxidant–antioxidant imbalance: smoke is loaded with free radicals that injure cells and inactivate α1-antitrypsin (a “functional” deficiency).
  • Infection does not start the disease but drives exacerbations.

α1-antitrypsin (gene SERPINA1, chromosome 14) is the main serum inhibitor of neutrophil elastase. PiZZ homozygotes have very low levels and develop panacinar emphysema early, much earlier if they smoke, plus liver disease (PAS-positive globules in hepatocytes).

Why emphysema obstructs although the airways are open. Small airways have no cartilage; they are held open by the elastic pull of the alveolar walls attached to them, like guy-ropes on a tent. Destroy those walls and two things happen: the lung loses elastic recoil (less force to push air out) and the bronchioles collapse during expiration. Air is trapped behind them, so the lungs hyperinflate and the patient breathes out through pursed lips to splint the airways open.

  • Advanced panacinar: pale, voluminous lungs that cover the heart when the chest is opened
  • Centriacinar: changes worse in the upper two-thirds
  • Bullae (large subpleural blebs) in distal acinar and irregular types
  • Thinned, destroyed alveolar walls; adjacent alveoli fuse into large airspaces
  • Free-floating fragments of septa; reduced capillary bed
  • Bronchioles deformed and narrowed; bronchiolar inflammation and submucosal fibrosis
Clinical: dyspnoea is the first symptom, insidious and steadily progressive; cough and wheeze; weight loss; barrel chest; sits hunched forward, breathing through pursed lips. Death from respiratory acidosis and coma, right heart failure (cor pulmonale) or pneumothorax.

Conditions called “emphysema” that are not true emphysema: compensatory hyperinflation (remaining lung after lobectomy), obstructive overinflation (air trapped behind a ball-valve tumour or foreign body), bullous emphysema (any type with bullae > 1 cm) and interstitial (mediastinal) emphysema (air in the lung stroma, mediastinum and subcutaneous tissue after an alveolar tear from ventilation, trauma or whooping cough).

9.3 Chronic bronchitis

Chronic bronchitis is defined clinically: a persistent productive cough for at least 3 consecutive months in at least 2 consecutive years, in the absence of another identifiable cause.

The defining abnormality is hypersecretion of mucus, beginning in the large airways. Long-standing irritation by tobacco smoke (most patients smoke), polluted city air and dusts (grain, cotton, silica) causes hypertrophy of submucosal mucous glands in the trachea and bronchi and an increase of goblet cells in small airways. Inflammation is by macrophages, neutrophils and CD8 T cells; unlike asthma, eosinophils are not prominent. Infection does not cause it but maintains it and triggers exacerbations.

  • Mucosa of large airways hyperaemic and oedematous, coated with mucinous or mucopurulent secretions
  • Small bronchi filled with similar secretions
  • Enlarged mucous glands: raised Reid index
  • Goblet cell metaplasia, mucus plugging, inflammation and fibrosis of bronchioles (small airway disease); bronchiolitis obliterans in severe cases
  • Squamous metaplasia and dysplasia of bronchial epithelium
Reid index = thickness of mucous gland layer ÷ thickness of wall between epithelium and cartilage
Normal about 0.4; raised in chronic bronchitis in proportion to severity

Clinical: cough and sputum for years; later exertional dyspnoea, hypoxaemia, hypercapnia and cyanosis; recurrent infections; cor pulmonale with peripheral oedema; respiratory failure.

COPD beyond cigarettes. About 19% of Pakistani adults use tobacco in some form (men about 32%, women about 6%), and smoking remains the main cause of COPD. 9.4 Asthma

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways causing recurrent episodes of wheezing, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough, particularly at night or early morning. Its hallmarks are intermittent, reversible airway obstruction, chronic bronchial inflammation with eosinophils, smooth muscle hypertrophy and hyperreactivity, and excess mucus. Status asthmaticus is a severe attack unresponsive to therapy that can be fatal.

9.5 Bronchiectasis

Bronchiectasis is permanent dilatation of bronchi and bronchioles caused by destruction of their muscle and elastic supporting tissue, resulting from or associated with chronic necrotising infection. It is not a primary disease but the end result of persistent infection or obstruction.

Causes

  • Congenital / hereditary: cystic fibrosis (thick mucus), primary ciliary dyskinesia and Kartagener syndrome, immunodeficiency, intralobar sequestration
  • Post-infectious: necrotising pneumonia from M. tuberculosis, S. aureus, H. influenzae, Pseudomonas; viruses (adenovirus, influenza, HIV); fungi (Aspergillus, including allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis in asthmatics)
  • Bronchial obstruction: tumour, foreign body, mucus impaction (localised to one segment)
  • Other: rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, IBD, chronic rejection after lung transplant; many are idiopathic

Kartagener syndrome = primary ciliary dyskinesia (defective dynein arms; autosomal recessive) with the triad situs inversus, chronic sinusitis, bronchiectasis. Men are infertile because sperm tails do not beat.

  • Usually lower lobes, bilateral, most vertical airways; localised to one segment if due to tumour or foreign body
  • Bronchi dilated up to four times normal; can be followed out to the pleura (normally impossible)
  • Cylindrical, fusiform or saccular; cut surface: cyst-like spaces full of mucopus
  • Active disease: intense acute and chronic inflammation of bronchial walls; ulceration of mucosa
  • Remaining epithelium pseudostratified or squamous metaplastic
  • Destruction of muscle and elastic tissue; necrosis may form an abscess
  • Chronic: peribronchial fibrosis, obliteration of bronchioles
Clinical: severe persistent cough with copious, foul-smelling, mucopurulent sputum (layers on standing), haemoptysis, dyspnoea, rhinosinusitis, clubbing; flares with respiratory infections. Complications: hypoxaemia, pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale, brain abscess, secondary (AA) amyloidosis.

9.6 Interstitial lung disease: IPF and the pneumoconioses

Chronic interstitial lung diseases inflame and scar the alveolar walls rather than the airways. Fibrosis makes the lung stiff (low compliance), so breathing takes more work, and thickened walls block gas exchange, so hypoxaemia develops early. Spirometry is restrictive with a low DLCO. The X-ray shows small nodules, irregular lines or ground-glass shadows; the end stage is a honeycomb lung with secondary pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale.

Robbins groups them as: fibrosing (IPF, non-specific interstitial pneumonia, cryptogenic organising pneumonia with Masson bodies, connective tissue disease, pneumoconioses, drugs such as bleomycin and amiodarone, radiation) · granulomatous (sarcoidosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis) · eosinophilic · smoking-related (desquamative interstitial pneumonia, respiratory bronchiolitis) · others (Langerhans cell histiocytosis, alveolar proteinosis).

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)

A progressive, patchy, bilateral interstitial fibrosis of unknown cause, in older adults (more often men and smokers). Its histological and radiological pattern is usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP); UIP is required for the diagnosis but can also be seen in asbestosis, connective tissue disease and chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis.

IPF is failed repair, not runaway inflammation. Repeated small injuries to alveolar epithelium (smoking, reflux, environmental exposures) in a genetically susceptible lung are repaired badly. Injured type I cells release TGF-β, which drives fibroblasts into fibroblastic foci that lay down collagen. Predisposition comes from short telomeres (TERT, TERC mutations) and a common MUC5B promoter variant. That is why steroids do not help and antifibrotic drugs (pirfenidone, nintedanib) only slow it.

  • Pleura cobblestoned by retracting scars along septa
  • Firm, rubbery white fibrosis, worst in the lower lobes, subpleural regions and along interlobular septa
  • Cystic honeycombing late
  • Patchy interstitial fibrosis alternating with near-normal lung
  • Fibroblastic foci (early) next to dense collagen (old): temporal heterogeneity, the hallmark of UIP
  • Honeycomb fibrosis: cystic spaces lined by bronchiolar epithelium or type II pneumocytes
  • Mild interstitial inflammation; pulmonary hypertensive vascular changes
Clinical: insidious exertional dyspnoea and dry cough; inspiratory “velcro” crackles, clubbing, cyanosis. Progressive; mean survival about 3 years or less; lung transplantation is the only cure. Non-specific interstitial pneumonia differs by uniform (temporally homogeneous) inflammation/fibrosis and a better prognosis.

Pneumoconioses

Lung reactions to inhaled mineral dusts (and other particulates) at work. The danger depends on particle size (1–5 µm particles reach and lodge at distal airway bifurcations), shape, solubility and reactivity. Alveolar macrophages engulf the particles; fibrogenic dusts (silica, asbestos) activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, releasing IL-1 and growth factors (TGF-β, PDGF) that drive fibroblasts. Smoking worsens the effect of every mineral dust, asbestos most of all.

Why amphiboles are worse. Asbestos comes as curly, flexible serpentine fibres (chrysotile, most used commercially) and straight, stiff amphiboles (crocidolite, amosite). Straight fibres align with airflow and reach deep into the lung and pleura, and they are less soluble, so amphiboles are far more likely to cause mesothelioma. Asbestos also acts as a tumour initiator and promoter, and carries carcinogens from tobacco smoke on its surface.

Silicosis is a documented occupational hazard among stone-crushing workers in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Punjab, where dry crushing without masks or water spraying exposes workers to fresh crystalline silica. A Pakistani stone crusher with upper-zone nodules and new cough should be screened for silicotuberculosis.

9.7 Granulomatous ILD and cor pulmonale

Sarcoidosis

A multisystem disease of unknown cause with non-caseating granulomas in many organs. Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy or lung involvement on chest X-ray is the commonest presentation; eyes and skin come next. It is a diagnosis of exclusion (TB, fungi and berylliosis also make granulomas). It usually begins before 40 and is one of the few diseases commoner in non-smokers.

Pathogenesis: an exaggerated CD4+ Th1 response to an unidentified antigen in a genetically predisposed host (HLA associations). Bronchoalveolar lavage shows a high CD4:CD8 ratio; Th1 cytokines (IL-2, IFN-γ) drive granulomas. Paradoxically, patients show anergy to skin-test antigens (tuberculin, Candida) and polyclonal hypergammaglobulinaemia.

  • Lungs (about 90%): granulomas in the interstitium along bronchovascular bundles, septa and pleura; 5–15% progress to fibrosis and honeycombing
  • Hilar and paratracheal nodes (75–90%): enlarged, firm, rubbery, non-matted, never ulcerate
  • Skin (about a quarter): erythema nodosum, subcutaneous nodules
  • Eyes: uveitis (iritis, iridocyclitis); lacrimal and salivary glands (Mikulicz syndrome); also spleen, liver, marrow, heart, CNS
  • Well-formed, tightly packed non-necrotising epithelioid granulomas with Langhans or foreign-body giant cells
  • Thin rim of lymphocytes; later concentric fibrosis and hyaline scars
  • Schaumann bodies (laminated calcium–protein concretions) and asteroid bodies (stellate inclusions in giant cells): typical but not specific
Labs and course: hypercalcaemia and hypercalciuria (granuloma macrophages make active vitamin D, calcitriol), raised serum ACE. About 65–70% recover with little or no residue; about 20% keep permanent lung or visual damage; 10–15% die, mostly from progressive pulmonary fibrosis and cor pulmonale.

Sarcoidosis vs tuberculosis. Sarcoid granulomas are non-caseating, tight and “naked” (few lymphocytes), with no acid-fast bacilli; nodes are discrete, non-matted; the tuberculin test is usually negative (anergy). TB granulomas caseate, nodes become matted and may form sinuses, ZN stain may show bacilli, and the tuberculin test is positive. In Pakistan always exclude TB before calling a granuloma “sarcoid”.

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (extrinsic allergic alveolitis)

An immunologically mediated, mainly interstitial disease caused by intense or prolonged inhalation of organic antigens. Unlike asthma, the damage is in the alveoli, so it is restrictive. Examples: farmer’s lung (thermophilic actinomycetes in mouldy hay), pigeon breeder’s (bird fancier’s) lung (avian serum, droppings, feathers), humidifier/air-conditioner lung, bagassosis (sugarcane bagasse). Evidence of immune injury: serum precipitating antibodies, immunoglobulin and complement in vessel walls (type III), and granulomas (type IV).

  • Micro: patchy bronchiolocentric interstitial infiltrate of lymphocytes, plasma cells and macrophages
  • Loose, poorly formed non-necrotising granulomas in about two-thirds
  • Acute: fever, cough, dyspnoea 4–6 hours after exposure; resolves if exposure stops
  • Chronic: upper-lobe fibrosis; irreversible. Removing the antigen is the key treatment

Cor pulmonale

Cor pulmonale is right ventricular hypertrophy and dilatation, often with right heart failure, caused by pulmonary hypertension due to disease of the lung parenchyma, pulmonary vessels or chest wall. Right heart changes secondary to left heart or congenital heart disease are excluded.

!COPD, ILD, chest wall disease, sleep apnoea -> Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction -> *Pulmonary hypertension Capillary bed lost (emphysema, fibrosis) -> *Pulmonary hypertension *Pulmonary hypertension -> RV pressure overload -> RV hypertrophy (wall > 0.5 cm) -> !Right heart failure

Acute cor pulmonale follows massive pulmonary embolism (RV dilatation without hypertrophy). Chronic cor pulmonale: RV wall thickens from the normal 0.3–0.5 cm to 1.0 cm or more; pulmonary arteries show medial hypertrophy, intimal fibrosis and atheroma. COPD is the commonest cause; clinically raised JVP, hepatomegaly and pitting oedema.

Answer

RAPID REVISION The Night-Before Page

15 one-liners

  1. Obstructive: FEV1/FVC < 0.7; restrictive: TLC low, ratio normal or high.
  2. Emphysema: airspaces beyond terminal bronchiole enlarged, walls destroyed.
  3. Centriacinar: smokers, upper lobes; panacinar: α1-AT, lower lobes.
  4. Paraseptal emphysema: bullae, spontaneous pneumothorax.
  5. COPD = proteases + oxidants beat antiproteases.
  6. Chronic bronchitis: cough 3 months × 2 years; Reid index > 0.4.
  7. Pink puffer = emphysema; blue bloater = chronic bronchitis.
  8. Atopic asthma: Th2, IgE, eosinophils; early and late phases.
  9. Asthma morphology: mucus plugs, Curschmann, Charcot–Leyden, thick BM.
  10. Bronchiectasis: obstruction + infection; bronchi traced to pleura.
  11. Kartagener: dynein defect, situs inversus, sinusitis, bronchiectasis.
  12. IPF = UIP: patchy fibrosis, fibroblastic foci, honeycombing.
  13. Silica: upper zone, whorled nodules, eggshell nodes, TB risk.
  14. Asbestos: lower lobes, ferruginous bodies, plaques, mesothelioma.
  15. Sarcoid: non-caseating granulomas, BHL, high calcium, anergy.
