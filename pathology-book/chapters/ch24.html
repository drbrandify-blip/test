24.1 Pituitary gland

The anterior pituitary has five cell types: somatotrophs (GH), lactotrophs (prolactin, held in check by hypothalamic dopamine), corticotrophs (ACTH, MSH), thyrotrophs (TSH) and gonadotrophs (FSH, LH). The posterior lobe stores hypothalamic ADH and oxytocin. Disease shows as hormone excess (usually an adenoma), deficit, or mass effects: bitemporal hemianopia (optic chiasm), raised intracranial pressure and pituitary apoplexy (sudden haemorrhage into an adenoma, an emergency).

Pituitary adenomas

Adenomas are classified by the hormone they make (immunohistochemistry and lineage factors PIT1, TPIT, SF1), as functioning or non-functioning, and by size: microadenoma < 1 cm, macroadenoma > 1 cm. Peak age 35–60 years. Genes: GNAS activation (constitutive Gsα → cAMP) in about 40% of somatotroph adenomas; germline MEN1, AIP, CDKN1B, PRKAR1A in familial cases.

  • Soft, well-circumscribed; small ones confined to the sella
  • Large ones erode the sella and grow upwards through the diaphragma sellae onto the optic chiasm; some invade sinuses
  • Sheets or cords of uniform, monomorphic polygonal cells
  • Loss of the reticulin network (the normal gland has mixed cells in acini)
  • Pleomorphism does not predict behaviour; carcinoma = metastasis

Not every high prolactin is a prolactinoma. Pregnancy, dopamine-blocking drugs (antipsychotics, metoclopramide), primary hypothyroidism (TRH stimulates lactotrophs) and the stalk effect (any mass compressing the stalk cuts off dopamine) all raise prolactin modestly.

Hypopituitarism

Deficit appears when about 75% of the anterior pituitary is lost. Causes: non-functioning adenomas and other sellar masses (craniopharyngioma), head injury, surgery or radiation, pituitary apoplexy, ischaemic necrosis (Sheehan syndrome), empty sella syndrome, hypothalamic lesions and infiltrations (sarcoidosis, TB).

In pregnancy the anterior pituitary nearly doubles in size (lactotroph hyperplasia), but its low-pressure portal venous supply does not grow with it. A massive postpartum haemorrhage with shock drops perfusion below what the swollen gland needs, and the anterior lobe infarcts. The posterior lobe, fed directly by arteries, is spared. The first clue is failure of lactation, followed by amenorrhoea, loss of pubic and axillary hair, hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency. The infarct heals into a shrunken fibrous nubbin.

Effects: growth failure in children (GH); amenorrhoea, infertility, loss of body hair (gonadotrophins); hypothyroidism (TSH); hypoadrenalism without pigmentation (ACTH); failed lactation (prolactin).

24.2 Adrenal cortex

Three zones, three hormone families, from outside in: glomerulosa (aldosterone, driven by renin–angiotensin), fasciculata (cortisol) and reticularis (androgens), both driven by ACTH. Remember “GFR → salt, sugar, sex”: the deeper you go, the sweeter it gets.

A cortisol-secreting adenoma is a yellow, encapsulated nodule (usually < 30 g); a carcinoma is large, with haemorrhage, necrosis and invasion. In the pituitary, high cortisol from any cause produces Crooke hyaline change: the granular cytoplasm of normal corticotrophs is replaced by homogeneous pale keratin filaments.

Clinical features

  • Central obesity, moon face, buffalo hump; hypertension
  • Proximal myopathy; osteoporosis; thin skin, bruising, purple striae
  • Hyperglycaemia; infections; hirsutism, menstrual upset; depression, psychosis
  • Ectopic ACTH: pigmentation, hypokalaemic alkalosis

Hyperaldosteronism

Primary (low renin): autonomous aldosterone. Causes: bilateral idiopathic hyperplasia (about 60%), aldosterone-producing adenoma = Conn syndrome (about 35%; often KCNJ5 potassium-channel mutations), familial glucocorticoid-remediable form, rarely carcinoma. It may be the commonest cause of secondary hypertension.

Secondary (high renin): renal artery stenosis, heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, pregnancy.

  • Solitary, small (< 2 cm), well circumscribed, bright yellow
  • Adjacent cortex not atrophic (ACTH unaffected)
  • Lipid-laden cortical cells like fasciculata
  • Spironolactone bodies: laminated eosinophilic inclusions after spironolactone

Clinical: hypertension, hypokalaemia (weakness, paraesthesias, arrhythmias), metabolic alkalosis; sodium is retained but oedema does not develop. Screening test: raised aldosterone-to-renin ratio.

Adrenogenital syndromes: congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH)

Androgen excess may come from a virilising cortical tumour or from CAH: autosomal recessive defects of cortisol synthesis. 21-hydroxylase (CYP21A2) deficiency causes more than 90% of cases.

!CYP21A2 mutation -> Cortisol and aldosterone low -> ACTH high (no feedback) -> Bilateral adrenal hyperplasia -> Precursors diverted to androgens -> *Virilisation Cortisol and aldosterone low -> *Salt wasting: low Na, high K
  • Salt-wasting (complete defect): neonate with vomiting, dehydration, low Na, high K, shock; ambiguous genitalia in girls.
  • Simple virilising (partial; aldosterone adequate): genital ambiguity in girls, precocious puberty in boys.
  • Non-classic (mild, late onset): hirsutism, acne, irregular menses; mimics polycystic ovary.

Morphology: both adrenals hyperplastic (up to 10–15 times normal), cortex thick and brown (lipid depleted). Labs: raised 17-hydroxyprogesterone. Glucocorticoids suppress ACTH. 11β-hydroxylase deficiency virilises but causes hypertension (deoxycorticosterone).

Adrenal insufficiency

Acute: Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome

Overwhelming bacterial sepsis, classically Neisseria meningitidis (also Pseudomonas, pneumococci, H. influenzae), mostly in children: endotoxin vascular injury and DIC → shock, purpura and massive bilateral adrenal haemorrhage; the glands become sacs of clotted blood. Other acute causes: stress in chronic insufficiency, abrupt steroid withdrawal.

Chronic primary: Addison disease

Appears when about 90% of the cortex is destroyed. Causes: autoimmune adrenalitis (60–70% in developed countries; alone or in APS-1 or APS-2), tuberculosis and fungi (histoplasmosis), AIDS (CMV, M. avium), metastatic carcinoma (lung, breast), amyloidosis. Morphology: autoimmune glands are small and shrunken, cortex replaced by lymphocytes, medulla spared; TB gives caseating granulomas.

24.3 Adrenal medulla and MEN syndromes

Phaeochromocytoma

A tumour of chromaffin cells that secretes catecholamines: an uncommon but surgically curable cause of hypertension. Paroxysms bring headache, palpitations and sweating with tremor and anxiety; catecholamine excess may cause cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, infarction and stroke. Labs: raised urinary and plasma metanephrines and urinary VMA. Remove after α-blockade.

  • Small and circumscribed to large and haemorrhagic
  • Cut surface yellow-tan with haemorrhage, necrosis, cysts
  • Potassium dichromate turns it dark brown (chromaffin reaction)
  • Polygonal to spindle chief cells in nests (Zellballen) wrapped by sustentacular cells and a rich capillary network
  • Finely granular cytoplasm; salt-and-pepper chromatin; dense-core granules on EM
  • Chromogranin, synaptophysin positive; S-100 in sustentacular cells
  • Malignancy is defined only by metastasis

The traditional “rule of 10s”

10% extra-adrenal (paragangliomas, e.g. organ of Zuckerkandl, carotid body) · 10% bilateral · 10% malignant · 10% without hypertension · 10% familial. The last figure is outdated: about 25% carry germline mutations (RET, NF1, VHL, SDH genes), and familial tumours are often bilateral.

Multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN)

Autosomal dominant syndromes of endocrine tumours that are younger, multifocal and preceded by hyperplasia.

24.4 Diabetes mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a group of metabolic disorders sharing hyperglycaemia caused by defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. Chronic hyperglycaemia damages blood vessels, kidneys, eyes and nerves; diabetes is a leading cause of end-stage renal disease, adult-onset blindness and non-traumatic leg amputation.

Fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L)
Random glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) with classic symptoms
2-hour OGTT (75 g) ≥ 200 mg/dL
HbA1c ≥ 6.5%
Prediabetes FPG 100–125 (IFG); 2-h 140–199 (IGT); HbA1c 5.7–6.4%
Normal FPG < 100 mg/dL. Without clear hyperglycaemia, repeat on another day

Classification: type 1 (5–10%; autoimmune β-cell destruction, absolute insulin deficiency); type 2 (90–95%; insulin resistance with relative deficiency); monogenic (MODY: GCK, HNF1A; mitochondrial); gestational; and secondary: exocrine pancreatic disease (chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, haemochromatosis), endocrinopathies (Cushing, acromegaly, phaeochromocytoma, glucagonoma), drugs (glucocorticoids, thiazides), infections (congenital rubella, CMV) and genetic syndromes (Down, Turner, Klinefelter).

How fat blocks insulin. In visceral obesity, free fatty acids flood muscle and liver; their products (diacylglycerol, ceramide) activate kinases that put serine phosphates on the insulin receptor substrate, muting the signal. Fat also makes less adiponectin (an insulin-sensitiser), and FFAs activate the inflammasome (IL-1β). β cells compensate for years; diabetes appears when they fail through lipotoxicity, glucotoxicity, islet amyloid (amylin) and a weak incretin (GLP-1) effect.

Diabetic ketoacidosis (type 1): no insulin + excess glucagon → lipolysis → free fatty acids → hepatic ketone bodies (acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate) → high anion gap metabolic acidosis. Glucose 250–600 mg/dL; osmotic diuresis, dehydration, vomiting, Kussmaul breathing, fruity breath; triggered by infection or missed insulin.

Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (type 2): elderly patient who cannot drink enough; glucose often > 600 mg/dL, severe dehydration, confusion, but no ketoacidosis (residual insulin blocks lipolysis). Hypoglycaemia is the commonest acute complication of treatment.

Chronic complications: how sugar damages vessels

Nerves, lens, kidney and vessel walls take up glucose without insulin, so their cells fill with glucose whenever the blood does; three pathways turn it into vascular damage.

!Persistent hyperglycaemia -> AGEs: nonenzymatic glycation -> RAGE: cytokines, ROS; matrix cross-linking -> *Micro- and macrovascular damage !Persistent hyperglycaemia -> PKC activation via DAG -> VEGF, TGF-β, PAI-1 -> *Micro- and macrovascular damage !Persistent hyperglycaemia -> Polyol: glucose to sorbitol -> NADPH used up: oxidative stress -> *Micro- and macrovascular damage

AGEs form when glucose binds proteins without an enzyme (HbA1c is an early, measurable example reflecting the past 2–3 months). They act on RAGE (cytokines, procoagulant activity, smooth muscle growth) and cross-link matrix, trapping LDL in arteries and albumin in capillary basement membranes. PKC raises VEGF (retinal new vessels), TGF-β (matrix) and PAI-1. The polyol pathway (aldose reductase) uses up NADPH, so glutathione is not regenerated: oxidative stress in nerve and lens.

Diabetic nephropathy

  • Kidneys normal to small with a finely granular surface (arteriolar nephrosclerosis)
  • Look for papillary necrosis and pyelonephritic scars
  • Glomerular capillary basement membrane thickening (earliest, on EM)
  • Diffuse mesangial sclerosis
  • Nodular glomerulosclerosis (Kimmelstiel–Wilson): ovoid, often laminated, PAS-positive nodules of matrix at the periphery of the glomerulus
  • Hyaline arteriolosclerosis of both afferent and efferent arterioles
  • Pyelonephritis, necrotising papillitis
Course: microalbuminuria (30–300 mg/day) → overt, often nephrotic proteinuria → end-stage renal disease (see Chapter 16).

About one in four Pakistani adults has diabetes: the IDF Diabetes Atlas 2021 estimated 26.7% (about 33 million), the third-largest number of people with diabetes in any country. With hypertension, diabetes is the leading cause of chronic kidney disease in Pakistan.

Source: IDF Diabetes Atlas 2021.
