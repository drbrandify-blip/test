25.1 Arteriosclerosis and hypertensive vascular disease

Arteriosclerosis (“hardening of the arteries”) means thickening and loss of elasticity of arterial walls. It has four patterns:

  1. Arteriolosclerosis — small arteries and arterioles: hyaline or hyperplastic; narrows the lumen and causes downstream ischaemia (kidney).
  2. Mönckeberg medial sclerosis — calcium in the media of muscular arteries, usually after 50 years; visible on X-ray and palpable as pipe-stem vessels, but the lumen is spared, so it is rarely of clinical importance.
  3. Fibromuscular intimal hyperplasia — muscular arteries thickened by smooth muscle and matrix after injury: healed arteritis, transplant arteriopathy, restenosis after angioplasty or stenting.
  4. Atherosclerosis — the commonest and most important (Section 25.2).

Hypertensive vascular disease

Hypertension is a sustained blood pressure above the guideline threshold: ≥ 130/80 mm Hg in current American guidance (older and many local guidelines use ≥ 140/90). About 90–95% is essential (polygenic, with salt, obesity, stress and inactivity acting on renal sodium handling and vascular resistance); the rest is secondary: renal (renal artery stenosis, glomerulonephritis), endocrine (Conn, Cushing, phaeochromocytoma, thyroid disease), coarctation of the aorta, raised intracranial pressure. A small fraction develops malignant hypertension: pressures above about 200/120 mm Hg with renal failure and retinal haemorrhages or papilloedema.

25.2 Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory and healing response of the arterial wall to endothelial injury. It produces intimal lesions called atheromas (atheromatous or fibrofatty plaques) that bulge into the lumen, weaken the underlying media and can rupture, causing sudden thrombosis. It underlies most myocardial infarctions, strokes, abdominal aortic aneurysms and peripheral vascular disease.

Pathogenesis: the response-to-injury hypothesis

!Endothelial injury: lipids, BP, smoke, flow -> Leaky, sticky endothelium -> LDL enters intima, is oxidised -> Monocytes become macrophages -> *Foam cells: fatty streak Leaky, sticky endothelium -> PDGF, FGF from platelets, macrophages Monocytes become macrophages -> PDGF, FGF from platelets, macrophages PDGF, FGF from platelets, macrophages -> SMCs migrate, multiply, make collagen -> *Fibrofatty atheroma with cap *Foam cells: fatty streak -> *Fibrofatty atheroma with cap
  1. Endothelial dysfunction (the first step): turbulent flow at branch points and ostia, hypercholesterolaemia, hypertension, smoking toxins and homocysteine make the endothelium leaky, sticky (VCAM-1) and prothrombotic, with less nitric oxide.
  2. Lipid accumulation: LDL seeps into the intima and is oxidised; oxidised LDL is toxic and chemotactic.
  3. Monocytes adhere, migrate in and become macrophages that swallow oxidised LDL through scavenger receptors: foam cells. T cells (Th1, IFN-γ) keep inflammation going; cholesterol crystals activate the inflammasome (IL-1).
  4. Platelets adhere to the damaged lining.
  5. Growth factors (PDGF, FGF, TGF-α) recruit smooth muscle cells from the media.
  6. Smooth muscle cells multiply and secrete collagen, forming a fibrous cap over a lipid core: the mature atheroma.

Why LDL and inflammation are partners. Lipid alone does not make a plaque; the immune reaction to lipid does. Oxidised LDL is the fuel, macrophages are the fire and smooth muscle cells are the scar that tries to wall it off. Lowering LDL starves the process, which is why statins prevent infarction; CRP rises in the blood because the plaque is an inflamed lesion.

Morphology

Fatty streaks are the earliest lesion: multiple minute, flat, yellow spots that join into streaks 1 cm or more long. They are made of lipid-filled foam cells, are not raised and do not disturb flow. They appear in the aortas of some infants and in virtually all children over 10; not all progress, but coronary fatty streaks of adolescence form at the sites that later develop plaques.

  • Raised white-yellow plaques, 0.3–1.5 cm, patchy and eccentric; may fuse into large masses
  • Red-brown thrombus on ulcerated plaques; gritty calcification
  • Order of involvement: lower abdominal aorta > coronary arteries > popliteal arteries > internal carotids > circle of Willis; worst around branch ostia
  • Fibrous cap: smooth muscle cells in dense collagen
  • Shoulder: macrophages, smooth muscle cells, T cells
  • Necrotic core: lipid, cholesterol clefts, foam cells, debris, fibrin, organised thrombus
  • New vessels at the periphery; thinned media beneath; calcification
Three components: cells (smooth muscle cells, macrophages, T cells), extracellular matrix (collagen, elastic fibres, proteoglycans) and lipid (inside and outside cells). Complicated plaques show rupture or erosion with thrombosis, haemorrhage into the plaque, atheroembolism (cholesterol emboli), aneurysmal dilatation from medial atrophy, and dystrophic calcification.

Consequences

  • Critical stenosis (about 70% narrowing): demand ischaemia, stable angina, chronic ischaemic heart disease, bowel ischaemia, intermittent claudication
  • Acute plaque change (rupture, erosion, haemorrhage) → thrombosis → MI, stroke
  • Embolism of thrombus or cholesterol (atheroemboli)
  • Aneurysm: weakened media, mainly abdominal aorta

The dangerous plaque is often the small one. Most plaques that rupture and cause infarction were not critically stenotic beforehand. Stability depends on the cap and the inflammation, not on size. Adrenergic surges (emotional stress, the early morning hours) raise wall stress and can trigger rupture.

Atheroma in 10 seconds: gross aorta opened longitudinally with raised yellow-white plaques, some ulcerated with red thrombus, worst in the abdominal segment and around ostia. Microscopy: thickened intima with a pink collagen cap over a pale core of empty needle-shaped cholesterol clefts and foamy macrophages; the media beneath is thin.

Ischaemic heart disease is a leading cause of death in Pakistan, and South Asians develop coronary disease at a younger age. The major risk factors are common: about one in three adults has hypertension, about one in four has diabetes, and about a fifth of adults use tobacco (31.8% of men; GATS 2014).

25.3 Aneurysms and aortic dissection

Aneurysm: a localised abnormal dilatation of a blood vessel or the heart. A true aneurysm is bounded by all three layers of the (attenuated) wall: atherosclerotic, syphilitic and berry aneurysms, ventricular aneurysm after infarction. A false aneurysm (pseudoaneurysm) is a breach in the wall with an extravascular haematoma that communicates with the lumen: contained ventricular rupture, leaking graft anastomosis. Shapes: saccular (outpouching) or fusiform (circumferential).

Why walls weaken. (1) Poor-quality connective tissue: Marfan syndrome (defective fibrillin-1, excess TGF-β activity), Loeys–Dietz syndrome (TGF-β receptor mutations), Ehlers–Danlos (collagen), scurvy. (2) Too much breakdown: inflammatory cells release matrix metalloproteinases. (3) Ischaemia of the media: atherosclerotic intima starves the inner media; hypertension narrows the vasa vasorum and starves the outer media (cystic medial degeneration); syphilis obliterates the vasa vasorum. The two great predisposing conditions are atherosclerosis (abdominal aorta) and hypertension (ascending aorta).

AAA rupture risk rises with diameter: rare below 4 cm, about 1% a year at 4–5 cm, about 11% a year at 5–6 cm and about 25% a year above 6 cm, so aneurysms of 5 cm or more are repaired. Most patients die of coronary disease, the companion of the same atherosclerosis.

Aortic dissection

Aortic dissection: blood splits the laminar planes of the media to form a blood-filled channel within the aortic wall, which may rupture outwards with catastrophic haemorrhage. It is not an aneurysm in the strict sense, although the term “dissecting aneurysm” is still heard.

  • Who: men aged 40–60 with hypertension (more than 90% of cases); younger patients with connective tissue disorders (Marfan); also cannulation injury, pregnancy, cocaine. Rare with atherosclerosis or syphilis, because medial scarring blocks propagation.
  • Lesion: cystic medial degeneration: fragmented elastic tissue and pools of proteoglycan-rich matrix in the media, characteristically without inflammation.
  • Morphology: a transverse intimal tear of 1–5 cm with jagged edges, usually in the ascending aorta within 10 cm of the valve; the haematoma runs between the middle and outer thirds of the media. It may rupture out (pericardium, pleura, peritoneum) or re-enter the lumen through a second tear, creating a double-barrelled aorta.
  • Clinical: sudden, excruciating tearing pain in the anterior chest radiating to the back between the scapulae and moving downwards; may mimic MI; pulse and BP differences between arms.
  • Causes of death: rupture into the pericardium (tamponade), pleura or peritoneum; retrograde extension into the root (aortic regurgitation); compression of coronary (MI), carotid (stroke), renal, mesenteric or spinal arteries.

“A for Ascending, A for Acute surgery”

Stanford type A (DeBakey I and II) involves the ascending aorta, is commoner and deadlier, and needs urgent surgery. Type B (DeBakey III) starts beyond the left subclavian artery and is usually managed by lowering blood pressure.

25.4 Vasculitis

Vasculitis is inflammation of vessel walls. Patients have constitutional symptoms (fever, myalgia, arthralgia, malaise) plus the effects of ischaemia in whatever organ the vessels supply. Most vasculitis is immune-mediated; some is caused by direct infection (Neisseria, rickettsiae, Pseudomonas, Aspergillus, Mucor). The disorders are classified by the size of vessel they prefer.

  • Immune complexes: SLE, cryoglobulinaemia (HCV), PAN with hepatitis B, IgA vasculitis, drug hypersensitivity
  • ANCA: autoantibodies to neutrophil granule enzymes (pauci-immune: no deposits)
  • Anti-endothelial cell antibodies: Kawasaki disease
  • Autoreactive T cells: granulomatous GCA, Takayasu

ANCA: PR3-ANCA (cytoplasmic, c-ANCA) → granulomatosis with polyangiitis; MPO-ANCA (perinuclear, p-ANCA) → microscopic polyangiitis, EGPA. Infection or drugs prime neutrophils, which move PR3 or MPO to their surface; ANCA binds and triggers degranulation and reactive oxygen species that kill endothelium. Titres roughly follow disease activity.

25.5 Vascular tumours

Benign vascular tumours form well-formed channels lined by a single layer of normal-looking endothelium and filled with blood; malignant ones are solid, cellular and atypical with poorly formed vessels. Endothelial markers: CD31, CD34, ERG, von Willebrand factor.

Haemangioma

The commonest tumour of infancy and childhood, mostly in the skin of the head and neck; the liver is the commonest internal site.

  • Capillary haemangioma: red-blue, 1 to several cm; lobules of thin-walled capillaries with scant stroma. The juvenile “strawberry” type grows fast for months, fades by 1–3 years and regresses by about 7 years in most children.
  • Cavernous haemangioma: large, dilated, blood-filled channels; poorly circumscribed, deep, does not regress; thrombosis and calcification; in the brain it may bleed.

Pyogenic granuloma and glomus tumour

  • Pyogenic granuloma (lobular capillary haemangioma): rapidly growing, pedunculated red nodule on skin, gingiva or oral mucosa that bleeds easily and ulcerates; often follows trauma. Lobules of proliferating capillaries with oedema and inflammation, like exuberant granulation tissue. Pregnancy tumour (granuloma gravidarum) of the gingiva regresses after delivery.
  • Glomus tumour: benign, exquisitely painful tumour of the modified smooth muscle cells of the glomus body; typically under a fingernail; small, red-blue; branching vessels surrounded by nests of uniform, round glomus cells. Excision cures.

A pyogenic granuloma is neither pyogenic nor a granuloma: it is a benign capillary proliferation with no pus and no granulomas. Bacillary angiomatosis (Bartonella in immunosuppressed patients) mimics Kaposi sarcoma but has neutrophils and bacilli and is cured by antibiotics.

Kaposi sarcoma

An intermediate-grade (locally aggressive, occasionally metastasising) vascular neoplasm caused by human herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8, KSHV). The virus infects endothelial cells; latent viral proteins drive proliferation and block death (v-cyclin, LANA inhibiting p53, vFLIP activating NF-κB), while immune deficiency lets infected cells escape and inflammatory cytokines feed the spindle cells.

Angiosarcoma

A malignant endothelial neoplasm of older adults: skin (scalp and face), soft tissue, breast and liver. Associations: hepatic angiosarcoma with arsenic, Thorotrast and polyvinyl chloride; lymphoedema after radical mastectomy (Stewart–Treves syndrome); radiation. Gross: small red nodules that grow into large, fleshy red-tan to grey-white masses with haemorrhage and necrosis. Microscopy: from plump atypical endothelial cells lining irregular channels to solid sheets of undifferentiated spindle cells; CD31 and ERG positive. Aggressive, with about 30% five-year survival.

15 one-liners

  1. Hyaline arteriolosclerosis: benign hypertension, diabetes; glassy pink walls.
  2. Hyperplastic: malignant hypertension; onion-skin, fibrinoid necrosis.
  3. Mönckeberg: medial calcification, lumen spared, little significance.
  4. Major risks: LDL, hypertension, smoking, diabetes; they multiply.
  5. Response to injury: endothelium → LDL → macrophages → PDGF → SMC cap.
  6. Fatty streak: earliest lesion, foam cells, seen in children.
  7. Plaque: cap, shoulder, necrotic core with cholesterol clefts.
  8. Worst site: lower abdominal aorta, then coronaries.
  9. Vulnerable plaque: thin cap, big core, many macrophages; may be small.
  10. AAA: below renals, atherosclerosis; repair at 5 cm or more.
  11. Syphilitic aneurysm: ascending aorta, tree bark, aortic regurgitation.
  12. Dissection: hypertension, cystic medial degeneration; Stanford A = ascending.
  13. GCA over 50, Takayasu under 50; both granulomatous.
  14. PAN: HBV, ANCA negative, mixed-age lesions; GPA PR3; MPA MPO.
  15. Kaposi: HHV-8; patch → plaque → nodule; four clinical forms.

  • A for Ascending, A for Acute surgery: Stanford type A
  • CRASH and Burn: Kawasaki disease
  • Size sorts vasculitis: large GCA/Takayasu; medium PAN/Kawasaki; small ANCA and IgA
