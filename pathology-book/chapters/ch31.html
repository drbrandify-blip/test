31.1 Reading the skin: structure and vocabulary

The epidermis is a stratified squamous epithelium of keratinocytes held together by desmosomes and anchored to the basement membrane by hemidesmosomes. Melanocytes sit in the basal layer and pass pigment to keratinocytes; Langerhans cells catch antigens and carry them to lymph nodes, making the skin an immune organ. Below lie the dermis and adnexa. Every skin disease is described with a small set of words: learn them once and every slide becomes readable.

Clinical terms: macule flat coloured spot ≤ 5 mm (patch if larger) · papule raised ≤ 5 mm, nodule > 5 mm · plaque raised, flat-topped > 5 mm · vesicle fluid-filled ≤ 5 mm, bulla > 5 mm · pustule pus-filled · wheal itchy, transient dermal oedema · scale plate of imperfect keratin · lichenification thick, rough skin from rubbing · excoriation scratch mark · onycholysis nail lifted off its bed.

31.2 Acute inflammatory dermatoses

Acute lesions last days to weeks; mononuclear cells, oedema and epidermal or vascular injury dominate.

Urticaria (hives)

Localised mast cell degranulation makes dermal venules leaky, producing itchy, oedematous wheals that appear and fade within hours (usually under 24 h). Peak age 20–40. Angioedema is the same process deeper in the dermis and subcutis.

Causes: IgE-dependent type I hypersensitivity (pollen, foods, drugs, insect venom); IgE-independent direct mast cell release (opiates, some antibiotics, radiocontrast); hereditary angioedema from inherited C1 inhibitor deficiency (uncontrolled bradykinin; laryngeal oedema can kill). Microscopy is subtle: superficial dermal oedema separating collagen bundles, sparse perivenular mononuclear cells and a few eosinophils.

Acute eczematous dermatitis

“Eczema” is a clinical term for red, papulovesicular, oozing, crusted lesions that, if they persist, become raised scaling plaques. Types: allergic contact (nickel, poison ivy, cosmetics), atopic, drug-related, photo-eczematous and primary irritant dermatitis.

  • Acute: red, itchy, weeping papulovesicles at the site of contact
  • Chronic: dry, scaly, lichenified plaques
  • Spongiosis: oedema stretches intercellular bridges → spongiotic vesicles
  • Superficial perivascular lymphocytes, papillary dermal oedema; eosinophils in drug reactions
  • Chronic: acanthosis and hyperkeratosis

Erythema multiforme (EM)

A self-limited hypersensitivity reaction to infections (herpes simplex, mycoplasma) and drugs (sulfonamides, penicillin, anticonvulsants such as phenytoin, salicylates, antimalarials). Cytotoxic CD8+ T cells attack basal keratinocytes. Lesions are “multiform” (macules, papules, vesicles, bullae); the hallmark is the target lesion: a red macule or papule with a pale, vesicular or eroded centre.

Stevens–Johnson syndrome: extensive disease with erosions of lips, mouth, conjunctiva, urethra and genitals, often drug-induced. Toxic epidermal necrolysis: full-thickness necrosis and sloughing of skin and mucosa, like a burn. Microscopy: lymphocytes along the dermoepidermal junction with necrotic keratinocytes (interface dermatitis), dermal oedema → blisters.

31.3 Chronic inflammatory dermatoses

Psoriasis

A common chronic immune-mediated dermatosis (1–2% of people in Western series), sometimes with arthritis (see Chapter 28), myopathy or enteropathy. Plaques favour the elbows, knees, scalp, lumbosacral area, intergluteal cleft and glans penis.

!HLA-C*06:02 + trigger (trauma, infection, drugs) -> Dendritic cells release IL-23 -> *TH17 and TH1 cells -> IL-17, IL-22, TNF -> Keratinocyte hyperproliferation -> !Thick plaque, silvery scale IL-17, IL-22, TNF -> Neutrophils recruited -> Munro microabscesses
  • Well-demarcated pink–salmon plaques with loosely adherent silver-white scale
  • Auspitz sign: pinpoint bleeding when the scale is lifted
  • Koebner phenomenon: lesions at sites of trauma
  • Nails (about a third): pitting, yellow-brown discolouration, onycholysis
  • Marked acanthosis with regular elongation of rete ridges (“test tubes in a rack”); mitoses above the basal layer
  • Thin or absent granular layer, thick parakeratotic scale
  • Thinned suprapapillary plates over dilated, tortuous papillary capillaries (hence Auspitz)
  • Neutrophils: spongiform pustules of Kogoj (spinous layer) and Munro microabscesses (stratum corneum)

Lichen planus

A self-limited (often 1–2 years) disorder of skin and mucosa. CD8+ T cells attack basal keratinocytes that display altered self antigens, so this is an interface dermatitis. Lesions favour the flexor wrists, elbows and glans penis; oral lesions (white, lacy, net-like) occur in about 70% and may persist for years. Healing leaves hyperpigmentation.

  • Itchy, violaceous, flat-topped polygonal papules
  • Wickham striae: fine white lines on the papules (hypergranulosis)
  • Koebner phenomenon
  • Dense band-like lymphocytic infiltrate hugging the dermoepidermal junction
  • Basal cell degeneration (vacuolisation), “squamatisation” of the basal layer
  • Sawtooth rete ridges; hyperkeratosis, wedge-shaped hypergranulosis
  • Civatte (colloid) bodies: anucleate necrotic basal cells in the papillary dermis

Lichen planus: the six Ps

Pruritic, Purple, Polygonal, Planar (flat-topped) Papules and Plaques. Psoriasis instead is Salmon, Silvery-Scaled and on extensor surfaces.

31.4 Blistering (bullous) diseases

The level of the split decides what you see. Keratinocytes are glued to each other by desmosomes (desmogleins) and to the basement membrane by hemidesmosomes (BP180, BP230). Antibodies against desmosomes make cells fall apart within the epidermis (acantholysis): the roof is thin, so blisters are flaccid, rupture easily and leave erosions. Antibodies against hemidesmosomes lift the whole epidermis off the dermis: the roof is thick, so blisters are tense and stay intact.

Pemphigus

An uncommon autoimmune disease (usually 40–60 years) in which IgG autoantibodies against desmogleins cause acantholysis (type II hypersensitivity). Pemphigus vulgaris (over 80% of cases) targets desmoglein 3 (± 1) and involves mucosa and skin: scalp, face, axilla, groin, trunk and pressure points. Pemphigus foliaceus targets desmoglein 1, gives superficial (subcorneal) blisters on scalp, face, chest and back and rarely affects mucosa. A paraneoplastic form accompanies lymphomas.

  • Flaccid vesicles and bullae that rupture easily, leaving painful erosions with crust
  • Oral erosions often appear first
  • Nikolsky sign positive
  • Suprabasal acantholytic blister containing rounded, detached keratinocytes
  • Basal cells stay attached to the BM: a “row of tombstones”
  • IF: intercellular IgG around keratinocytes: “fishnet” / net-like

Bullous pemphigoid

The commonest autoimmune blistering disease, typically in the elderly. IgG autoantibodies bind hemidesmosome proteins BPAG2 (BP180, type XVII collagen) and BPAG1 (BP230); complement is activated and neutrophils and eosinophils are recruited, splitting the epidermis from the dermis at the lamina lucida.

  • Tense bullae with clear fluid on normal or red skin
  • Inner thighs, flexor forearms, axillae, groin, lower abdomen
  • Oral involvement in only about 10–15%; heals without scarring
  • Subepidermal, non-acantholytic blister; roof is the whole epidermis
  • Dermal infiltrate rich in eosinophils, some lymphocytes and neutrophils
  • IF: linear IgG and C3 along the basement membrane

Dermatitis herpetiformis (DH)

Intensely itchy, grouped vesicles and urticarial plaques, bilateral and symmetrical on extensor surfaces: elbows, knees, upper back, buttocks. Men in the 3rd–4th decades. Closely linked to coeliac disease (HLA-DQ2/DQ8); both respond to a gluten-free diet (dapsone controls the rash).

Pathogenesis: gluten → IgA against gliadin and transglutaminase → IgA cross-reacts with skin (epidermal transglutaminase, reticulin) and deposits at the tips of dermal papillae → complement → neutrophils → microabscesses → blister. Microscopy: neutrophils and fibrin at the papillary tips, basal vacuolisation and small subepidermal blisters; IF: granular IgA at the papillary tips.

Nikolsky sign (epidermis shears off with sideways pressure) is positive in pemphigus vulgaris, and also in toxic epidermal necrolysis and staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome, all of which split within or just below a weak epidermis. It is negative in bullous pemphigoid. Do not confuse “pemphigus” (desmosome, young-middle age, mouth) with “pemphigoid” (hemidesmosome, elderly, tense).

31.5 Benign and premalignant epithelial lesions

Seborrhoeic keratosis

Very common in middle-aged and older people, mainly on the trunk. Activating FGFR3 mutations in many. The sudden eruption of hundreds of lesions (Leser–Trélat sign) is a paraneoplastic marker, usually of GI adenocarcinoma.

  • Round, flat, coin-like, waxy tan–brown plaque with a velvety or granular surface; “stuck-on” look
  • Exophytic, sharply demarcated sheets of small basaloid cells; variable melanin
  • Hyperkeratosis with horn cysts and pseudo-horn cysts (keratin-filled invaginations)
  • Acanthosis nigricans: thick, velvety, hyperpigmented skin in flexures (axilla, neck, groin). Usually benign (obesity, insulin resistance); in older adults it may signal an occult adenocarcinoma, often gastric.
  • Keratoacanthoma: a flesh-coloured, dome-shaped nodule with a central keratin-filled crater on sun-exposed skin of older men; grows rapidly over weeks and often regresses spontaneously. Microscopy: cup-shaped mass of glassy eosinophilic keratinocytes around a keratin plug; many regard it as a well-differentiated SCC variant.

Actinic (solar) keratosis: the precursor of SCC

Caused by chronic sun exposure in fair-skinned people; lesions are usually < 1 cm, tan-brown, red or skin-coloured, with a rough, sandpaper-like feel; some form a cutaneous horn. TP53 mutations are common. A minority progress to SCC, so they are treated by cryotherapy or topical agents.

  • Rough, scaly patch on face, scalp, back of hands
  • Atypia of the lower epidermis (basal layers), not full thickness
  • Parakeratosis, loss of granular layer
  • Solar elastosis: blue-grey degenerated elastic fibres in the dermis

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)

The second commonest skin cancer, on sun-exposed skin of older people, men more than women. Risk factors: UV light (TP53 mutation), immunosuppression (transplant recipients; HPV-related), xeroderma pigmentosum, arsenic, ionising radiation, and chronic scars, burns and draining sinuses (Marjolin ulcer). SCC in situ (Bowen disease) is a sharply defined, red, scaly plaque with full-thickness atypia.

  • Nodular, hyperkeratotic, scaly lesion that may ulcerate
  • Nests and sheets of atypical keratinocytes invading the dermis
  • Well differentiated: polygonal cells, intercellular bridges, keratin pearls, dyskeratosis
  • Poorly differentiated: anaplastic cells, little keratin
Behaviour: grows locally; fewer than 5% of skin SCCs metastasise to regional nodes (higher in mucosa, scars and the immunosuppressed).

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC)

The commonest invasive cancer in humans. It grows slowly on sun-exposed skin of fair people and rarely metastasises, but untreated it erodes deeply (“rodent ulcer”) into bone and sinuses. It is driven by the Hedgehog pathway: loss of PTCH1 (germline in Gorlin / naevoid BCC syndrome, with multiple BCCs, jaw cysts and medulloblastoma) or activating SMO mutations; the SMO inhibitor vismodegib treats advanced cases.

  • Pearly papule with rolled edges and telangiectasia; some pigmented
  • Late: central ulcer with rolled border
  • Face, especially around the nose and eyes
  • Nests and cords of basaloid cells (hyperchromatic, scant cytoplasm) from the epidermis
  • Peripheral palisading of nuclei at the edge of the nests
  • Retraction clefts between tumour and fibrotic or mucinous stroma
  • Superficial multifocal or nodular patterns

Consanguinity and childhood skin cancer. Cousin marriage is common in Pakistan and raises the risk of autosomal recessive disease. Xeroderma pigmentosum is one: defective nucleotide excision repair leaves UV damage unrepaired, so affected children develop freckling, actinic keratoses, SCC, BCC and melanoma at a young age. Strict sun protection and genetic counselling of the family are essential.

31.7 Melanocytic naevi and malignant melanoma

Melanocytic naevi

A melanocytic naevus is any benign, congenital or acquired neoplasm of melanocytes. Acquired naevi carry activating BRAF (or NRAS) mutations; the cells grow, then stop because the oncogene triggers senescence (p16). Clinically they are small (usually < 6 mm), tan–brown, uniformly pigmented with round, sharp borders.

Maturation: the single feature that separates a naevus from melanoma. In a benign naevus, the superficial cells are large, nested and pigmented; deeper cells become smaller with less pigment, form cords and finally spindle cells that resemble nerve (“neurotisation”). This orderly change with depth is maturation. Melanoma cells stay large and atypical all the way down: no maturation.

Dysplastic naevus

Larger than ordinary naevi (often > 5 mm), flat or slightly raised with a “pebbly” surface, variegated pigment and irregular borders; may number in the hundreds (dysplastic naevus syndrome, familial melanoma with germline CDKN2A mutation). Microscopy: compound naevus with architectural atypia (enlarged junctional nests that bridge adjacent rete ridges, lentiginous single-cell spread along the basal layer) and cytologic atypia (enlarged, angulated, hyperchromatic nuclei), plus lamellar fibrosis around rete ridges and a sparse lymphocytic infiltrate. Most never progress, but they mark a person at risk of melanoma.

Malignant melanoma

Far less common than BCC and SCC but far more deadly. Risk factors: intermittent intense sun exposure (childhood sunburns), fair skin, many or dysplastic naevi, family history, xeroderma pigmentosum. Most arise in the skin; others in mucosa, eye (uvea) and meninges.

ABCDE of melanoma

Asymmetry · Border irregular · Colour variegated · Diameter > 6 mm · Evolution (change in size, shape, colour; itching, bleeding). A new pigmented lesion in adult life, or a naevus that changes, needs a biopsy.

Melanoma slide in 10 seconds. Atypical melanocytes scattered upward through the epidermis (pagetoid) instead of neat junctional nests; dermal sheets of large cells with cherry-red nucleoli and pigment that do not get smaller with depth; mitoses, ulceration. A naevus is symmetrical, nested and matures; a melanoma is not.

Skin from the face: nests of dark, crowded cells with scant cytoplasm budding from the epidermis into the dermis; nuclei at the edge of each nest line up in a row; clear gaps separate the nests from the stroma. (a) Diagnosis. (b) Name the two features described. (c) Its behaviour.

Answer

(a) Basal cell carcinoma. (b) Peripheral palisading; retraction clefts. (c) Locally invasive (rodent ulcer); metastasis is extremely rare.

Station 3 · Immunofluorescence1 minute
Task

Direct IF, perilesional skin; 50-year-old with oral erosions and flaccid blisters: IgG outlines every keratinocyte in a net-like pattern. (a) Diagnosis. (b) Target antigen. (c) Level of the blister.

Answer

(a) Pemphigus vulgaris. (b) Desmoglein 3 (with or without desmoglein 1). (c) Suprabasal (intraepidermal) with acantholysis.

RAPID REVISION The Night-Before Page

15 one-liners

  1. Parakeratosis = nuclei in stratum corneum; spongiosis = intercellular oedema.
  2. Acantholysis = keratinocytes lose cohesion (desmosomes fail).
  3. Urticaria: mast cells, wheals within hours; C1 inhibitor deficiency = hereditary angioedema.
  4. Allergic contact dermatitis: type IV, Langerhans cells, spongiosis.
  5. Erythema multiforme: HSV or drugs, CD8 T cells, target lesions.
  6. Psoriasis: TH17/IL-23, parakeratosis, Munro microabscesses, Auspitz.
  7. Lichen planus: band-like lymphocytes, sawtooth rete, Civatte bodies.
  8. Pemphigus vulgaris: desmoglein 3, suprabasal, fishnet IgG, Nikolsky +.
  9. Bullous pemphigoid: BP180, subepidermal, eosinophils, linear IgG/C3.
  10. DH: coeliac, granular IgA at papillary tips, neutrophil microabscesses.
  11. Seborrhoeic keratosis: stuck-on, horn cysts; many at once = Leser–Trélat.
  12. Actinic keratosis: lower-epidermal atypia, solar elastosis → SCC.
  13. BCC: commonest; PTCH1; palisading, retraction clefts; rarely metastasises.
  14. Naevus matures with depth; melanoma does not.
  15. Melanoma: BRAF, CDKN2A; ABCDE; Breslow is the key prognostic factor.

Memory hooks

  • ABCDE: Asymmetry, Border, Colour, Diameter > 6 mm, Evolution
  • Six Ps of lichen planus: pruritic, purple, polygonal, planar papules, plaques
  • Pemphig-US splits Up in the epidermis; pemphig-OID lifts Off the dermis
  • BCC = 3 Ps: Pearly, Palisading, PTCH1
