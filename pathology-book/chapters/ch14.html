14.1 Lymphadenopathy: reactive or neoplastic?

Lymph nodes enlarge for two reasons: lymphocytes and macrophages react to antigen, or a clone takes over. Reactive nodes keep their architecture and contain a mixture of cells; a lymphoma effaces the architecture with a monotonous, clonal population.

Red flags in a node

  • Size over 2 cm; hard (carcinoma) or rubbery (lymphoma); fixed or matted
  • Supraclavicular node (always abnormal); painless and progressive growth
  • B symptoms: fever, drenching night sweats, weight loss over 10%
  • Hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias, older age

FNA is useful for metastatic carcinoma and tuberculosis, but a lymphoma is diagnosed on an excised whole node: architecture, IHC and clonality are needed.

Pakistan is among the five highest tuberculosis-burden countries (incidence about 266 per 100,000). Tuberculous lymphadenitis is therefore a very common cause of neck swelling and the chief mimic of lymphoma. Matted nodes with caseating granulomas mean TB; a monotonous infiltrate effacing the node means lymphoma. A node that fails to respond to anti-TB therapy, or shows no granulomas, needs excision with IHC and GeneXpert.

14.2 Lymphoid neoplasms: the principles

About 85–90% of lymphoid neoplasms are of B-cell origin; the rest are T or NK cell. Each is a monoclonal expansion (one light chain, κ or λ, in B-cell tumours; one T-cell receptor rearrangement in T-cell tumours) of a cell frozen at a normal stage of development. That stage decides its markers and its behaviour.

The lymphoma paradox. Indolent lymphomas (follicular, CLL/SLL, MALT) grow slowly, so patients live for years, but chemotherapy, which kills dividing cells, rarely cures them. Aggressive lymphomas (DLBCL, Burkitt) kill within months if untreated, yet because their cells divide fast, intensive chemotherapy cures many. Slow is not always good; fast is not always fatal.

14.3 Hodgkin lymphoma (HL)

Hodgkin lymphoma is a lymphoid neoplasm, usually arising in a single node or chain of nodes, in which a few large neoplastic Reed–Sternberg (RS) cells (germinal-centre B-cell origin) lie in an abundant reactive background of lymphocytes, eosinophils, plasma cells, histiocytes and fibrosis. It spreads in an orderly fashion to anatomically contiguous nodes.

One of the commonest cancers of young adults, with a second peak in older age. The RS cells are a small minority of the tumour mass; the rest is an army they have recruited.

The Reed–Sternberg cell

  • Large (about 45 µm), with two mirror-image nuclei or a bilobed nucleus
  • Each lobe has a large, eosinophilic, inclusion-like nucleolus the size of a small lymphocyte, with a clear halo: the “owl-eye” appearance
  • Abundant cytoplasm
  • Classical HL: CD15+, CD30+, PAX5 weak; CD45−, usually CD20−

Variants

  • Hodgkin cell: mononuclear variant
  • Lacunar cell: folded nucleus in a clear space (cytoplasm retracts in formalin); nodular sclerosis
  • LP (“popcorn”) cell: multilobed nucleus; NLPHL; CD20+, CD15−, CD30−
  • Mummified cell: shrunken, dark, apoptotic

14.4 B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas

Follicular lymphoma

The commonest indolent NHL; middle-aged adults, both sexes. A neoplasm of germinal-centre B cells driven by t(14;18)(q32;q21), which places BCL2 next to the IGH locus. BCL2 blocks apoptosis, so cells that should die in the germinal centre survive. Mutations of the histone methyltransferase KMT2D are also common.

  • Painless, often generalised lymphadenopathy that may wax and wane
  • Cut surface may show vague nodularity
  • Marrow involved in most patients (paratrabecular aggregates)
  • Nodular (follicular) growth effacing the node
  • Centrocytes: small cells with cleaved, angular nuclei (most)
  • Centroblasts: larger cells, open chromatin, several nucleoli (few)
  • CD19, CD20, CD10, BCL6 and BCL2 positive
Course: patients live for many years, but the disease is rarely cured. In 30–50% it transforms to DLBCL, after which survival is short.

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL)

The commonest NHL of adults (about a third). Median age about 60, but it occurs at any age. It arises de novo or by transformation of an indolent lymphoma. About a third have rearrangement of BCL6 (3q27); others carry t(14;18) or MYC rearrangements. Typical presentation: a rapidly enlarging mass at one nodal or extranodal site (Waldeyer ring, gut, skin, bone, brain).

  • Fleshy, white, “fish-flesh” mass
  • Often extranodal; may show necrosis
  • Diffuse sheets of large B cells (nuclei several times a small lymphocyte)
  • Vesicular nuclei, prominent nucleoli (centroblast- or immunoblast-like)
  • CD19, CD20; variable CD10, BCL6
Special settings: EBV-driven DLBCL in AIDS and after transplantation; primary effusion lymphoma (HHV-8/KSHV) in body cavities; primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma in young women. Aggressive and fatal if untreated, but rituximab plus combination chemotherapy (R-CHOP) brings remission in most and cures about half.

Burkitt lymphoma

A highly aggressive germinal-centre B-cell lymphoma, the fastest-growing human tumour, defined by translocation of MYC on chromosome 8, usually t(8;14) with IGH (or t(2;8), t(8;22) with the light-chain genes). MYC drives growth and the Warburg (aerobic glycolytic) metabolism needed to build new cells.

  • Endemic (African): children; jaw (mandible/maxilla) mass; EBV in nearly all
  • Sporadic: children and young adults; ileocaecal or peritoneal mass, ovaries, kidneys; EBV in a minority
  • Immunodeficiency-associated: HIV
  • Responds to intensive chemotherapy: most children and young adults are cured; risk of tumour lysis syndrome
  • Diffuse sheets of monotonous intermediate-sized cells, round nuclei, 2–5 small nucleoli
  • Deeply basophilic cytoplasm with lipid vacuoles (smear/touch prep)
  • Very high mitotic and apoptotic rate (Ki-67 near 100%)
  • Tingible-body macrophages with nuclear debris: clear spaces in a blue sea = “starry sky”
  • CD19, CD20, CD10, BCL6+; BCL2 negative

Mantle cell lymphoma

A neoplasm of naive B cells of the mantle zone; usually older men. t(11;14)(q13;q32) joins the CCND1 gene to IGH, causing cyclin D1 overexpression, which pushes cells from G1 into S phase. Most patients present with generalised lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly and marrow involvement; the gut may show multiple polyps (lymphomatoid polyposis). Small-to-intermediate cells with irregular nuclei, diffuse or vaguely nodular, with no proliferation centres. Phenotype: CD19, CD20, CD5+, CD23−, cyclin D1+. Moderately aggressive and incurable with standard chemotherapy.

Extranodal marginal zone (MALT) lymphoma

Indolent B-cell lymphomas that arise in mucosa or glands affected by chronic inflammation: the stomach in H. pylori gastritis, salivary glands in Sjögren syndrome, the thyroid in Hashimoto thyroiditis. They show how chronic antigen stimulation becomes a cancer.

Microscopy: marginal-zone cells (small, with abundant pale cytoplasm; “monocytoid”), plasma-cell differentiation and lymphoepithelial lesions (tumour cells invading glandular epithelium). CD20+, CD5−, CD10−, CD23−. Usually localised for long periods.

CLL / small lymphocytic lymphoma

The same neoplasm as CLL (Chapter 13), called SLL when the nodes rather than the blood dominate. Nodes are effaced by small round lymphocytes with pale proliferation centres; the cells are CD5+ and CD23+.

14.6 Plasma cell neoplasms

Plasma cell neoplasms are clonal expansions of immunoglobulin-secreting cells. They usually make a complete immunoglobulin or a free light chain, detected as a monoclonal (M) protein. The M protein, the bone damage and the marrow takeover explain almost every feature.

Multiple myeloma

Multiple myeloma is a neoplasm of plasma cells that grows mainly in the bone marrow, produces multifocal lytic bone lesions and usually secretes an M protein into serum and/or urine.

Older adults (median about 70); men and people of African descent more often. Early lesions are translocations joining IGH (14q32) to genes such as CCND1 or FGFR3; MYC rearrangement and TP53 loss come later. Myeloma cells grow on IL-6 from marrow stromal cells.

Clinical features

  • Bone pain (back), pathological fractures, cord compression
  • Hypercalcaemia: confusion, weakness, constipation, polyuria
  • Recurrent bacterial infections (pneumococcus, S. aureus, E. coli): the leading cause of death, with renal failure second
  • Normocytic anaemia; hyperviscosity in some (especially IgA)
  • Bortezomib, lenalidomide and stem-cell transplantation prolong survival, but cure is rare

Why is ALP normal and the bone scan cold in myeloma? Myeloma lesions are purely lytic: osteoclasts are switched on while osteoblasts are switched off (DKK1). ALP and isotope uptake come from osteoblast activity, so they stay normal. Plain X-rays (or MRI/CT), not a bone scan, show myeloma; a raised ALP points instead to fracture healing, liver disease or osteoblastic metastases.

Station 1 · Slide2 minutes
Task

Cervical lymph node from a 22-year-old: architecture effaced; broad bands of collagen divide the node into nodules; scattered large cells with folded nuclei sit in clear spaces, among lymphocytes, eosinophils and histiocytes. (a) Identify. (b) Name the large cells and two markers they express. (c) Which mediastinal finding often accompanies it?

Answer

(a) Classical Hodgkin lymphoma, nodular sclerosis type. (b) Lacunar cells (RS variant): CD15+, CD30+ (CD45−). (c) A mediastinal mass.

Station 2 · Slide2 minutes
Task

Ileocaecal mass in a 10-year-old: diffuse sheets of uniform medium-sized cells with round nuclei and many mitoses; scattered pale macrophages containing nuclear debris give a speckled look. (a) Diagnosis? (b) Name the pattern. (c) Translocation and gene.

Answer

(a) Burkitt lymphoma (sporadic). (b) Starry-sky pattern (tingible-body macrophages). (c) t(8;14), MYC.

Task

A 68-year-old: Hb 8.4 g/dL, ESR 120 mm/h, total protein 10.2 g/dL with albumin 3.1 g/dL, calcium 11.8 mg/dL, creatinine 2.4 mg/dL, ALP normal. Smear: red cells in stacks. Serum electrophoresis: narrow band in the γ region. (a) Diagnosis? (b) Name the smear finding and the band. (c) Two further tests.

Answer

(a) Multiple myeloma. (b) Rouleaux; M spike (monoclonal Ig). (c) Urine Bence Jones protein / serum free light chains; bone marrow for clonal plasma cells; skeletal survey.

RAPID REVISION The Night-Before Page

15 one-liners

  1. Reactive nodes keep architecture; lymphoma effaces it with a clone.
  2. Paracortical hyperplasia = viral (EBV); sinus histiocytosis = draining cancer.
  3. Lymphoma needs an excision biopsy, not just FNA.
  4. RS cell: owl-eye nucleoli, CD15+, CD30+, CD45−.
  5. Nodular sclerosis: commonest HL, young women and men, mediastinum, lacunar cells.
  6. Mixed cellularity: older men, EBV, many RS cells.
  7. NLPHL: popcorn cells, CD20+, CD15/30 negative.
  8. HL spreads contiguously; NHL jumps and goes extranodal.
  9. Ann Arbor: II one side, III both sides, IV extralymphatic organs.
  10. Follicular: t(14;18) BCL2, centrocytes, BCL2+ follicles, indolent.
  11. DLBCL: commonest NHL; BCL6; R-CHOP cures about half.
  12. Burkitt: t(8;14) MYC, starry sky, jaw (endemic) or ileocaecum.
  13. Mantle cell: t(11;14) cyclin D1, CD5+ CD23−, gut polyps.
  14. MALT: H. pylori, Sjögren, Hashimoto; lymphoepithelial lesions.
  15. Myeloma: CRAB, M spike, Bence Jones, rouleaux, normal ALP.

Memory hooks

  • IGH lives on 14: 8 MYC (Burkitt), 11 cyclin D1 (Mantle), 18 BCL2 (Follicular)
  • CRAB: Calcium, Renal, Anaemia, Bone
  • Slow is not always good: indolent lymphomas are incurable; aggressive ones often curable
