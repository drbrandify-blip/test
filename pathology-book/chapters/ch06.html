6.1 Cirrhosis

Cirrhosis is a diffuse process in which the normal architecture of the whole liver is replaced by regenerative parenchymal nodules surrounded by bridging fibrous septa. It is the end stage of many chronic liver diseases.

All three features are needed. (1) Bridging fibrous septa, delicate bands or broad scars linking portal tracts to each other and to central veins; (2) parenchymal nodules of regenerating hepatocytes trapped by the septa; (3) diffuse involvement of the entire liver. A single scar is not cirrhosis; nodules without fibrosis are not cirrhosis.

Causes

  • Chronic viral hepatitis: HCV, HBV (± HDV)
  • Alcoholic liver disease; non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)
  • Biliary disease: primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, long-standing duct obstruction
  • Metabolic: haemochromatosis, Wilson disease, α1-antitrypsin deficiency
  • Autoimmune hepatitis; drugs (e.g. methotrexate)
  • Chronic right heart failure (cardiac fibrosis)
  • Cryptogenic: no cause found (many are burnt-out NAFLD)

HEPATIC

Hepatitis viruses B, C (± D) · Ethanol and Excess fat (NAFLD) · Primary biliary cholangitis / PSC · Autoimmune hepatitis and α1-antitrypsin · Toxins and drugs · Iron and copper (haemochromatosis, Wilson) · Cardiac and Cryptogenic

Hepatitis C is the leading cause of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma in Pakistan, with hepatitis B second (prevalence figures in Chapter 5). With about one in four adults diabetic, NAFLD is a growing contributor.

Pathogenesis: the stellate cell is the scar-maker

!Chronic injury: virus, alcohol, fat -> Hepatocyte death, inflammation -> Kupffer cells: TGF-β, PDGF, TNF -> *Stellate cells activated -> Myofibroblasts lay down collagen -> Bridging fibrous septa -> !Cirrhosis Myofibroblasts lay down collagen -> Capillarised, contracted sinusoids -> *Portal hypertension, failing function Hepatocyte death, inflammation -> Survivors regenerate as nodules -> !Cirrhosis

Hepatic stellate cells (Ito cells) lie in the space of Disse and normally store vitamin A. Injury activates them through cytokines from Kupffer cells (TGF-β, PDGF, TNF), reactive oxygen species and damaged matrix. They lose their vitamin A and become contractile myofibroblasts that lay down type I and III collagen.

  • Collagen in the space of Disse and loss of endothelial fenestrae (“capillarisation” of sinusoids) block exchange between plasma and hepatocytes → liver failure
  • Contraction of myofibroblasts and septal scarring raise sinusoidal resistance → portal hypertension
  • Vascular shunts in the septa divert blood past hepatocytes
  • Surviving hepatocytes regenerate as nodules, helped by progenitor cells (ductular reaction)
  • Fibrosis can slowly regress if the cause is removed (e.g. HCV cured)
  • Liver usually shrunken, firm, with a nodular surface
  • Micronodular: uniform nodules < 3 mm (alcohol, haemochromatosis, biliary)
  • Macronodular: nodules > 3 mm, varying sizes, broad scars (post-viral)
  • Cut surface: nodules separated by grey-white fibrous bands; nodules yellow (fat), green (bile) or rusty brown (iron)
  • Regenerative nodules of hepatocytes without normal lobular architecture or central veins
  • Nodules encircled by fibrous septa joining portal tracts and central veins
  • Septa contain lymphocytes and ductular reaction
  • Clues to the cause: Mallory–Denk bodies (alcohol, NAFLD), ground-glass cells (HBV), lymphoid aggregates (HCV), iron (Prussian blue), PAS+ globules (α1-AT)
Outcome: many patients are silent for years (compensated). Decompensation brings anorexia, weight loss, jaundice, ascites, variceal bleeding and encephalopathy. Death comes from progressive liver failure, a complication of portal hypertension, or hepatocellular carcinoma.

Micro- vs macronodular is a weak classification. One cause can produce both: alcoholic cirrhosis begins micronodular and becomes macronodular as nodules keep growing. Robbins therefore classifies cirrhosis by cause; quote the size cut-off (3 mm) but give the aetiology.

6.2 Portal hypertension

Portal pressure rises when resistance to portal flow increases or, less often, when flow increases. In cirrhosis both happen: the scarred, contracted sinusoids resist flow, and nitric oxide-mediated dilatation of splanchnic arteries pours more blood into the portal system (a hyperdynamic circulation).

Why ascites forms

  1. Sinusoidal hypertension pushes fluid into the space of Disse; hepatic lymph flow overwhelms the thoracic duct and lymph weeps into the peritoneum.
  2. Splanchnic vasodilatation lowers effective arterial volume → RAAS and ADH → sodium and water retention.
  3. Hypoalbuminaemia lowers plasma oncotic pressure.

Ascitic fluid is serous, low in protein; infection without an obvious source is spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.

6.3 Hepatic failure

About 80–90% of hepatic function must be lost before the liver fails. Failure may be acute (massive necrosis, Chapter 5), chronic (cirrhosis, the commonest route), acute-on-chronic (a new insult to a cirrhotic liver), or occur without overt necrosis when hepatocytes are poisoned (microvesicular fat in Reye syndrome and acute fatty liver of pregnancy).

6.4 Alcoholic and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease

Alcohol and the metabolic syndrome injure the liver in the same three overlapping stages. Only a minority progress: about 10–15% of heavy drinkers develop cirrhosis.

How alcohol injures hepatocytes

  • Acetaldehyde (from alcohol dehydrogenase, CYP2E1) forms toxic protein adducts
  • CYP2E1 generates reactive oxygen species
  • Excess NADH diverts metabolism to fat synthesis; lipoprotein export is impaired
  • Gut endotoxin triggers TNF release from Kupffer cells

Mallory–Denk bodies are tangled keratin 8 and 18 intermediate filaments forming eosinophilic, ropy cytoplasmic inclusions in ballooned hepatocytes. Typical of alcoholic hepatitis but not specific: also NAFLD, Wilson disease, chronic cholestasis and HCC.

6.5 Hereditary haemochromatosis

Haemochromatosis is excessive accumulation of body iron, most of it deposited in parenchymal cells of the liver, pancreas, heart, joints and endocrine glands. The hereditary form is an autosomal recessive disorder of excess intestinal iron absorption; the secondary form (haemosiderosis) follows parenteral iron, mainly repeated transfusions (β-thalassaemia major, Chapter 12), or ineffective erythropoiesis.

Total body iron is normally 2–6 g, and we absorb only 1–2 mg a day; there is no route for excreting excess iron. In hereditary haemochromatosis iron builds up by about 0.5–1 g a year and may exceed 50 g. Disease usually appears in the 40s–50s, after about 20 g has collected, and men are affected far more often (women lose iron through menstruation and pregnancy).

!HFE mutation (C282Y) -> *Hepcidin falls -> Ferroportin stays on enterocytes -> Excess iron absorbed -> Iron in parenchymal cells -> ROS: lipid peroxidation, DNA damage -> !Fibrosis, organ failure, HCC

HFE lies on chromosome 6p close to the HLA-A locus; most patients are homozygous for the C282Y mutation (cysteine → tyrosine at position 282). Hepcidin, made by the liver, binds the iron exporter ferroportin and makes the cell degrade it; less hepcidin means ferroportin keeps pumping iron out of enterocytes and macrophages into blood. Rarer forms: mutations of hepcidin itself or haemojuvelin (severe juvenile haemochromatosis), transferrin receptor 2, ferroportin.

  • Liver: dark rusty-brown, finely nodular (micronodular pigment cirrhosis)
  • Pancreas: deeply pigmented, fibrotic
  • Heart: enlarged, brown myocardium
  • Skin: slate-grey to bronze
  • Haemosiderin granules (golden-brown) first in periportal hepatocytes, then the whole lobule, bile ducts and Kupffer cells
  • Prussian blue (Perls) stain turns iron blue
  • Little inflammation; fibrosis leads to cirrhosis
  • Pancreas: iron in acini and islets → fibrosis; heart: iron in cardiac myocytes
Skin pigmentation is mainly increased melanin (with some dermal haemosiderin).

Haemochromatosis: the seven Cs

Cirrhosis · Carcinoma (HCC, risk about 200-fold) · Cardiomyopathy (failure, arrhythmia) · Carbohydrate intolerance (diabetes) · Colour (bronze skin) · Calcium pyrophosphate arthropathy · Castration (hypogonadism)

Clinical and lab picture

  • Classic but late triad: micronodular cirrhosis, diabetes mellitus, skin pigmentation (“bronze diabetes”)
  • Transferrin saturation high (the earliest clue), ferritin high, serum iron high; HFE genotyping; liver iron on biopsy or MRI
  • Treatment: repeated phlebotomy. Started before cirrhosis, life expectancy is normal; screen first-degree relatives

Hereditary vs secondary iron overload. In hereditary haemochromatosis iron enters through the gut and lands first in parenchymal cells (periportal hepatocytes). In transfusional overload, iron from destroyed red cells lands first in macrophages (Kupffer cells) and spleen, spilling into hepatocytes only when they are saturated.

6.7 Hepatocellular carcinoma

The commonest malignant tumours found in the liver are metastases (from colon, breast, lung, pancreas), which form multiple nodules that are often umbilicated. Among primary malignancies, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) dominates. Over 85% of cases arise in countries with high rates of chronic HBV infection (Asia, sub-Saharan Africa), where infection in infancy can produce HCC in young adults. Men are affected several times more often than women.

Risk factors

  • Chronic HBV: viral DNA integrates; HBx protein disturbs growth control; HCC can arise without cirrhosis
  • Chronic HCV: mostly through cirrhosis
  • Alcohol; NAFLD, obesity and diabetes
  • Aflatoxin B1 from Aspergillus flavus on badly stored grain and groundnuts: causes a characteristic TP53 codon 249 mutation
  • Haemochromatosis, α1-AT deficiency, tyrosinaemia
  • Cirrhosis of any cause: most HCCs arise in cirrhotic livers
!Chronic HBV, HCV, alcohol, NAFLD -> Repeated death and regeneration -> *Cirrhosis -> Dysplastic nodules -> !Hepatocellular carcinoma Repeated death and regeneration -> Mutations: TERT, β-catenin, TP53 -> Dysplastic nodules ~HBV integration, HBx protein -> !Hepatocellular carcinoma ~Aflatoxin: TP53 codon 249 -> !Hepatocellular carcinoma

Precursors: in cirrhotic livers, large-cell and small-cell change (dysplasia) and dysplastic nodules (low- and high-grade) precede HCC. Common mutations activate TERT (telomerase) and β-catenin or inactivate TP53.

  • Three patterns: unifocal (often large) mass, multifocal nodules, or diffusely infiltrative
  • Paler than the surrounding liver; soft; may be bile-stained green, haemorrhagic or necrotic
  • Usually on a background of cirrhosis
  • Strong tendency to invade veins: portal vein and hepatic veins/IVC, sometimes reaching the right atrium
  • Trabecular (cords several cells thick), pseudoglandular or solid sheets
  • Polygonal cells resembling hepatocytes: eosinophilic cytoplasm, central nuclei, prominent nucleoli; pleomorphism and giant cells when poorly differentiated
  • Bile in canaliculi or cells proves hepatocellular origin
  • No portal tracts; endothelium-lined sinusoid-like vessels
  • IHC: HepPar-1, arginase-1, glypican-3

Cirrhosis vs HCC in 10 seconds. Cirrhosis: round islands of liver cells, each ringed by pink-blue fibrous bands carrying lymphocytes and little ducts; no normal central veins. HCC: no fibrous rings and no portal tracts, just thick trabeculae of atypical liver-like cells with big nucleoli, separated by blood spaces; look for green bile and vascular invasion. Both together on one slide (HCC beside cirrhotic nodules) is a favourite.

Fibrolamellar carcinoma

A distinct variant of young adults (20–40 years), with equal sex incidence and no link to HBV, cirrhosis or other risk factors. It forms a single, large, hard, scirrhous tumour with fibrous bands. Microscopy: large polygonal oncocytic cells (abundant pink, mitochondria-rich cytoplasm) with prominent nucleoli, separated by parallel lamellae of dense collagen. It carries a DNAJB1–PRKACA fusion gene; AFP is usually normal, and the outlook is better because the tumour is often resectable from a healthy liver.

Clinical features and spread

  • Vague upper abdominal pain, malaise, weight loss; hard, irregular hepatomegaly
  • In a cirrhotic: sudden decompensation, bloody ascites, variceal bleed
  • Paraneoplastic: hypoglycaemia, erythrocytosis, hypercalcaemia
  • Spread within the liver and along veins; distant spread mainly to lungs, late
  • Death from cachexia, GI or variceal bleeding, liver failure with coma, or tumour rupture with haemorrhage

α-Fetoprotein (AFP) is raised in about half or more of patients with HCC, but it is neither sensitive nor specific: it also rises in yolk sac tumours, cirrhosis, chronic hepatitis, massive necrosis, normal pregnancy and fetal neural tube defects. Cirrhotic patients are screened with ultrasound (± AFP) every 6 months.

A 58-year-old man with HCV cirrhosis known for 6 years now has weight loss, increasing abdominal girth and right upper quadrant pain. The liver is hard and irregular. AFP 1,200 ng/mL. Ultrasound: a 7 cm mass in the right lobe with thrombus in the portal vein.

Reasoning: cirrhosis + sudden decompensation + hard mass + very high AFP + portal vein invasion → hepatocellular carcinoma arising in HCV cirrhosis. The portal vein thrombus is tumour invading the vein and worsens his ascites and portal hypertension.
