17.1 Renal function and the renal syndromes

Glomeruli are injured mainly by immune mechanisms (Chapter 16). Tubules and interstitium are injured by ischaemia, toxins and infection, and betray themselves not by heavy proteinuria or RBC casts but by failure of tubular work: poor concentration (polyuria, nocturia), salt wasting and metabolic acidosis.

Azotaemia is a rise in blood urea and creatinine caused by a fall in GFR. It is prerenal when perfusion is poor, postrenal when urine flow is obstructed. Uraemia is azotaemia plus the clinical and biochemical failure of many organs (pericarditis, gastritis, neuropathy, bleeding, acidosis).

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a rapid fall in GFR over hours to days with rising creatinine, often with oliguria (< 400 mL/day). Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is GFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m², or evidence of kidney damage such as albuminuria, lasting more than 3 months.

Urinalysis: the casts tell you where the injury is

A cast is a mould of a tubule: Tamm–Horsfall protein (uromodulin) from the thick ascending limb gels in the distal tubule and traps passing cells, proving that they came from the kidney, not the bladder.

AKI has three doors. Prerenal: a healthy kidney underperfused (haemorrhage, diarrhoea, burns, heart failure, sepsis). Intrinsic renal: ATN (commonest), RPGN, acute interstitial nephritis, HUS, malignant hypertension. Postrenal: obstruction of both outflows (BPH, stones, pelvic tumours).

17.2 Acute tubular injury (acute tubular necrosis)

Acute tubular injury / necrosis (ATN) is a clinicopathological entity in which damage to tubular epithelial cells causes an acute fall in renal function, usually with granular casts and tubular cells in the urine. It is the commonest cause of AKI. Robbins prefers “injury” because frank necrosis is often scanty.

17.3 Pyelonephritis

Tubulointerstitial nephritis (TIN) is inflammation falling mainly on tubules and interstitium, sparing glomeruli until late; acute (oedema, leukocytes) or chronic (mononuclear cells, fibrosis, atrophy). Causes: infection, drugs, urate, hypercalcaemia, obstruction, myeloma casts, immune disease.

Acute pyelonephritis

A suppurative bacterial inflammation of the kidney and renal pelvis. Gut gram-negative bacilli cause most cases: E. coli by far the commonest, then Proteus, Klebsiella, Enterobacter; Pseudomonas and enterococci follow instrumentation. Infection usually ascends; the haematogenous route (septicaemia, endocarditis, staphylococci, TB, fungi) is less common and seeds abscesses at random.

!Gut flora colonise distal urethra -> Enter bladder: short female urethra, catheter -> Stasis: obstruction, residual urine -> Vesicoureteral reflux -> Intrarenal reflux at poles -> *Acute pyelonephritis

Predisposing factors

  • Obstruction (BPH, stones, gravid uterus); catheters
  • Vesicoureteral reflux (congenital, neurogenic bladder)
  • Pregnancy, female sex, diabetes, immunosuppression

Why reflux matters. The ureter tunnels obliquely through the bladder wall, so voiding squeezes it shut. A short tunnel or atonic bladder lets urine shoot up and fall back as infected residual urine. Compound papillae at the poles have open ducts that admit it, so scars cluster at the poles.

  • One or both kidneys; patchy or diffuse
  • Discrete yellowish raised abscesses on the cortical surface
  • Yellow streaks of pus in the medulla
  • Patchy suppurative interstitial inflammation
  • Neutrophil aggregates within tubules (the source of WBC casts) and tubular necrosis
  • Glomeruli are spared: they resist infection
Complications: papillary necrosis (diabetes, obstruction); pyonephrosis (pus filling an obstructed pelvicalyceal system); perinephric abscess (pus breaks through the capsule). Healing leaves fibrous cortical scars over deformed calyces.

Clinical: sudden fever with chills, loin pain and tenderness, dysuria and frequency; pyuria, bacteriuria (≥ 105/mL) and WBC casts, which prove renal involvement. It settles with antibiotics; recurrence signals obstruction or reflux.

Papillary necrosis

Ischaemic (coagulative) necrosis of the renal papillae. The tips or distal two-thirds of the pyramids turn grey-white to yellow; microscopy shows ghost outlines of tubules with a band of neutrophils at the junction with living tissue. A sloughed papilla can cause colic, haematuria or obstruction.

Papillary necrosis = “DOSA”

Diabetes mellitus · Obstruction with infection · Sickle cell disease (or trait) · Analgesic abuse. All four starve the papilla, the end of a long, thin blood supply.

Chronic pyelonephritis and reflux nephropathy

Chronic pyelonephritis is chronic tubulointerstitial inflammation and scarring that involves the calyces and pelvis, the feature that separates it from every other scarred kidney. Two forms: reflux nephropathy (commoner), infection on congenital vesicoureteral and intrarenal reflux in early childhood, often silent until hypertension appears; and chronic obstructive pyelonephritis, recurrent infection behind an obstruction (bilateral with posterior urethral valves, unilateral with a stone).

  • One or both kidneys; if both, asymmetrically shrunken
  • Coarse, discrete, U-shaped corticomedullary scars, mostly at the poles
  • Underlying calyces dilated, blunted or deformed; papillae flattened
  • Uneven interstitial fibrosis with lymphocytes, plasma cells (± neutrophils)
  • Atrophic tubules; dilated tubules filled with pink, glassy colloid casts: “thyroidisation”
  • Chronic inflammation and fibrosis of the calyceal mucosa
  • Glomeruli normal or periglomerular fibrosis; later secondary FSGS

17.4 Drug-induced interstitial nephritis and analgesic nephropathy

Acute drug-induced (hypersensitivity) interstitial nephritis

An immune reaction, not direct toxicity, so it is unrelated to dose. The drug acts as a hapten: it binds tubular cell or matrix proteins and makes them immunogenic. Raised IgE and eosinophils point to a type I reaction; granulomas and T-cell infiltrates to a type IV reaction.

Common culprits

  • Antibiotics: penicillins (methicillin), rifampicin, sulfonamides
  • NSAIDs, proton pump inhibitors, thiazides, allopurinol

Clinical picture

  • About 2 weeks (2–40 days) after starting the drug
  • Fever, eosinophilia, rash; haematuria, mild proteinuria, urinary eosinophils
  • Rising creatinine, AKI in about half; recovers on stopping the drug
  • Interstitial oedema with infiltrates of lymphocytes and macrophages
  • Eosinophils (and neutrophils) may be numerous
  • Tubulitis: lymphocytes crossing the tubular basement membrane
  • Non-necrotising granulomas with giant cells (methicillin, thiazides, rifampicin)
  • Glomeruli normal, except NSAIDs, which can add minimal change disease (nephrotic syndrome)

Analgesic nephropathy

Chronic renal disease from years of heavy use of analgesic mixtures (classically phenacetin + aspirin + caffeine). The first lesion is papillary necrosis from two hits: paracetamol metabolites cause oxidative injury and aspirin blocks vasodilator prostaglandins, making the medulla ischaemic. Cortical tubulointerstitial nephritis follows.

Morphology: papillae brown, shrunken and necrotic (coagulative necrosis, ghost tubules, calcification) or sloughed; the cortex over them is depressed with tubular atrophy and fibrosis, while spared columns of Bertin bulge. Clinical: polyuria, nocturia, hypertension, anaemia, UTI, colic from a sloughed papilla; risk of urothelial carcinoma of the renal pelvis (Chapter 18).

NSAIDs and the kidney: four lesions. (1) Haemodynamic AKI (loss of vasodilator prostaglandins in dehydration or heart failure). (2) Hypersensitivity interstitial nephritis. (3) Minimal change or membranous nephropathy. (4) Papillary necrosis with long-term use.

17.5 Cystic diseases of the kidney

Renal cysts are dilated nephron segments. Hereditary forms cause renal failure and carry extrarenal lesions; a simple cyst must not be mistaken for a tumour.

Autosomal dominant (adult) polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD)

A hereditary disease in which multiple expanding cysts of both kidneys gradually destroy the parenchyma. It affects about 1 in 400–1000 people and accounts for roughly 5–10% of end-stage renal disease. Penetrance is high, but symptoms usually wait until the fourth decade.

PKD1 (16p13.3) encodes polycystin-1, a receptor-like membrane protein: about 85% of cases, more severe. PKD2 (4q21) encodes polycystin-2, a calcium channel: about 15%, slower course. Together they form a flow sensor in the primary cilium. Loss lowers cell calcium and raises cAMP, driving epithelial proliferation and fluid secretion. Cysts form only where a second somatic hit removes the normal allele; the V2 blocker tolvaptan lowers cAMP and slows cyst growth.

  • Both kidneys massively enlarged (up to 4 kg each)
  • Surface made entirely of cysts up to 3–4 cm, no intervening parenchyma visible
  • Cysts hold clear serous, turbid or red-brown haemorrhagic fluid
  • Cysts arise from any part of the nephron, even Bowman’s capsule
  • Lined by flattened or cuboidal, sometimes papillary epithelium
  • Compressed functioning nephrons, interstitial fibrosis between cysts
Clinical: flank pain and dragging heaviness, bilateral masses, haematuria, hypertension, UTI, stones; end-stage renal disease around 50 years (later with PKD2). Extrarenal: liver cysts (about 40%), berry aneurysms (subarachnoid haemorrhage), mitral valve prolapse (about a quarter), colonic diverticula.

Autosomal recessive (childhood) polycystic kidney disease (ARPKD)

Caused by mutations of PKHD1 (6p), encoding the ciliary protein fibrocystin; perinatal, neonatal, infantile and juvenile forms (severe perinatal cases: oligohydramnios, Potter sequence). Kidneys are enlarged with a smooth surface; the cut surface is sponge-like, with cuboidal-lined dilated collecting ducts running at right angles to the capsule. The liver always shows congenital hepatic fibrosis (portal fibrosis, bile-duct proliferation), bringing portal hypertension in survivors.

17.6 Acid–base balance and the ABG

Blood pH depends on the ratio HCO3− / PaCO2. The lungs adjust the denominator within minutes; the kidneys adjust the numerator over 3–5 days (reclaiming bicarbonate, excreting H+ as ammonium and titratable acid). So metabolic disorders are compensated fast, respiratory ones slowly, which is why respiratory disorders are called acute or chronic.

What the anion gap really measures. Blood is electrically neutral, but routine tests measure only Na+, Cl− and HCO3−. The “gap” is the unmeasured anions, mostly albumin. When an acid (HA) is added, H+ uses up bicarbonate and the A− stays behind as a new unmeasured anion: the gap widens (ketoacids, lactate, formate). When bicarbonate itself is lost (diarrhoea, RTA), the kidney holds on to chloride instead: the gap stays normal and the acidosis is hyperchloraemic.

ROME · MUDPILES

Respiratory Opposite (pH and PaCO2 move in opposite directions), Metabolic Equal (pH and HCO3 move together). High-AG acidosis: Methanol · Uraemia · DKA · Propylene glycol · Isoniazid, iron · Lactic acidosis · Ethylene glycol · Salicylates.

A 30-year-old woman swallowed a bottle of aspirin. pH 7.44, PaCO2 22, HCO3 15, Na 140, Cl 103. Reasoning: pH “normal”, but PaCO2 and HCO3 are both far off. AG = 140 − 118 = 22 → high-AG metabolic acidosis; expected PaCO2 = 1.5 × 15 + 8 = 30.5 (± 2), but measured 22 is lower → an added respiratory alkalosis (salicylate drives the respiratory centre). Diagnosis: mixed high-AG metabolic acidosis + respiratory alkalosis, classic of salicylate poisoning. A normal pH never excludes an acid–base disorder.

(a) Diagnosis? (b) Name the colloid-tubule change. (c) Commonest underlying cause.

Answer

(a) Chronic pyelonephritis. (b) Thyroidisation. (c) Vesicoureteral reflux with recurrent infection (reflux nephropathy).

RAPID REVISION The Night-Before Page

15 one-liners

  1. Tubulointerstitial disease = polyuria, nocturia, salt wasting, acidosis.
  2. Creatinine rises late; use eGFR. BUN:Cr > 20 = prerenal.
  3. Prerenal: FENa < 1%, urine Na < 20; ATN: FENa > 2%, Na > 40.
  4. RBC casts = GN; WBC casts = pyelonephritis/AIN; waxy = CKD.
  5. ATN is the commonest cause of AKI.
  6. Ischaemic ATN: patchy, straight PCT + TAL, tubulorrhexis.
  7. ATN phases: initiation, maintenance (high K), recovery (low K).
  8. Acute pyelonephritis: E. coli, ascending, abscesses, glomeruli spared.
  9. Chronic pyelonephritis: coarse polar scars, blunt calyces, thyroidisation.
  10. Papillary necrosis: diabetes, obstruction, sickle cell, analgesics.
  11. Drug AIN: 2 weeks, fever, rash, eosinophils, granulomas.
  12. ADPKD: PKD1 16p polycystin-1; huge kidneys, berry aneurysms.
  13. ARPKD: PKHD1 fibrocystin; smooth kidneys, hepatic fibrosis.
  14. Dialysis cysts → renal cell carcinoma.
  15. Metabolic acidosis: Winter PaCO2 = 1.5 × HCO3 + 8 ± 2; check AG.

Memory hooks

  • DOSA: papillary necrosis (Diabetes, Obstruction, Sickle, Analgesics)
  • ROME: Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal
  • MUDPILES: high anion gap acidosis
  • “Healthy but thirsty” prerenal kidney vs “sick and leaky” ATN kidney
