5.1 Bilirubin metabolism and jaundice

Jaundice (icterus) is yellow discolouration of the skin and sclerae caused by retention of bilirubin. It becomes visible when serum bilirubin exceeds about 2.0–2.5 mg/dL (normal adult level < 1.2 mg/dL). Cholestasis is the wider failure of bile flow, in which bile salts and cholesterol are retained as well as bilirubin: it adds pruritus, xanthomas, raised alkaline phosphatase and malabsorption of fat-soluble vitamins.

Bilirubin is the end product of haem breakdown; about 0.2–0.3 g is made each day. Roughly 85% comes from senescent red cells destroyed by macrophages in the spleen, liver and marrow; the rest comes from turnover of haem proteins (such as cytochrome P-450) and from red-cell precursors destroyed in the marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis).

!Haem from old red cells -> *Unconjugated bilirubin on albumin -> *Liver: conjugated by UGT1A1 -> Into bile via MRP2 -> Gut bacteria: urobilinogen -> Stool: stercobilin (brown) Gut bacteria: urobilinogen -> ~About 20% reabsorbed; trace in urine
  1. Haem oxygenase opens haem to biliverdin; biliverdin reductase turns it into unconjugated bilirubin, which is water-insoluble.
  2. In blood it is tightly bound to albumin, so it is not filtered by the glomerulus.
  3. Hepatocytes take it up across the sinusoidal membrane; in the endoplasmic reticulum UGT1A1 (bilirubin uridine diphosphate-glucuronosyltransferase) adds one or two glucuronic acids, making it water-soluble.
  4. Bilirubin glucuronides are pumped into the canaliculus by MRP2, an energy-dependent step.
  5. In the colon, bacteria deconjugate and reduce it to colourless urobilinogens. Most leave in the stool (oxidised to brown stercobilin); about 20% is reabsorbed and re-excreted by the liver, and a trace reaches the urine.

Solubility explains the urine and stools. Unconjugated bilirubin is insoluble and albumin-bound, so it never enters urine: haemolytic jaundice is “acholuric”. But free unconjugated bilirubin is lipid-soluble and can cross the immature blood–brain barrier of a newborn, causing kernicterus. Conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble and only loosely bound, so when it backs up it spills into urine (dark, tea-coloured urine). If bile cannot reach the gut, no stercobilin forms (pale, clay-coloured stools) and no urobilinogen is reabsorbed (urine urobilinogen absent).

Ask the DR for Direct bilirubin

Dubin–Johnson and Rotor raise direct (conjugated) bilirubin; Dubin–Johnson gives a Dark liver. Gilbert and Crigler–Najjar raise indirect bilirubin: Gilbert is gentle, Crigler–Najjar I is catastrophic.

Physiological jaundice of the newborn

  • Immature UGT1A1 + high red-cell turnover after birth → transient unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia
  • Appears after 24 hours, peaks around days 3–5, settles within about 2 weeks in term babies
  • Exaggerated in breast-fed babies: milk β-glucuronidases deconjugate bilirubin in the gut and it is reabsorbed

Pathological: investigate if

  • Appears in the first 24 hours (haemolytic disease: Rh, ABO, G6PD deficiency)
  • Very high or rapidly rising levels (risk of kernicterus: basal ganglia staining)
  • Conjugated or lasting > 2 weeks: neonatal cholestasis from biliary atresia or neonatal hepatitis

5.4 Viral hepatitis: the five hepatotropic viruses

“Hepatitis viruses” are the five viruses whose main target is the hepatocyte; EBV, CMV, herpes simplex and yellow fever can also inflame the liver during systemic infection. Any hepatitis virus can cause: acute asymptomatic infection; acute symptomatic hepatitis (icteric or anicteric) with recovery; chronic hepatitis (disease lasting > 6 months), with or without cirrhosis; a carrier state; or fulminant hepatitis (acute liver failure).

“Vowels hit the bowels”

Hepatitis A and E spread by the faecal–oral route and do not become chronic in healthy people. B, C and D need Blood: C is the Chronic one, and D is Dependent on B for its coat.

The immune system, not the virus, injures the liver. HBV is not directly cytopathic: hepatocytes die when CD8+ cytotoxic T cells recognise viral antigens on their surface. A vigorous T-cell response gives florid acute hepatitis but clears the virus; a weak, tolerant response lets the virus persist. That is why 90% of infected neonates (immature, tolerant immunity) become chronic carriers, but only 5–10% of adults do.

Hepatitis A

Benign and self-limited; most childhood infections are silent. Virus is shed in stool for 2–3 weeks before and about a week after jaundice appears, so spread has happened by the time the patient turns yellow. IgM anti-HAV = acute infection; IgG anti-HAV = lifelong immunity. No carrier state.

5.5 Hepatitis B and its serology

The complete virion (Dane particle) has an envelope and a core. Its genome is a small, partially double-stranded circular DNA that replicates through an RNA intermediate using a reverse transcriptase, and can integrate into host DNA.

  • HBsAg: envelope protein; made in excess and released into blood
  • HBcAg: core protein; stays inside hepatocytes (not in serum)
  • HBeAg: secreted precore protein: marker of active replication and high infectivity
  • HBx: transactivating protein; important in hepatocellular carcinoma
!HBV infection, adult -> Subclinical, recovers (about 65%) !HBV infection, adult -> Acute hepatitis (about 25%) Acute hepatitis (about 25%) -> Recovery (about 99%) Acute hepatitis (about 25%) -> *Fulminant hepatitis (about 1%) !HBV infection, adult -> Persistent infection (5–10%) Persistent infection (5–10%) -> Inactive carrier Persistent infection (5–10%) -> *Chronic hepatitis -> Cirrhosis -> !Hepatocellular carcinoma

The serological markers in time order

  1. HBsAg — appears before symptoms, peaks during the illness and disappears within 3–6 months. Persistence beyond 6 months = chronic infection.
  2. HBeAg, HBV DNA, DNA polymerase — appear soon after HBsAg; all mean active replication. Persistent HBeAg predicts chronicity.
  3. IgM anti-HBc — rises just before symptoms, with the ALT; over months it is replaced by IgG anti-HBc, which stays for life.
  4. Anti-HBe — appears as HBeAg vanishes: the infection has peaked.
  5. Anti-HBs — rises only after the disease is over, weeks to months after HBsAg has gone; persists for life and is protective. It is the only marker after vaccination.

Three serology traps. (1) A negative HBsAg does not exclude acute HBV: in the window period only IgM anti-HBc is positive. (2) Anti-HBc (any class) means infection, never vaccination; the vaccine contains only HBsAg, so a vaccinated person has anti-HBs alone. (3) HBeAg-negative is not always “safe”: precore mutants cannot make HBeAg yet replicate actively (high HBV DNA).

5.6 Hepatitis C, D and E

Hepatitis C

HCV is an enveloped RNA virus whose polymerase makes many copying errors. Its envelope proteins keep changing, so each patient carries a swarm of variants (quasispecies) that stay one step ahead of neutralising antibodies. This explains its three hallmarks: persistence (most infections become chronic), no vaccine, and possible re-infection.

  • Acute infection is usually mild or silent; many are found only when they present with chronic disease
  • Chronic HCV: episodic rises of ALT with normal spells between them
  • Anti-HCV screens but does not protect; HCV RNA proves active infection
  • About one in five develop cirrhosis over about 20 years; risk of HCC. Direct-acting antivirals now cure most patients

Extrahepatic HCV. Mixed cryoglobulinaemia with membranoproliferative GN (see Chapter 16), porphyria cutanea tarda, lichen planus, B-cell lymphoma and insulin resistance. Think HCV when a patient has purpura, arthralgia and proteinuria with low complement.

Viral hepatitis is a major public-health problem in Pakistan, which carries one of the largest hepatitis C burdens in the world. The national survey of 2007–08 found anti-HCV in about 4.8% and HBsAg in about 2.5% of the general population. The main routes are unsafe injections and reuse of syringes, unscreened blood, barbers’ razors and dental procedures. HDV co-infection is relatively common in HBV carriers in some regions. Every blood bag must be screened and every needle used once.

Source: PMRC national survey of hepatitis B and C, 2007–08.

Hepatitis D (delta)

HDV is a defective RNA virus: it can replicate only inside a cell already infected by HBV, because it borrows HBsAg as its envelope. Preventing HBV (vaccination) therefore prevents HDV.

Hepatitis E

A waterborne, faecal–oral infection that causes epidemics after contamination of drinking water and sporadic cases in South Asia. Like HAV it is self-limited and does not become chronic in healthy people (chronic HEV occurs in transplant recipients). Its notorious feature is the high mortality, up to about 20%, in pregnant women, from fulminant hepatitis.

5.7 Morphology of viral hepatitis

The time course, not the virus, decides the pattern: acute injury is scattered through the lobule; chronic inflammation is centred on portal tracts, gnaws into the parenchyma and lays down fibrosis.

Acute viral hepatitis

  • Liver normal-sized or slightly enlarged, swollen and soft; tense capsule
  • May be greenish if cholestatic
  • Severe necrosis: shrunken, soft liver (see fulminant hepatitis)
  • Ballooning degeneration: swollen hepatocytes with pale, wispy cytoplasm
  • Acidophil (Councilman) bodies: apoptotic hepatocytes, shrunken and deeply eosinophilic with pyknotic or absent nuclei
  • Lobular disarray; spotty necrosis with lymphocytes in the lobule
  • Kupffer cell hypertrophy, laden with debris; mild portal inflammation
  • Canalicular bile plugs; in severe cases bridging necrosis

Chronic viral hepatitis

  • Early: liver looks normal
  • Late: firm, finely or coarsely nodular as cirrhosis develops (Chapter 6)
  • Portal tracts expanded by lymphocytes, macrophages and plasma cells
  • Interface hepatitis (formerly piecemeal necrosis): inflammation crosses the limiting plate and kills periportal hepatocytes
  • Lobular inflammation, apoptosis; bridging necrosis when active
  • Fibrosis: portal → periportal → bridging septa → cirrhosis
HBV clue: ground-glass hepatocytes (finely granular, pale-pink cytoplasm because the ER is packed with HBsAg; confirmed by orcein stain or immunostaining); “sanded” nuclei hold HBcAg. HCV clue: dense portal lymphoid aggregates or follicles, bile-duct injury and steatosis (fatty change).

Chronic hepatitis slide in 10 seconds. Look at the portal tracts first: swollen with lymphocytes that spill into the surrounding liver cells (interface hepatitis) = chronic hepatitis. A lymphoid follicle + fat droplets = HCV; glassy pink hepatocytes = HBV; septa joining tracts = advanced stage. In acute hepatitis the tracts are quiet and ballooning and Councilman bodies are scattered through the lobule.

Fulminant hepatitis (acute liver failure)

Acute liver illness with encephalopathy and coagulopathy within 26 weeks of the initial injury, without pre-existing liver disease. Paracetamol overdose leads in Western countries; in South Asia viral hepatitis (HEV, HBV ± HDV, HAV) is a leading cause. Others: drugs, autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson disease, Amanita mushrooms, ischaemia.

  • Massive hepatic necrosis: liver shrunken (can fall to 500–700 g), soft, limp, red-brown
  • Wrinkled capsule
  • Whole lobules destroyed; collapsed reticulin framework
  • Only portal tracts remain, with a ductular reaction
  • Survivors regenerate; extensive collapse heals with coarse scars
Clinical: jaundice, encephalopathy, high INR, hypoglycaemia, cerebral oedema, renal failure, sepsis; high mortality without transplantation (see Chapter 6).

Answer

(a) Pre-hepatic (haemolytic), unconjugated. (b) Unconjugated bilirubin is not filtered (acholuric); more pigment reaches the gut, so more urobilinogen. (c) Hereditary spherocytosis, thalassaemia, G6PD deficiency.

15 one-liners

  1. Jaundice is visible above about 2–2.5 mg/dL bilirubin.
  2. Unconjugated bilirubin: albumin-bound, not in urine, causes kernicterus.
  3. UGT1A1 conjugates; MRP2 exports into bile.
  4. Haemolytic: unconjugated, acholuric, urobilinogen ↑.
  5. Hepatocellular: ALT/AST very high, mixed bilirubin.
  6. Obstructive: ALP/GGT very high, pale stools, itching, PT corrects with vitamin K.
  7. Gilbert: mild, fasting-induced, benign; Crigler–Najjar I: fatal kernicterus.
  8. Dubin–Johnson: MRP2, conjugated, black liver.
  9. A and E: faecal–oral, no chronicity; HEV kills pregnant women.
  10. HBV: DNA virus; 90% of neonates, 5–10% of adults become chronic.
  11. HBsAg > 6 months = chronic; HBeAg = infectivity.
  12. Window period: only IgM anti-HBc; vaccine gives anti-HBs only.
  13. HCV: most become chronic; quasispecies; no vaccine.
  14. Acute hepatitis: ballooning, Councilman bodies, lobular disarray.
  15. Chronic: interface hepatitis + fibrosis; HBV ground glass, HCV lymphoid aggregates.

Memory hooks

  • Vowels hit the bowels: A and E are faecal–oral
  • Ask the DR for Direct: Dubin–Johnson, Rotor = conjugated
  • C is Chronic, D is Dependent on B’s coat
  • S for Surface, S for Shield: anti-HBs protects; anti-HBc only says “infected”
