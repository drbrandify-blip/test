10.1 Classification and the big divide

Lung cancer is the most frequently diagnosed major cancer and the commonest cause of cancer death worldwide. Most patients are in their 50s to 70s; it is uncommon before 40. About 95% of primary lung tumours are carcinomas (bronchogenic carcinoma); the rest are mainly carcinoids, mesenchymal tumours and lymphomas.

Why we still say “small cell versus non-small cell”. The split is about treatment. A non-small cell carcinoma found before it has spread can be cured by surgery, and many adenocarcinomas carry a driver mutation that a tablet can switch off. A small cell carcinoma has almost always spread by the time it is found, so it gets chemotherapy and radiotherapy, not surgery: it melts with the first course, then returns.

10.2 Aetiology and pathogenesis

Tobacco and other carcinogens

  • Cigarette smoking: about 85–90% of cases. Risk rises with pack-years: about 10-fold for average and up to 60-fold for heavy smokers. Stopping lowers risk but never to baseline; passive smoking adds risk.
  • Carcinogens: polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (benzo[a]pyrene), nitrosamines, activated by CYP1A1. Only about 1 in 10 heavy smokers gets lung cancer, so susceptibility matters.
  • Asbestos: raises risk on its own; with smoking the effects multiply (synergy).
  • Radon and uranium (miners), arsenic, chromium, nickel, vinyl chloride, mustard gas, crystalline silica, ionising radiation.
  • Air pollution; scars (old infarcts, healed TB) may host a peripheral “scar carcinoma”.

About one in five Pakistani adults uses tobacco (GATS 2014: 19.1%; men 31.8%, women 5.8%). Silicosis is a documented hazard among stone-crushing workers in KP and Punjab, and silica is itself a lung carcinogen. Take a smoking and occupational history from every adult with a chest mass.

Source: Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS) Pakistan, 2014.

Stepwise molecular pathogenesis

Lung cancer develops through a stepwise accumulation of mutations. The earliest changes (loss of chromosome 3p, then TP53 mutation) are found even in normal-looking bronchial epithelium of smokers. The whole airway lining has been exposed, so smokers carry a field effect and second primary tumours are common.

~EGFR / KRAS mutation in alveolar epithelium -> AAH: atypia, ≤ 5 mm -> AIS: ≤ 3 cm, lepidic only -> MIA: ≤ 3 cm, invasion ≤ 5 mm -> *Invasive adenocarcinoma !Tobacco carcinogens (PAH, nitrosamines) -> 3p loss, TP53 mutation -> Squamous metaplasia -> Dysplasia -> Carcinoma in situ -> *Invasive squamous carcinoma

AAH = atypical adenomatous hyperplasia; AIS = adenocarcinoma in situ (old “bronchioloalveolar carcinoma”); MIA = minimally invasive adenocarcinoma. AIS and MIA are cured by resection.

10.3 Morphology of the carcinomas

Why the type predicts the site: squamous and small cell carcinomas arise in the large bronchi, where smoke particles land first and neuroendocrine cells sit, so they are central and block a bronchus early (cough, haemoptysis, collapse, pneumonia). Adenocarcinoma arises from peripheral airway and alveolar epithelium and stays silent until it reaches the pleura or spreads. All types start as small, firm, grey-white mucosal thickenings. They may grow into the bronchial lumen, infiltrate along the wall or push a bulky mass into the lung. Large tumours show necrosis, cavitation and haemorrhage, then reach the pleura, pericardium and chest wall.

Squamous cell carcinoma

  • Central (hilar) mass arising in a main, lobar or segmental bronchus
  • Grey-white, firm, gritty cut surface; may cavitate from central necrosis
  • Bronchial obstruction: distal collapse, bronchiectasis, pneumonia, abscess
  • Islands and sheets of polygonal cells with abundant pink cytoplasm and distinct borders
  • Keratin pearls, individual cell keratinisation, intercellular bridges (prickles)
  • Pleomorphic hyperchromatic nuclei, mitoses; desmoplastic stroma
  • Adjacent mucosa: squamous metaplasia, dysplasia, carcinoma in situ
  • IHC: p40/p63, CK5/6 positive; TTF-1 negative
Associations: men, heavy smokers; hypercalcaemia from PTHrP; the type that most often cavitates.

SCC lung slide in 10 seconds: look for pink, glassy, sharply outlined cells in nests, then find a whorled pink keratin pearl. At high power, tiny spines join neighbouring cells (intercellular bridges). Bronchial cartilage or respiratory mucosa nearby tells you the organ is lung.

Adenocarcinoma

  • Peripheral, usually smaller than central tumours
  • Grey-white, may be glistening with mucin; pleural puckering over it
  • Mucinous type can look like consolidation (pneumonia-like)
  • Malignant glands (acinar), papillae, micropapillae or solid sheets with mucin
  • Lepidic pattern: tumour cells creep along intact alveolar walls (AIS, MIA)
  • Mucin on PAS-diastase or mucicarmine
  • IHC: TTF-1 and napsin A positive (non-mucinous types)
Behaviour: grows more slowly than squamous carcinoma but tends to metastasise widely and early. Test every advanced adenocarcinoma for EGFR, ALK, ROS1 and other drivers.

Small cell carcinoma (SCLC)

  • Central, perihilar grey-white mass
  • Early, bulky spread to hilar and mediastinal nodes; may encase bronchi and vessels
  • Small cells (less than 3 resting lymphocytes across), round to spindle, scant cytoplasm
  • Finely granular “salt-and-pepper” chromatin; nucleoli absent or inconspicuous
  • Nuclear moulding, very high mitotic count, extensive necrosis, crush artefact
  • Basophilic DNA coating vessel walls (Azzopardi effect)
  • EM: dense-core neurosecretory granules; IHC chromogranin, synaptophysin, CD56, TTF-1
Behaviour: the most aggressive type. Staged as limited (one hemithorax, one radiation field) or extensive. Very chemosensitive but nearly always recurs; median survival is about a year even with treatment. Source of most endocrine paraneoplastic syndromes.

Large cell carcinoma is an undifferentiated malignant epithelial tumour: sheets of large cells with vesicular nuclei, prominent nucleoli and moderate cytoplasm, showing no glandular, squamous or small-cell features on light microscopy and IHC. It is a diagnosis of exclusion. Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (organoid nests, rosettes, NE markers) behaves like SCLC.

Old names in old papers. “Oat cell” carcinoma = SCLC. “Bronchioloalveolar carcinoma” is no longer used: Robbins splits it into adenocarcinoma in situ (non-mucinous) and invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma. A peripheral mass in a non-smoking woman is adenocarcinoma until proved otherwise.

10.4 Clinical course, spread and paraneoplastic syndromes

Lung cancer is silent and insidious; many tumours have spread before the first symptom. Common complaints are cough, weight loss, chest pain, dyspnoea and haemoptysis. Sometimes a metastasis (brain, bone, adrenal) is the first sign.

Pancoast (superior sulcus) tumour: an apical tumour that invades the brachial plexus (C8, T1, T2), causing severe pain along the ulnar nerve distribution and wasting of hand muscles, often with rib destruction.

Horner syndrome: invasion of the cervical sympathetic chain gives ipsilateral ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis and apparent enophthalmos. With a Pancoast tumour it forms the Pancoast syndrome.

Superior vena cava syndrome: a central tumour or nodal mass compresses the SVC: facial and arm swelling, dusky cyanosis, distended neck and chest-wall veins, headache worse on bending. SCLC is the classic cause.

Distant spread. Lymphatic spread goes to hilar, then mediastinal and supraclavicular (scalene) nodes. Blood-borne metastases favour the adrenals (more than half of cases), liver (30–50%), brain (about 20%) and bone (about 20%). Adrenal deposits rarely cause adrenal failure.

Paraneoplastic syndromes

Effects not explained by local or metastatic tumour, caused by hormones, cytokines or antibodies; they may be the first sign of an occult cancer. Most endocrine syndromes come from small cell, hypercalcaemia from squamous, clotting problems from adenocarcinoma.

Staging and prognosis. NSCLC is staged by TNM; tumours found before nodal or distant spread are resected (lobectomy or pneumonectomy) with a real chance of cure. Overall, fewer than one in five patients survive 5 years, because most present late.

10.5 Carcinoid and benign lung tumours

Bronchial carcinoid tumour

A low-grade malignant neuroendocrine tumour, 1–5% of lung tumours. Patients are usually under 40; there is no link with smoking. A few occur with MEN1. It sits at the mild end of a spectrum whose aggressive end is SCLC.

  • Main bronchus: polypoid, spherical, intraluminal mass up to 3–4 cm, covered by intact mucosa
  • Or a “collar-button” lesion: a small endobronchial knob with a larger part pushing through the wall into the lung
  • Organoid nests, trabeculae, ribbons of uniform cells
  • Regular round nuclei, salt-and-pepper chromatin, little pleomorphism, rare mitoses
  • Neurosecretory granules; chromogranin, synaptophysin positive
Clinical: cough, haemoptysis, recurrent pneumonia or collapse distal to the block. Carcinoid syndrome (flushing, diarrhoea, cyanosis) is uncommon. Typical carcinoids are cured by resection in most cases.

Pulmonary hamartoma, the commonest benign lung tumour, is a round “coin lesion” under 3–4 cm: nodules of mature hyaline cartilage with fat, fibrous tissue and clefts lined by respiratory epithelium; clonal HMGA2 (12q14–15) rearrangements make it a true neoplasm. Other solitary nodules: healed tuberculous granuloma, carcinoma, carcinoid, metastasis.

10.6 Metastatic tumours and malignant mesothelioma

Metastatic tumours of the lung

The lung is the commonest site of metastases. It receives the whole venous return and lymph through the thoracic duct, so its capillary bed is the first filter for tumour emboli from almost anywhere. Both carcinomas and sarcomas spread here.

  • Multiple discrete nodules scattered through all lobes, mostly peripheral: the “cannonball” pattern (sarcomas, renal cell carcinoma, choriocarcinoma and testicular tumours, colon, breast)
  • Lymphangitic carcinomatosis: tumour fills peribronchial and perivascular lymphatics (breast, stomach, pancreas)
  • Solitary nodule, endobronchial deposit or malignant pleural effusion
  • Lepidic spread along alveolar walls, mimicking primary adenocarcinoma

Malignant mesothelioma

Malignant mesothelioma is a rare, highly malignant tumour of the mesothelial cells lining the pleura (less often peritoneum, pericardium or tunica vaginalis), strongly linked to occupational asbestos exposure.

!Inhaled asbestos fibres (amphiboles) -> Fibres reach pleura, not cleared -> Macrophages: ROS, chronic inflammation -> BAP1, CDKN2A loss -> ~Latency 25–40 years -> *Malignant mesothelioma
  • Amphibole fibres (crocidolite: long, straight, stiff) are far more dangerous than serpentine chrysotile: they penetrate deep and stay.
  • Lifetime risk in heavily exposed workers is as high as about 7–10%.
  • Smoking does not increase the risk of mesothelioma, unlike asbestos-related lung carcinoma.
  • Germline BAP1 mutations cause familial mesothelioma (with uveal melanoma).
  • Markers of exposure: pleural plaques and asbestos (ferruginous) bodies: golden-brown beaded rods with an iron-protein coat, Prussian blue positive.
  • Starts as small pleural nodules, then spreads over the surface
  • Lung encased by a thick, yellow-white, firm, sometimes gelatinous rind that obliterates the pleural space
  • Often a pleural effusion; invasion of chest wall, fissures and diaphragm; distant spread is late
  • Epithelioid (commonest): cuboidal cells forming tubules and papillae, resembling adenocarcinoma
  • Sarcomatoid: malignant spindle cells
  • Biphasic: both patterns
  • EM: long, slender, branching microvilli
Clinical: chest pain, dyspnoea, recurrent effusions. Prognosis is grim: about half die within a year of diagnosis and few survive 2 years.
