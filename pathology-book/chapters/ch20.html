20.1 Vulva

The vulva is covered by skin, so most of its diseases behave like skin diseases: itchy white patches, warts and squamous neoplasia. A white plaque (leukoplakia) is a clinical description, not a diagnosis: it may be a dermatosis, VIN or invasive carcinoma, so it must be biopsied.

Condyloma acuminatum is a sexually transmitted wart caused by low-risk HPV 6 and 11: papillary, exophytic lesions whose surface cells show koilocytosis. It is not a precursor of cancer.

Vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN) and squamous cell carcinoma

Squamous cell carcinoma makes up about 90% of vulvar cancers, mostly in women over 60. It arises along two separate pathways that differ in age, cause and precursor. Learn them as a pair.

20.2 HPV and cervical precursor lesions

Almost every cervical carcinoma begins with a persistent high-risk HPV infection. The virus is very common in young sexually active women, but most infections are transient: about 90% are cleared within 2 years. Cancer appears only when infection persists and the viral oncogenes take over the cell cycle.

Why the transformation zone? HPV can only infect immature basal cells that it reaches through tiny breaks in the epithelium; the virus then replicates in the cells as they mature (a productive infection, seen as koilocytes). The transformation zone, where columnar epithelium is being replaced by squamous metaplasia, is full of immature metaplastic squamous cells, so it is the most vulnerable target. That is why nearly all CIN and carcinoma arise there, and why the Pap smear must sample the squamocolumnar junction.

HPV types

  • Low-risk (6, 11): condylomas, some LSIL; virus stays episomal
  • High-risk (16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58 and others): HSIL and carcinoma; HPV 16 causes almost 60% and HPV 18 about 10% of cervical cancers
  • HPV 18 is relatively more frequent in adenocarcinoma

Risk factors (help HPV persist)

  • Early first intercourse; multiple partners; male partner with many partners
  • Smoking; immunodeficiency (HIV)
  • High parity; long-term oral contraceptives; other STIs (Chlamydia)

E6 and E7, the oncoproteins. E6 binds p53 and marks it for ubiquitin-mediated destruction; it also activates TERT (telomerase), so cells become immortal. E7 binds hypophosphorylated RB, releasing E2F to drive the cell into S phase; it also inactivates the CDK inhibitors p21 and p27. Integration of the viral genome breaks the viral E2 gene, which normally restrains E6/E7, so their expression rises. With RB disabled, the cell over-produces p16INK4a: strong, diffuse (“block”) p16 staining is a surrogate marker of high-risk HPV-driven HSIL.

Screening and prevention

  • Pap smear (exfoliative cytology): cells scraped from the transformation zone, Papanicolaou-stained (or liquid-based). Dysplastic cells show nuclear enlargement, hyperchromasia, irregular membranes and a high N:C ratio.
  • HPV DNA test for high-risk types: more sensitive; used with cytology (co-testing) or as the primary screen. WHO now favours primary HPV testing from age 30.
  • Colposcopy after an abnormal result: acetic acid turns abnormal epithelium acetowhite; directed biopsy confirms the grade.
  • HSIL is excised (LEEP, cone biopsy); LSIL is usually followed up.
  • Why screening works: the precursor phase lasts years and the cervix is easy to sample. Robbins (US guidance): cytology from 21 years every 3 years; from 30 to 65, cytology plus HPV co-testing every 5 years.
  • HPV vaccines are prophylactic virus-like particles of the L1 capsid protein: bivalent (16, 18), quadrivalent (6, 11, 16, 18) and nine-valent (adds 31, 33, 45, 52, 58). Best given before sexual debut (girls 9–14 years).
  • Vaccines prevent infection but do not treat it and do not cover every oncogenic type, so vaccinated women still need screening.

Pakistan reports a lower incidence of cervical cancer than many countries, but most women have never been screened, so cases present late. Pakistan began HPV vaccination of girls aged 9–14 years in a national campaign in September 2025. For the next generation, vaccination plus once-in-a-while HPV testing could make this cancer rare.

20.3 Carcinoma of the cervix

Invasive carcinoma means the neoplastic cells have broken through the basement membrane. The peak age is about 45 years, roughly 15 years after the peak of HSIL. All the main types are HPV-related.

  • Types: squamous cell carcinoma about 80%; adenocarcinoma about 15% (precursor adenocarcinoma in situ; harder to detect on Pap smear); adenosquamous and neuroendocrine (small cell) carcinoma about 5%, the latter most aggressive.
  • Clinical: early disease is silent (found by screening); then postcoital or intermenstrual bleeding, discharge, dyspareunia; late pelvic pain, fistulae and uraemia.
  • Exophytic: fungating, cauliflower-like, friable growth that bleeds on touch
  • Ulcerative: crater replacing the cervix
  • Endophytic: infiltrates the wall, giving a hard “barrel-shaped” cervix
  • Irregular nests and tongues of malignant squamous cells invading the stroma
  • Keratinising type: keratin pearls, intercellular bridges; non-keratinising: sheets of polygonal cells
  • Desmoplastic stroma with lymphocytes; often HSIL at the edge

Spread

  • Direct: vagina, parametrium, pelvic wall; bladder in front, rectum behind
  • Parametrial spread encircles the ureters → hydronephrosis → renal failure, the commonest cause of death
  • Lymphatic: paracervical, obturator, internal and external iliac, then para-aortic nodes
  • Blood (late): liver, lungs, bone
FIGO staging (simplified)
IConfined to cervix. IA: microscopic, invasion ≤ 5 mm deep; IB: larger
IIBeyond uterus but not to pelvic wall or lower third of vagina (IIB: parametrium)
IIIPelvic wall, lower third of vagina, or hydronephrosis; IIIC: pelvic/para-aortic nodes
IVIVA: bladder or rectal mucosa; IVB: distant metastasis

Prognosis follows stage and node status: microinvasive disease is almost always curable.

A 45-year-old mother of six, never screened, has postcoital bleeding for 4 months. A friable cauliflower growth replaces the cervix; the parametrium is thick up to the pelvic wall; creatinine 3.1 mg/dL with bilateral hydronephrosis. Reasoning: invasive cervical carcinoma (biopsy: keratinising SCC); pelvic wall plus hydronephrosis = stage III; the raised creatinine is the classic killer, ureteric obstruction.

20.4 Endometritis, adenomyosis and endometriosis

Endometrium grows, bleeds and sheds every month. Misplaced endometrium still obeys the cycle, and that misplaced bleeding explains the pain, scarring and cysts of endometriosis.

Endometritis

  • Acute endometritis: bacterial infection after delivery or abortion, usually with retained products of conception; neutrophils in the stroma. Treated with curettage and antibiotics.
  • Chronic endometritis: the diagnostic clue is plasma cells in the endometrial stroma (normal endometrium has lymphocytes but no plasma cells). Causes: pelvic inflammatory disease (gonococcus, chlamydia), retained products, intrauterine device, and tuberculosis.
  • Tuberculous endometritis usually spreads from tuberculous salpingitis and is an important cause of infertility where TB is common, as in Pakistan. Granulomas are small and seldom caseate, because the endometrium is shed each month; they are best seen in a premenstrual biopsy.

Adenomyosis

Adenomyosis is the presence of endometrial glands and stroma deep within the myometrium, between the muscle bundles, conventionally more than about one low-power field (2–3 mm) below the endometrial–myometrial junction.

It results from downgrowth of the basal layer of the endometrium into the myometrium, and is found in up to a fifth of hysterectomy specimens. Because the misplaced tissue is basalis, it usually does not bleed cyclically. The surrounding smooth muscle reacts with hypertrophy, so the wall thickens.

  • Uterus diffusely enlarged and globular, wall thickened
  • Cut surface coarsely trabeculated with small haemorrhagic or cystic spaces
  • No capsule, no sharp edge (unlike a fibroid); a localised nodular form is an adenomyoma
  • Islands of endometrial glands with surrounding endometrial stroma deep in the myometrium
  • Glands usually inactive (basalis type)
  • Hypertrophied smooth muscle around the islands
Clinical: parous women in their 40s with menorrhagia, colicky dysmenorrhoea, dyspareunia and premenstrual pelvic pain; the uterus is bulky and tender.

Endometriosis

Endometriosis is the presence of endometrial glands and stroma outside the uterus. It affects up to about 10% of women of reproductive age and nearly half of infertile women.

Sites, commonest first: ovaries (often bilateral) → uterine ligaments, rectovaginal septum, pouch of Douglas → pelvic peritoneum, bowel, appendix, bladder → cervix, vagina, tubes, laparotomy scars → rarely umbilicus, lymph nodes, lungs. Theories: regurgitation (Sampson; retrograde menstruation, the leading theory for pelvic disease), benign metastasis by lymphatics or veins (explains nodes and lung), metaplasia of coelomic epithelium, and stem/progenitor cells from bone marrow or basalis.

  • Red-blue to yellow-brown “powder-burn” nodules on serosal surfaces

20.5 Endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma

Oestrogen grows the endometrium; progesterone matures it and lets it shed. In every ovulatory cycle the progesterone of the corpus luteum switches the glands to secretion and then withdrawal causes menstruation. When ovulation fails, or oestrogen comes from elsewhere, the glands keep proliferating with no brake and no shedding. Years of this unopposed oestrogen produce hyperplasia, and once mutations such as PTEN loss are added, atypical hyperplasia and endometrioid carcinoma follow.

Sources of unopposed oestrogen

  • Anovulatory cycles (perimenopause), PCOS
  • Obesity: adipose aromatase converts androstenedione to oestrone
  • Oestrogen-only hormone replacement
  • Oestrogen-secreting ovarian tumours (granulosa cell tumour)
  • Tamoxifen (weak oestrogen agonist on endometrium)

Endometrial hyperplasia (WHO two-tier)

  • Hyperplasia without atypia: increased gland-to-stroma ratio; glands of irregular size and shape, some cystically dilated; normal cytology. Low risk of carcinoma (about 1–3%).
  • Atypical hyperplasia / endometrioid intraepithelial neoplasia (EIN): crowded, back-to-back complex glands with nuclear atypia (rounded vesicular nuclei, nucleoli, loss of polarity). High risk: many already harbour carcinoma, so treatment is hysterectomy (progestins if fertility is wanted).

Endometrial carcinoma

The commonest invasive cancer of the female genital tract in high-income countries. The classic presentation is postmenopausal bleeding; every episode must be investigated (transvaginal ultrasound of endometrial thickness, then endometrial biopsy). A Pap smear is not a reliable test for it. Robbins describes two clinicopathological types.

20.6 Tumours of the myometrium

Leiomyoma (fibroid)

The commonest benign tumour in women, present in about 30–50% of women of reproductive age. It is a benign smooth-muscle tumour that depends on oestrogen: it grows in pregnancy and on oestrogen therapy and shrinks after menopause. About 70% carry MED12 mutations; others have HMGA2 rearrangements; germline fumarate hydratase (FH) mutation causes hereditary leiomyomatosis with renal cell carcinoma.

  • Sharply circumscribed, round, firm, grey-white nodules; usually multiple
  • Cut surface bulges and shows a whorled pattern
  • Sites: intramural (commonest), submucosal (bleeding), subserosal (may be pedunculated)
  • Interlacing whorled fascicles of uniform smooth-muscle cells
  • Elongated, blunt-ended “cigar-shaped” nuclei, eosinophilic cytoplasm
  • Few mitoses, no tumour necrosis, no atypia
Secondary (degenerative) changes: hyaline (commonest), cystic, myxoid, calcification (postmenopausal), and red (carneous) degeneration in pregnancy: haemorrhagic infarction with acute pain. Malignant change is extremely rare. Clinical: often silent; menorrhagia with iron-deficiency anaemia, pelvic pressure, urinary frequency, infertility, miscarriage.

Leiomyosarcoma

A malignant smooth-muscle tumour that arises de novo, not from a leiomyoma, usually in women aged 40–60. It recurs locally and metastasises by blood, mainly to the lungs; five-year survival is about 40%.

Section of uterine wall: bundles of smooth muscle with scattered islands of endometrial glands, each surrounded by cellular endometrial stroma, lying deep within the muscle far from the endometrial surface. (a) Identify. (b) Give two features. (c) Name two symptoms.

Answer

(a) Adenomyosis. (b) Glands with endometrial stroma between muscle bundles; hypertrophied smooth muscle around them. (c) Menorrhagia and dysmenorrhoea (bulky tender uterus).

Endometrial curettings: closely packed, back-to-back glands with no stroma in between, lined by stratified columnar cells with enlarged vesicular nuclei and mitoses; a few cribriform areas. (a) Diagnosis. (b) Two risk factors. (c) Which gene is most often lost?

Answer

(a) Endometrioid (type I) endometrial adenocarcinoma. (b) Obesity, unopposed oestrogen (anovulation/PCOS/HRT), diabetes, nulliparity, Lynch syndrome. (c) PTEN.

Pap smear of a 32-year-old: many small cells with scanty cytoplasm, high N:C ratio, dark irregular nuclei, singly and in clusters; mature superficial cells are few. (a) Report this smear in Bethesda terms. (b) What is the next step? (c) Which virus is responsible?

Answer

(a) HSIL (CIN II–III). (b) Colposcopy with directed biopsy, then excision (LEEP/cone). (c) High-risk HPV, most often type 16.
