16.1 How glomeruli get injured

The glomerular filter has three layers: a fenestrated endothelium, the glomerular basement membrane (GBM: type IV collagen, laminin and negatively charged heparan sulfate) and the podocytes, whose interdigitating foot processes are bridged by the slit diaphragm (nephrin, podocin). The mesangium holds the capillary tuft together. Damage any layer and the filter fails, but how it fails depends on which layer is hit.

Antibody-mediated injury (most GN)

  • In situ immune complexes: antibodies bind antigens already in the glomerulus. Intrinsic antigen: PLA2R on podocytes (membranous). Planted antigen: bacterial, DNA, drugs.
  • Circulating immune complexes trap in the glomerulus: lupus, PSGN, IgA nephropathy, HBV/HCV.
  • Anti-GBM antibodies against the α3 chain of type IV collagen: Goodpasture.

Non-antibody injury

  • Podocyte injury (toxins, cytokines, genetic: nephrin, podocin) → foot-process effacement → proteinuria. MCD and FSGS.
  • Complement dysregulation of the alternative pathway → C3 glomerulopathy.
  • Nephron loss → remaining glomeruli hyperfilter → secondary FSGS → progression of any chronic disease.

Where the deposit sits decides the syndrome. Deposits on the blood side (subendothelial, mesangial) are in contact with circulating leukocytes. They draw in neutrophils and monocytes, the tuft becomes inflamed and hypercellular, and red cells leak: a nephritic picture. Deposits on the urinary side (subepithelial) are walled off from leukocytes by the GBM. Complement (C5b–9) attacks the podocytes quietly and the filter leaks protein: a nephrotic picture. PSGN is the exception that proves the rule: it has subepithelial humps but also intense complement activation and subendothelial and mesangial deposits, so it is nephritic.

16.3 Nephrotic syndrome

All the features of nephrotic syndrome flow from one event: the filter lets large amounts of plasma protein through. Draw this chain in every answer on nephrotic syndrome; it earns the “pathogenesis” marks.

*Podocyte / GBM injury -> Increased glomerular permeability -> !Massive proteinuria > 3.5 g/day !Massive proteinuria > 3.5 g/day -> Hypoalbuminaemia -> Low plasma oncotic pressure -> *Generalised oedema Hypoalbuminaemia -> Liver makes more lipoproteins -> Hyperlipidaemia + lipiduria !Massive proteinuria > 3.5 g/day -> Loss of antithrombin III -> Hypercoagulability (renal vein thrombosis) !Massive proteinuria > 3.5 g/day -> Loss of IgG -> Infections (pneumococcal peritonitis) Low plasma oncotic pressure -> Low plasma volume -> RAAS: Na and water retention -> *Generalised oedema

Causes in children

  • Minimal change disease (about two-thirds)
  • FSGS
  • MPGN, membranous (uncommon)

Causes in adults

  • Primary: FSGS and membranous (each about one-third)
  • Secondary: diabetes mellitus (commonest systemic cause), amyloidosis, SLE

Minimal change disease (lipoid nephrosis)

The commonest cause of nephrotic syndrome in children (peak 2–6 years). Often follows a respiratory infection or immunisation. A circulating T-cell factor is thought to injure podocytes. The lesion is invisible on light microscopy, which gives the disease its name.

  • Kidneys normal or slightly swollen
  • Cortex may look pale-yellow from lipid in tubules
  • LM: glomeruli normal
  • PCT cells packed with lipid droplets (hence “lipoid nephrosis”)
  • IF: negative
  • EM: diffuse effacement of foot processes; no deposits
Clinical: selective proteinuria, no hypertension, normal renal function. > 90% of children respond to corticosteroids; relapses are common but chronic renal failure is rare. In adults it can follow NSAIDs or accompany Hodgkin lymphoma.

Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)

Focal = some glomeruli; segmental = part of each affected tuft. Podocyte injury causes scarring that starts in juxtamedullary glomeruli, so a superficial biopsy can miss it and be mislabelled MCD.

Types

  • Primary (idiopathic): circulating permeability factor; recurs after transplant
  • Secondary: HIV (collapsing variant), heroin, sickle cell disease, obesity, reflux nephropathy, nephron loss
  • Genetic: NPHS1 (nephrin), NPHS2 (podocin), APOL1 variants

Versus MCD

  • Haematuria and hypertension common
  • Non-selective proteinuria
  • Poor steroid response
  • About half progress to end-stage renal disease within 10 years
  • Normal early; small, scarred kidneys late
  • LM: segmental sclerosis with hyalinosis and lipid foam cells in some glomeruli
  • IF: IgM and C3 trapped in scars (non-specific)
  • EM: effacement of foot processes
  • Collapsing variant: tuft collapses, podocytes proliferate; worst prognosis

Membranous nephropathy

A leading cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults (30–60 years). It is a chronic immune-complex disease in which IgG and complement accumulate under the podocytes. The GBM lays down new matrix between the deposits, producing the famous spikes.

Primary (about 75%)

  • Autoantibodies to PLA2R (phospholipase A2 receptor) on podocytes in most cases; THSD7A in a few
  • Serum anti-PLA2R is used for diagnosis and follow-up

Secondary: “DIMS”

  • Drugs: penicillamine, captopril, gold, NSAIDs
  • Infections: HBV, HCV, syphilis, malaria
  • Malignancy: carcinoma of lung/colon, melanoma
  • SLE (class V), autoimmune thyroiditis
  • Enlarged, pale kidneys early
  • Look for renal vein thrombosis, which is classically linked to membranous nephropathy
  • LM: diffuse uniform thickening of capillary walls, no hypercellularity
  • Silver stain: “spikes” of GBM between deposits
  • IF: granular IgG and C3 along GBM
  • EM: subepithelial deposits (“spike and dome”)
Course: unpredictable. About a third remit, a third persist, and roughly 40% progress to renal failure over years. Poorly steroid-responsive; immunosuppression is used for high-risk patients.

Membranoproliferative GN (MPGN) and C3 glomerulopathy

MPGN gives a mixed nephritic–nephrotic picture. The GBM is thickened (membrano-) and the tuft is hypercellular (proliferative). Mesangial cells grow into the capillary wall and lay down new basement membrane, so silver stain shows a double contour (“tram-track”).

16.4 Nephritic syndrome

Acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN)

PSGN is an immune-complex glomerulonephritis that follows infection with nephritogenic strains of group A β-haemolytic streptococci, typically 1–4 weeks after pharyngitis or skin infection (impetigo), and presents as acute nephritic syndrome.

!Group A strep: throat or skin -> Latent period 1–4 weeks -> Antibodies to strep antigens (SpeB) Antibodies to strep antigens (SpeB) -> Immune complexes in GBM (in situ and circulating) -> *Complement activation (low C3) *Complement activation (low C3) -> Neutrophils + monocytes; cell proliferation -> Low GFR, RBC leak -> !Nephritic syndrome

Clinical picture

  • Child of 6–10 years; sudden onset after sore throat or impetigo
  • Smoky / cola-coloured urine, RBC casts
  • Periorbital oedema, oliguria, mild hypertension
  • Raised ASO titre (throat) or anti-DNase B (skin); low serum C3 that normalises in 6–8 weeks
  • > 95% of children recover fully; adults do worse (RPGN or chronic disease in a minority)
  • Kidneys symmetrically enlarged and pale
  • Petechial haemorrhages may dot the cortex
  • LM: diffuse enlargement and hypercellularity of glomeruli: proliferating endothelial and mesangial cells plus neutrophils and monocytes; occasional crescents
  • IF: granular IgG, IgM and C3 (“starry sky”)
  • EM: subepithelial humps

PSGN = “4 Ps + 1 C”

Pharynx/skin infection first · Periorbital puffiness · Pee is cola-coloured · Pressure (BP) up · C3 down

IgA nephropathy (Berger disease)

The commonest glomerulonephritis worldwide on biopsy. Children and young adults get visible haematuria within 1–2 days of a respiratory or GI infection (synpharyngitic), recurring every few months. A defect in IgA1 glycosylation (galactose-deficient IgA1) makes it immunogenic. Autoantibodies form complexes that lodge in the mesangium and activate the alternative and lectin complement pathways.

  • LM: normal to mesangial proliferation
  • IF: mesangial IgA (often with C3): diagnostic
  • EM: mesangial deposits
  • Secondary IgA nephropathy: coeliac disease, chronic liver disease/cirrhosis (poor hepatic clearance of IgA)
  • IgA vasculitis (Henoch–Schönlein purpura): the systemic form, with purpura on buttocks and legs, arthritis and abdominal pain
  • Serum C3 normal; slow progression to chronic renal failure in about a quarter to a half over 20 years

PSGN vs IgA nephropathy. Both follow a sore throat. The interval separates them: PSGN appears 1–4 weeks later with low C3; IgA haematuria appears within 1–2 days with normal C3 and recurs.

Hereditary nephritis: Alport syndrome

Mutation of type IV collagen, usually X-linked (COL4A5), so boys are worst affected. The triad is haematuria progressing to renal failure, sensorineural deafness and eye lesions (anterior lenticonus, lens dislocation, cataract). EM shows a thin, then irregularly thickened and split, laminated (“basket-weave”) GBM. Thin basement membrane disease (heterozygous COL4A3/4) causes benign familial haematuria.

16.5 Rapidly progressive (crescentic) GN

RPGN is a clinical syndrome: a nephritic picture with severe oliguria and a fall of GFR over days to weeks. The common microscopic lesion is the crescent: severe injury ruptures the capillary wall and fibrin leaks into Bowman’s space. Parietal epithelial cells proliferate and monocytes/macrophages migrate in, forming a crescent that compresses the tuft and later scars.

16.6 Chronic glomerulonephritis

The common end point of many glomerular diseases: RPGN (most cases), FSGS, MPGN, membranous and IgA nephropathy. PSGN in children rarely ends here. Patients present with hypertension, anaemia and uraemia, often without any history of renal disease.

  • Kidneys symmetrically contracted
  • Diffusely granular cortical surface; thin cortex
  • Increased peripelvic fat
  • Obliterated, hyalinised glomeruli (“glomerular ghosts”)
  • Tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis, lymphocytic infiltrate
  • Thick-walled arteries and arterioles from hypertension
Uraemic complications: fibrinous pericarditis, uraemic gastroenteritis, secondary hyperparathyroidism with renal osteodystrophy, normocytic anaemia (low erythropoietin), uraemic pneumonitis.

Glomerulus under the microscope in 10 seconds: (1) Count cells in the tuft. More than 3 nuclei per mesangial area, or neutrophils, means proliferative GN (PSGN). (2) Look at the capillary walls. Thick, rigid walls point to membranous; tram-tracks point to MPGN; wire loops point to lupus. (3) Look in Bowman’s space for a crescent (RPGN). (4) A tuft that looks entirely normal in a nephrotic child is MCD.

A 58-year-old man has anasarca for 3 months. Urine protein 6 g/day, albumin 2.1 g/dL, cholesterol high, no RBC casts, BP normal. Biopsy: thick capillary walls, silver spikes, granular IgG along GBM.

Reasoning: nephrotic range + adult + thick walls without proliferation + spikes → membranous nephropathy. Next steps: anti-PLA2R antibody, and screen for the secondary causes in “DIMS”. At his age, look carefully for a hidden carcinoma.
