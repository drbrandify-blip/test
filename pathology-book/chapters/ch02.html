2.1 Mucosal defence, acute gastritis and stress ulcers

Every gastric lesion in this chapter is a lost balance. Acid (pH about 1) and pepsin are kept off the epithelium by a surface layer of mucus into which the cells pour bicarbonate; tight junctions and rapid restitution seal gaps; a rich blood flow carries away acid and brings oxygen; and prostaglandins (PGE2, PGI2) drive mucus, bicarbonate and blood flow. Anything that weakens defence or increases attack causes gastritis or ulcer.

Defences weakened by

  • NSAIDs, aspirin: block COX, so prostaglandins fall
  • Ischaemia, shock, hypoxia (high altitude), ageing
  • Alcohol, smoking, chemotherapy, radiation
  • Uraemia: ammonia inhibits bicarbonate transport

Attack increased by

  • H. pylori (antral infection → acid hypersecretion)
  • Gastrinoma (Zollinger–Ellison syndrome)
  • Vagal overdrive after head injury (Cushing ulcer)
  • Bile reflux, swallowed acids or alkalis

Acute gastritis

A transient inflammation of the gastric mucosa. It may be silent or cause epigastric pain, nausea and vomiting; severe cases erode and bleed, giving haematemesis or melaena.

  • Mild: oedema and redness
  • Severe: multiple erosions with dark punctate haemorrhages (acute erosive haemorrhagic gastritis)
  • Oedema, congestion; neutrophils above the basement membrane in contact with epithelium = active inflammation
  • Erosion: loss of surface epithelium not crossing the muscularis mucosae, with fibrin and neutrophils

Stress-related mucosal disease (acute stress ulcers)

Seen in critically ill patients: shock, sepsis, major trauma or surgery, burns and intracranial injury. The main cause is local ischaemia from hypotension and splanchnic vasoconstriction; systemic acidosis and low prostaglandins add to it.

Curling ulcer: in the proximal duodenum after severe burns or trauma.

Cushing ulcer: in the stomach, duodenum or oesophagus with intracranial disease (head injury, tumour, surgery). Direct vagal stimulation causes acid hypersecretion; perforation is common.

Curling = Cooking (burns) · Cushing = Cranium

Burns → duodenum; brain → acid hypersecretion and perforation.

2.2 Chronic gastritis

Chronic mucosal inflammation that, over years, leads to atrophy and intestinal metaplasia, usually without erosions. H. pylori is by far the commonest cause; autoimmune gastritis accounts for under 10%. Rarer causes: radiation, chronic bile reflux, Crohn disease, graft-versus-host disease.

H. pylori gastritis

Helicobacter pylori is a curved, flagellated, Gram-negative rod that lives in the mucus layer over the antral epithelium. It is acquired in childhood, spreads by the faeco-oral and oral–oral routes, and thrives where there is crowding, poverty and poor sanitation. Untreated, it persists for life.

Flagella swim through mucus · Urease splits urea to ammonia, buffering acid around the bacterium (the basis of the urea breath test) · Adhesins (BabA) bind epithelial blood-group antigens · Toxins: CagA (injected into epithelial cells, pro-proliferative) and VacA. CagA-positive strains carry the higher risk of ulcer and cancer.

!H. pylori infection in childhood -> Antral-predominant gastritis -> High acid output -> *Duodenal ulcer !H. pylori infection in childhood -> Pangastritis, multifocal atrophy -> Low acid, intestinal metaplasia -> *Intestinal-type adenocarcinoma !H. pylori infection in childhood -> Lymphoid follicles (acquired MALT) -> *MALT lymphoma
  • Antrum reddened, coarse, nodular (lymphoid follicles)
  • Long-standing: thin, flat mucosa (atrophy)
  • Curved rods in the surface mucus over the epithelium and in the pits (H&E; better with Giemsa, Warthin–Starry or immunostain)
  • Neutrophils within the epithelium and in pit lumens (pit abscesses)
  • Many plasma cells, lymphocytes, macrophages in superficial lamina propria
  • Lymphoid aggregates with germinal centres
  • Late: atrophy and intestinal metaplasia (goblet cells)
Tests: non-invasive: urea breath test, stool antigen, serology (shows exposure, not current infection). Invasive (endoscopic biopsy): histology, rapid urease (CLO) test, culture. Treatment: a proton-pump inhibitor plus two antibiotics; recurrence is usually reinfection.

H. pylori gastritis slide in 10 seconds: (1) Antral mucosa with a plasma-cell-rich lamina propria. (2) Neutrophils crawling into the pit epithelium. (3) Look at the surface mucus at high power for slender curved or S-shaped rods. (4) A lymphoid follicle with a germinal centre clinches chronic H. pylori gastritis.

Autoimmune gastritis

T cells (CD4+) destroy the parietal cells of the body and fundus, sparing the antrum. Antibodies to parietal cells (H+/K+-ATPase) and intrinsic factor are markers; the IF antibodies also block B12 binding. Patients are older (median about 60), slightly more often women, and often have other autoimmune diseases.

The letters mislead: type A (autoimmune) sits in the body, type B (bacterial) in the antrum. And acid goes up in antral H. pylori gastritis but down in autoimmune gastritis, so gastrin is high only in the latter.

H. pylori infection is acquired in childhood and is highly prevalent in Pakistan, as in other developing countries where crowding and unsafe water favour spread. About 19% of adults use tobacco in some form, and smoking delays ulcer healing. Treating H. pylori, not just the acid, is what stops ulcers from coming back.

Source: Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS) Pakistan, 2014.

2.3 Peptic ulcer disease

Peptic ulcer is a chronic, usually solitary breach of the mucosa that extends through the muscularis mucosae into the submucosa or deeper, in any part of the gut exposed to acid-pepsin. Most are caused by H. pylori or NSAIDs.

  • Sites: first part of duodenum (anterior wall, within a few cm of the pylorus) about 4× commoner than stomach (lesser curvature, body–antrum junction); also lower oesophagus (GERD), gastro-jejunostomy margins, Meckel diverticulum
  • Frequency: lifetime risk about 10% in men and 4% in women. Most ulcer patients carry H. pylori, yet only a small minority of infected people ever develop an ulcer
!H. pylori antral gastritis -> Less somatostatin, more gastrin -> *Acid hypersecretion -> Gastric metaplasia in duodenum -> H. pylori colonises duodenum -> !Duodenal ulcer !NSAIDs -> COX blocked: fewer prostaglandins -> Less mucus, HCO3, blood flow -> !Gastric ulcer ~Gastrinoma (Zollinger–Ellison) -> *Acid hypersecretion ~Smoking, steroids -> Less mucus, HCO3, blood flow

Other associations: cirrhosis, COPD, chronic kidney disease and hyperparathyroidism (calcium stimulates gastrin). Zollinger–Ellison syndrome (gastrinoma, often with MEN1) gives multiple, refractory ulcers in unusual sites such as the jejunum, with diarrhoea.

Morphology of a chronic peptic ulcer

  • Round to oval, sharply punched-out defect with straight walls
  • Margins level with the surrounding mucosa (heaped-up margins suggest cancer)
  • Base smooth and clean (exudate digested); a thrombosed or open vessel may be visible
  • Mucosal folds radiate from the crater like wheel spokes
  • Depth limited by muscularis, or by adherent pancreas, omentum or liver
  • Base in four zones (Fig 2.4): necrotic fibrinoid debris → neutrophilic exudate → granulation tissue → fibrous scar
  • Scar replaces the muscularis propria; vessels in it are thick-walled, sometimes thrombosed
  • Surrounding mucosa: chronic (H. pylori) gastritis

Clinical features

  • Epigastric burning or gnawing pain 1–3 hours after meals, worse at night, relieved by alkali or food
  • Nausea, vomiting, bloating, belching, weight loss
  • Some present first with iron-deficiency anaemia, bleeding or perforation

Bleed, Burst, Block, Burrow

The four complications of a peptic ulcer, in order of frequency of the first three, plus penetration into neighbouring organs.

2.5 Gastric adenocarcinoma

Adenocarcinoma makes up over 90% of gastric cancers. Incidence is highest in Japan, Korea, Chile and Eastern Europe, and has fallen sharply in countries where H. pylori has declined and refrigeration replaced salting and smoking of food. Men are affected more than women.

Risk factors

  • Infection: H. pylori (chronic gastritis → atrophy → metaplasia); EBV (about 10% of cases)
  • Diet: nitrites/nitrosamines in smoked, salted and pickled foods; lack of fresh fruit and vegetables
  • Host: autoimmune gastritis, partial gastrectomy, gastric adenoma, smoking
  • Genetic: germline CDH1 (hereditary diffuse gastric cancer), FAP, Lynch syndrome

Diffuse type: loss of CDH1 (E-cadherin), the glue that holds cells together, so cells infiltrate singly; germline in familial cases, somatic in about half of sporadic ones. Intestinal type: Wnt pathway (APC/β-catenin), microsatellite instability, TP53. EBV-positive tumours: PIK3CA mutation, heavy DNA methylation. ERBB2 (HER2) amplification in a subset → trastuzumab.
