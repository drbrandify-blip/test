11.1 Anaemia: definition, effects and classification

Anaemia is a reduction in the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood, usually because the total red cell mass has fallen below normal. In practice it is diagnosed when the haemoglobin (or haematocrit) is below the reference range for age and sex. WHO cut-offs: Hb < 13 g/dL in men, < 12 g/dL in non-pregnant women, < 11 g/dL in pregnancy and in children aged 6 months to 5 years.

Why patients feel what they feel

  • Tissue hypoxia: pallor, fatigue, weakness, dyspnoea and palpitations on exertion, headache, faintness; angina in those with coronary disease.
  • Compensation: raised cardiac output (flow murmurs, high-output failure if severe), more 2,3-BPG so Hb releases O2 more easily, and renal erythropoietin drives marrow erythroid hyperplasia.
  • Severe chronic hypoxia: fatty change of liver, kidney and heart (“tigered” myocardium).

Blood loss anaemia

  • Acute: the danger is hypovolaemia and shock, not anaemia. The Hb is normal at first and falls over 2–3 days as tissue fluid dilutes the blood. Reticulocytes rise to about 10–15% by day 7.
  • Chronic: small repeated losses deplete iron, so it presents as iron deficiency anaemia.

11.2 Reading the blood: indices, reticulocytes and the smear

Two numbers do most of the work. The MCV tells you what kind of factory fault exists: too little haemoglobin to fill the cell (small, pale cells) or too little DNA synthesis to let the cell divide on time (big cells). The reticulocyte count tells you whether the marrow is answering: high in bleeding and haemolysis (the factory works overtime), low when the factory itself is short of raw materials or damaged.

11.3 Iron metabolism and iron deficiency anaemia

Iron balance and hepcidin

The body holds about 2.5 g of iron in women and 3.5 g in men. Roughly 80% is functional (haemoglobin, myoglobin, cytochromes, catalase); the rest is stored as ferritin and haemosiderin in liver, spleen and marrow macrophages. There is no regulated route of excretion: we lose only 1–2 mg a day in shed skin and gut cells (more with menstruation). Iron balance is therefore controlled entirely at the point of absorption, in the duodenum. Haem iron (meat) is absorbed far better than non-haem iron (cereals, vegetables).

Dietary Fe3+ -> Enterocyte: Dcytb, DMT1 -> *Ferroportin: iron exit (gut cells, macrophages) -> Plasma transferrin (about 33% saturated) -> Marrow: haemoglobin -> Old red cells eaten by macrophages -> *Ferroportin: iron exit (gut cells, macrophages) Plasma transferrin (about 33% saturated) -> Stores: ferritin, haemosiderin ~IL-6 (inflammation), iron excess -> !Hepcidin from liver degrades ferroportin -> *Ferroportin: iron exit (gut cells, macrophages)

Hepcidin is the iron thermostat. When iron is plentiful or infection is present, the liver releases hepcidin, which binds ferroportin and causes its destruction: absorption stops and macrophages hold on to their iron. When iron is short, or the marrow is working hard, hepcidin falls and the doors open. Too little hepcidin → iron overload (haemochromatosis, Chapter 6; thalassaemia, Chapter 12). Too much hepcidin → anaemia of chronic disease.

Iron deficiency anaemia (IDA)

Iron deficiency is the commonest nutritional disorder in the world and the commonest cause of anaemia. It develops whenever iron loss or need outruns intake and absorption.

  • Low intake: infants on prolonged milk feeds, the poor, the elderly, strict vegetarians.
  • Poor absorption: coeliac disease, gastrectomy, achlorhydria, chronic diarrhoea.
  • Increased need: growing infants and children, adolescents, pregnancy and lactation.
  • Chronic blood loss (the commonest cause in adults): menorrhagia; GI bleeding from peptic ulcer, NSAIDs, piles, colorectal carcinoma; hookworm infestation in developing countries.

In an adult man or post-menopausal woman, iron deficiency means GI blood loss until proved otherwise: look for an occult colonic or gastric cancer.

Anaemia is very common in Pakistan: the National Nutrition Survey 2018 found it in about half of children under five (54%) and about two-fifths of women of reproductive age (42%). Iron deficiency is the main cause, from diets low in haem iron, repeated pregnancies, worm infestation and menstrual loss. Dietary B12 deficiency is seen in vegetarians and folate deficiency in pregnancy.

Source: National Nutrition Survey Pakistan, 2018.

11.4 Anaemia of chronic disease (inflammation)

The commonest anaemia in hospitalised patients. It accompanies chronic infections (tuberculosis, osteomyelitis, lung abscess, infective endocarditis), chronic immune disorders (rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease) and cancers (Hodgkin lymphoma, carcinoma of lung and breast). It is mild to moderate and does not progress beyond a certain level.

Pathogenesis

  1. IL-6 from inflammation stimulates the liver to make hepcidin.
  2. Hepcidin degrades ferroportin: iron is trapped in macrophages and gut absorption falls. Serum iron drops although stores are full.
  3. Erythropoietin output is inappropriately low, and IL-1, TNF and IFN-γ suppress red cell progenitors.

Why hide iron? Bacteria need iron to grow. Locking it inside macrophages starves invading microbes, a defence called nutritional immunity. The price is an iron-starved marrow despite plentiful stores.

Labs: normocytic normochromic (sometimes mildly microcytic) cells; low serum iron, low TIBC, normal or high ferritin; increased iron in marrow macrophages. Treat the underlying disease; erythropoietin helps some patients.

Ferritin is an acute-phase protein. Infection, inflammation, liver disease and cancer raise it, so a “normal” ferritin in a sick patient does not exclude iron deficiency; a low ferritin, however, always means low stores. Do not give long-term iron to a thalassaemia carrier: the Mentzer index and Hb electrophoresis prevent this common error (see Chapter 12).

11.5 Megaloblastic anaemias

The two causes are vitamin B12 (cobalamin) deficiency and folate deficiency. Both vitamins are needed to make thymidine for DNA.

The nucleus lags behind the cytoplasm. Without enough DNA precursors, cells cannot complete S phase on time, but RNA and protein synthesis carry on. The cytoplasm keeps growing and fills with haemoglobin while the nucleus stays large and immature: nuclear–cytoplasmic asynchrony. The result is giant precursors (megaloblasts), many of which die in the marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis: raised LDH and indirect bilirubin). Every dividing cell line is hit, so there is pancytopenia, and rapidly dividing epithelium suffers too (sore, smooth tongue).

Vitamin B12 absorption

  1. Stomach — pepsin frees B12 from food; it binds haptocorrin (R-binder) from saliva
  2. Duodenum — pancreatic proteases release B12, which binds intrinsic factor from parietal cells
  3. Terminal ileum — the IF–B12 complex is taken up by the cubilin receptor; transcobalamin II carries B12 to liver and marrow

Pernicious anaemia

Pernicious anaemia is a megaloblastic anaemia caused by an autoimmune atrophic gastritis that destroys parietal cells, so there is no intrinsic factor and dietary B12 cannot be absorbed. It is the commonest cause of B12 deficiency.

!Autoreactive T cells against parietal cells -> Atrophic gastritis of fundus and body -> No acid, no intrinsic factor -> *B12 not absorbed in ileum *B12 not absorbed in ileum -> Megaloblastic anaemia *B12 not absorbed in ileum -> Subacute combined degeneration

Autoantibodies

  • Type I (blocking): stop B12 binding to IF; in about 75%; highly specific.
  • Type II (binding): stop the IF–B12 complex binding its ileal receptor.
  • Type III: against the parietal cell proton pump (H⁺,K⁺-ATPase); in 85–90%, less specific.

Associated autoimmune diseases: Hashimoto thyroiditis, Addison disease, type 1 diabetes. Usually older adults (median about 60 years).

  • Thin, flat mucosa of fundus and body (atrophic gastritis); antrum spared
  • Smooth, red, shiny “beefy” tongue (atrophic glossitis)
  • Loss of parietal and chief cells; lymphocytes and plasma cells in mucosa
  • Intestinal metaplasia; megaloblastic change in epithelial cells
  • Spinal cord: demyelination of dorsal and lateral columns
Clinical: insidious anaemia with mild jaundice (“lemon-yellow” pallor), glossitis, and neurological signs: symmetric tingling and numbness of feet and hands, loss of vibration and position sense, ataxia. Nerve damage can occur without anaemia and may not reverse. Risk of gastric carcinoma and carcinoid tumour is raised (Chapter 2). Diagnosis: low serum B12, raised MMA and homocysteine, anti-IF antibodies, and a brisk reticulocyte response within days of parenteral B12.

Never treat a macrocytic anaemia with folate alone until B12 deficiency is excluded. Folate bypasses the methyl-folate trap and corrects the blood picture, but the neurological damage progresses. Also remember that not every macrocytosis is megaloblastic: alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, a high reticulocyte count and MDS raise the MCV without megaloblasts.

B12 = “Brain and Blood”; Folate = “Foetus”

B12 lack damages brain/cord and blood (MMA up). Folate lack damages blood and the foetal neural tube (MMA normal). Both raise homocysteine.

11.6 Aplastic and myelophthisic anaemia

Aplastic anaemia is a failure of multipotent myeloid stem cells, causing pancytopenia (anaemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia) with a hypocellular, fat-filled marrow, in the absence of marrow infiltration or fibrosis.

Causes

  • Idiopathic: more than half of all cases.
  • Drugs and chemicals: dose-related (alkylating agents, antimetabolites, benzene) or idiosyncratic (chloramphenicol, gold salts, carbamazepine, phenylbutazone).
  • Whole-body radiation.
  • Viruses: seronegative (non-A to E) hepatitis, EBV, CMV, HIV.
  • Inherited: Fanconi anaemia (defective DNA repair; short stature, absent thumbs or radii, renal anomalies); telomerase gene defects.
  • Associated: PNH may emerge from an aplastic marrow (Chapter 12).

  • Markedly hypocellular: more than 90% of the space is fat
  • Scattered lymphocytes and plasma cells; few or no haematopoietic cells
  • Pancytopenia; red cells normocytic or slightly macrocytic; reticulocytopenia
  • No blasts, no abnormal cells
  • Secondary changes: fatty liver, haemorrhages, infections
Clinical: any age; insidious weakness and pallor (anaemia), petechiae and bruising (thrombocytopenia), and recurrent infections, fever and mouth ulcers (neutropenia). Splenomegaly is characteristically absent; if the spleen is big, look for another diagnosis (leukaemia, lymphoma, myelofibrosis, hypersplenism).

Pure red cell aplasia affects only the erythroid line: parvovirus B19 infects red cell progenitors (a transient “aplastic crisis” in haemolytic anaemias), and an acquired form accompanies thymoma and large granular lymphocytic leukaemia.

Myelophthisic anaemia

Here the marrow is not empty but replaced by a space-occupying lesion: metastatic carcinoma (breast, lung, prostate), myeloma, lymphoma, leukaemia, granulomas (tuberculosis), myelofibrosis or storage diseases. The distorted marrow architecture lets immature cells escape early, giving a leucoerythroblastic picture.

(a) Anaemia of chronic disease (inflammation). (b) Stores are full; iron is trapped in macrophages, and ferritin is an acute-phase protein. (c) Hepcidin, induced by IL-6.

Bone marrow trephine from a young man with pancytopenia: bony trabeculae with marrow spaces almost entirely filled by fat cells; only a few lymphocytes and plasma cells; no tumour cells or fibrosis. (a) Diagnosis. (b) Two causes. (c) One clinical sign that should be absent.

Answer

(a) Aplastic anaemia. (b) Idiopathic; drugs (chloramphenicol), benzene, radiation, viral hepatitis, Fanconi anaemia. (c) Splenomegaly.
