30.1 General features of CNS tumours

Intracranial tumours arise in roughly 10–17 per 100,000 people a year (half to three-quarters primary) and make up as many as 20% of childhood cancers.

Four rules that make brain tumours different. (1) Benign vs malignant is blurred: even low-grade gliomas infiltrate widely, cannot be fully removed and slowly progress. (2) Location decides outcome: a WHO grade 1 tumour in the brainstem can kill, while a convexity meningioma is cured by surgery. (3) They almost never spread outside the CNS (no lymphatics), but some seed through the CSF to the spinal cord and cauda equina (“drop metastases”: medulloblastoma, ependymoma). (4) Symptoms come from raised ICP (morning headache, vomiting, papilloedema), seizures and focal deficits; posterior fossa tumours block CSF and cause hydrocephalus early.

WHO grading (grade 1–4)

  • 1: low proliferation, often cured by resection (pilocytic astrocytoma, most meningiomas, schwannoma)
  • 2: infiltrative, few mitoses, recur and progress (diffuse astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma)
  • 3: anaplasia, brisk mitoses (anaplastic astrocytoma)
  • 4: mitoses, necrosis, rapidly fatal (glioblastoma, medulloblastoma)

Robbins vs the newest WHO book. Robbins (10th ed) uses grades I–IV and the 2016 WHO scheme; the 2021 WHO (CNS5) writes grades 1–4 and gives an integrated diagnosis (histology + molecular). It reserves “glioblastoma” for IDH-wildtype tumours; the IDH-mutant grade 4 tumour is now “astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, grade 4” (old “secondary GBM”). Either answer earns marks if you name the IDH status.

Children fall backwards, adults look up

Child, posterior fossa: P-M-E: Pilocytic (cystic, lateral), Medulloblastoma (solid, midline), Ependymoma (fourth ventricle floor). Adult, above the tentorium: G-M-M: Glioblastoma, Metastasis, Meningioma.

30.2 Diffuse gliomas: astrocytoma, glioblastoma, oligodendroglioma

Gliomas arise from glial cells (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, ependymal cells). Diffuse (infiltrating) astrocytomas make up about 80% of adult primary brain tumours, usually in the cerebral hemispheres of people in their 4th to 6th decades, presenting with seizures, headache or focal deficits. They form a spectrum: diffuse astrocytoma (grade 2) → anaplastic astrocytoma (grade 3) → glioblastoma (grade 4).

Why IDH matters. Mutant IDH1/2 makes the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate, which blocks DNA and histone demethylases; the hypermethylated cells cannot differentiate. IDH-mutant gliomas strike younger adults and do better than IDH-wildtype tumours of the same grade.

Astrocytoma: IDH + TP53 + ATRX loss. Oligodendroglioma: IDH + 1p/19q codeletion. GBM, IDH-wildtype (about 90% of GBMs): EGFR amplification, PTEN loss, TERT promoter mutation, CDKN2A deletion. MGMT promoter methylation predicts temozolomide response.

Diffuse astrocytoma (grade 2–3)

  • Poorly defined, grey, infiltrative mass that expands and distorts the brain; firm or gelatinous, may be cystic
  • More glial nuclei with atypia in a fibrillary GFAP+ background; no clear edge with normal brain
  • Anaplastic (grade 3): denser cells, more pleomorphism, mitoses

Glioblastoma (GBM, grade 4)

The commonest and most malignant primary brain tumour of adults. It may cross the corpus callosum into both hemispheres (“butterfly glioma”). With surgery, radiotherapy and temozolomide, median survival is only about 15 months.

!Rapid growth outstrips blood supply -> Vessel thrombosis, hypoxia -> *Central necrosis; cells flee outward = pseudopalisades Vessel thrombosis, hypoxia -> HIF-1α: VEGF secreted -> *Microvascular (glomeruloid) proliferation
  • Variegated cut surface: firm white areas, soft yellow necrosis, haemorrhage, cysts
  • Ill-defined edge; may cross the corpus callosum
  • Highly cellular, pleomorphic astrocytic tumour with many mitoses
  • Pseudopalisading necrosis: tumour nuclei line up around serpiginous necrotic zones
  • Microvascular proliferation: tufts of proliferating endothelial cells (glomeruloid)
  • Either hallmark makes a diffuse astrocytoma grade 4

Oligodendroglioma (grade 2–3)

About 5–15% of gliomas, mostly in the 4th and 5th decades, in the white matter of the frontal and temporal lobes; often a long history of seizures. Defined by IDH mutation + 1p/19q codeletion, which also predicts sensitivity to chemotherapy and a better prognosis than astrocytomas of the same grade.

  • Well-circumscribed, gelatinous, grey mass
  • Cysts, focal haemorrhage and calcification (visible on imaging)
  • Sheets of uniform round cells with finely granular chromatin
  • Perinuclear clear halo: “fried-egg” cells (a fixation artefact)
  • Delicate branching capillaries: “chicken-wire” pattern
  • Microcalcifications; grade 3 adds mitoses, necrosis, vascular proliferation

30.3 Circumscribed gliomas: pilocytic astrocytoma and ependymoma

Pilocytic astrocytoma (grade 1)

A slow-growing, well-demarcated tumour of children and young adults, usually in the cerebellum; also the third ventricle floor, hypothalamus and optic nerves (NF1). Driven by BRAF activation, most often a KIAA1549–BRAF fusion (tandem duplication), less often BRAF V600E; no IDH mutation. Resection is often curative.

  • Classic: cyst with a mural nodule
  • Otherwise a well-circumscribed solid mass
  • Bipolar cells with long, thin, hair-like (“piloid”) processes, GFAP-positive
  • Compact areas alternating with loose microcystic areas
  • Rosenthal fibres (bright eosinophilic, corkscrew-shaped) and eosinophilic granular bodies
  • Hyalinised vessels; mitoses and necrosis rare

Ependymoma

Arises next to the ependyma-lined ventricles and the central canal. In the first two decades it is usually in the fourth ventricle, where it blocks CSF flow and causes hydrocephalus; in adults it favours the spinal cord (linked to NF2). Supratentorial tumours carry a ZFTA (C11orf95)–RELA fusion. Posterior fossa ependymomas do worse because complete removal is difficult; CSF spread can occur.

  • Solid or papillary mass arising from the floor of the fourth ventricle
  • Spinal cord: well-demarcated intramedullary mass
  • Myxopapillary ependymoma: filum terminale of adults
  • Uniform round–oval nuclei with granular chromatin; dense fibrillary background
  • Perivascular pseudorosettes (common): cells around a vessel with a nucleus-free zone of processes
  • True ependymal rosettes (canals with a central lumen): diagnostic but fewer
  • GFAP-positive

30.4 Medulloblastoma

Medulloblastoma is a highly malignant embryonal tumour of the cerebellum, mainly of children (about 20% of childhood brain tumours). It is midline (vermis) in children and more often lateral in adults. The cells are primitive but may show neuronal differentiation (synaptophysin-positive).

  • Well-circumscribed, grey, friable midline mass
  • May reach the folial surface and seed the leptomeninges; compresses the fourth ventricle
  • Very cellular sheets of small round blue cells: scant cytoplasm, hyperchromatic, often crescent-shaped nuclei, many mitoses
  • Homer Wright rosettes: tumour cells around a central core of pink fibrillary neuropil (no lumen)
  • Desmoplastic/nodular pattern in the SHH group
Behaviour: spreads through CSF (drop metastases to cord and cauda equina) but is exquisitely radiosensitive; with excision, craniospinal radiation and chemotherapy, 5-year survival can reach about 75%.

30.5 Meningioma

A predominantly benign tumour of adults arising from meningothelial (arachnoid cap) cells and attached to the dura. Commoner in women (they often carry progesterone receptors and may grow in pregnancy). Sites: parasagittal (falx, superior sagittal sinus), cerebral convexity, sphenoid wing, olfactory groove, sella, foramen magnum, thoracic spinal canal. Risk factors: NF2 (multiple meningiomas), loss of chromosome 22 / NF2 mutation in about half of sporadic tumours, and prior radiation.

  • Rounded, well-defined, dura-based mass that indents but separates easily from the brain
  • Firm, grey-white, may feel gritty (psammoma bodies)
  • May invade overlying bone (hyperostosis) or spread as a sheet along the dura (en plaque)
  • Meningothelial (syncytial): lobules of cells with indistinct borders in whorls
  • Fibrous: elongated cells with collagen; transitional: both, with many whorls
  • Psammomatous: numerous psammoma bodies (laminated calcified whorls)
  • Others: secretory, microcystic; EMA and PR positive

30.6 CNS lymphoma, metastases and familial tumour syndromes

Primary CNS lymphoma

  • Mostly diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
  • Commonest CNS tumour in immunosuppressed patients (AIDS, transplant), where it is almost always EBV-positive; also seen in older immunocompetent adults
  • Often multifocal, deep (periventricular white matter, basal ganglia, corpus callosum)
  • Micro: angiocentric cuffs of large CD20+ cells around vessels, infiltrating brain

Metastatic tumours

  • Mostly carcinomas: a quarter to half of intracranial tumours in hospital practice
  • Primaries (about 80%): lung, breast, skin (melanoma), kidney, GI tract
  • Gross: sharply demarcated, often multiple masses at the grey–white junction with marked oedema
  • Paraneoplastic: cerebellar degeneration (anti-Yo), limbic encephalitis (anti-Hu, small cell lung cancer)

Familial syndromes (besides NF1/NF2, section 30.7): tuberous sclerosis (TSC1/TSC2): cortical tubers, subependymal giant cell astrocytoma, seizures, angiofibromas, renal angiomyolipoma; von Hippel–Lindau (VHL, 3p): cerebellar haemangioblastoma, retinal angiomas, renal cell carcinoma, phaeochromocytoma, polycythaemia; Li–Fraumeni (TP53): gliomas; Gorlin (PTCH1): SHH medulloblastoma.

30.7 Peripheral nerve sheath tumours and neurofibromatosis

Schwannoma

A benign, encapsulated tumour of neural-crest-derived Schwann cells that causes symptoms by compressing the nerve or neighbouring structures. The classic site is the cerebellopontine angle on the vestibular branch of CN VIII (vestibular schwannoma, “acoustic neuroma”): tinnitus and sensorineural hearing loss. Others arise on spinal roots and limb nerves. Sporadic and NF2-associated tumours both lose merlin (NF2 gene); bilateral vestibular schwannomas mean NF2. Malignant change is extremely rare.

  • Well-circumscribed, encapsulated globular mass attached to the nerve but separable from it (nerve pushed to one side)
  • Firm grey-tan; yellow, cystic or haemorrhagic areas
  • Antoni A: dense, cellular fascicles of spindle cells; nuclear palisading; Verocay bodies (two rows of palisaded nuclei separated by a nucleus-free zone of processes)
  • Antoni B: loose, hypocellular, myxoid, microcystic
  • Thick hyalinised vessels, haemosiderin; axons largely excluded
  • S-100 strongly and diffusely positive

Neurofibroma

The commonest peripheral nerve sheath tumour. Three forms: localised cutaneous (dermis and subcutis; sporadic or NF1), diffuse, and plexiform, which is pathognomonic of NF1 and carries a risk of transformation to malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (MPNST), a high-grade sarcoma of which about half arise in NF1.

  • Cutaneous: soft, skin-coloured nodule, often pedunculated; not encapsulated
  • Plexiform: diffuse expansion of a large nerve and its branches, a “bag of worms”
  • Mixed cells: Schwann cells, perineurial-like cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, CD34+ cells
  • Wavy, serpentine nuclei in a loose myxoid stroma with “shredded-carrot” collagen
  • Axons run through the tumour; S-100 patchy
