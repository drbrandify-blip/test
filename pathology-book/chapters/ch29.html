29.1 Cerebral oedema, raised ICP and herniation

The skull is a closed box. Brain, blood and CSF fill it, and the total volume cannot grow. A little extra volume (oedema, haematoma, tumour, abscess) is absorbed by squeezing out CSF and venous blood; once that reserve is used up, intracranial pressure (ICP) rises steeply. Perfusion then falls, adding ischaemia, and the brain is pushed past the rigid folds of dura (falx, tentorium) and through the foramen magnum. This displacement, herniation, is what kills.

Cerebral oedema

  • Vasogenic: blood–brain barrier breaks down; extracellular fluid around tumours, abscesses, haemorrhage, contusion
  • Cytotoxic: energy failure stops the Na⁺/K⁺ pump; cells swell (global hypoxia–ischaemia, toxins)
  • Interstitial (hydrocephalic): CSF forced across the ependyma into periventricular white matter
  • Gross: heavy brain, flattened gyri, narrowed sulci, slit-like ventricles

Hydrocephalus (excess CSF in ventricles)

  • Non-communicating: block inside the ventricles (aqueduct stenosis, colloid cyst, posterior fossa tumour)
  • Communicating: poor resorption at arachnoid villi after meningitis or SAH
  • Infants (sutures open): the head enlarges
  • Ex vacuo: passive ventricular enlargement after brain loss (Alzheimer, old infarct)

29.2 Hypoxia, ischaemia and cerebral infarction

The brain receives about 15% of resting cardiac output, uses about 20% of the body’s oxygen and has almost no energy reserve. It can be starved globally (whole-brain hypoperfusion or hypoxia) or focally (one artery blocked). A stroke is an acute focal neurological deficit of vascular origin; a TIA is one that resolves, usually within an hour and by definition within 24 hours.

Red neuron (acute neuronal injury) is the earliest light-microscopic sign of irreversible hypoxic damage, seen 12–24 hours after the insult: shrunken cell body, intensely eosinophilic cytoplasm, loss of Nissl substance and a pyknotic nucleus without a nucleolus.

Global ischaemia (cardiac arrest, shock)

Mild episodes cause transient confusion; severe ones kill neurons in a selectively vulnerable pattern: hippocampal CA1 pyramidal cells (Sommer sector), cerebellar Purkinje cells and neocortical pyramidal neurons of layers 3, 5, 6 (pseudolaminar necrosis). The most severe injury ends in brain death.

Border-zone (watershed) infarcts

Hypotension starves the most distal parts of arterial territories. The ACA–MCA border gives a sickle-shaped band of necrosis over the convexity, a few centimetres lateral to the interhemispheric fissure.

Why an embolic infarct turns red. An embolus often fragments or lyses. Blood flows back into vessels whose walls have died and leaks out as petechial haemorrhages that may merge. A thrombus on a plaque stays put, so its infarct stays pale. This is why CT comes first: thrombolytics and anticoagulants are dangerous in a bleeding brain. And unlike other organs, the brain undergoes liquefactive necrosis: it has little stroma and much lipid, and enzymes from neutrophils and microglia dissolve it into a cavity.

Primary (hypertensive) intracerebral haemorrhage

Spontaneous bleeding into the brain substance, peaking around 60 years; hypertension is the leading cause. Sites: putamen (50–60%), thalamus, pons, cerebellum. Basal ganglia and thalamic bleeds are ganglionic haemorrhages.

!Chronic hypertension -> Hyaline arteriolosclerosis of penetrating arteries -> Weak wall: Charcot–Bouchard microaneurysms -> *Rupture: ganglionic haemorrhage Hyaline arteriolosclerosis of penetrating arteries -> Narrowed lumen -> *Lacunar infarcts (<15 mm)
  • Acute: clot compresses brain; may rupture into ventricles
  • Old: slit-like cavity with brown haemosiderin lining
  • Lacunes: small cavities in lenticular nucleus, thalamus, internal capsule, pons; pure motor or sensory strokes. Slit haemorrhages come from small penetrating vessels.
  • Cerebral amyloid angiopathy: Aβ stiffens cortical vessels (Congo red positive) → recurrent lobar haemorrhages in the elderly; linked to Alzheimer disease.

Subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH) and berry aneurysm

The commonest cause of non-traumatic SAH is a ruptured saccular (berry) aneurysm; others are AVMs, trauma, extension of an intracerebral bleed, bleeding disorders and tumours.

  • Outpouching at a branch point of the circle of Willis; about 90% anterior circulation; multiple in 20–30%
  • Sites: ACA–anterior communicating junction (commonest) > MCA bifurcation > ICA–posterior communicating junction > basilar tip
  • Not truly congenital: a gap in the media at the branch point balloons in adult life
  • Associations: ADPKD, Ehlers–Danlos IV, NF1, Marfan, coarctation; smoking and hypertension; rupture risk rises sharply above 10 mm
  • Wall: hyalinised intima and adventitia; no smooth muscle or internal elastic lamina; rupture at the apex
  • Peak 5th decade: sudden “worst headache of my life”, neck stiffness, collapse; 25–50% die with the first bleed
  • Complications: rebleeding, vasospasm → infarcts, communicating hydrocephalus (meningeal fibrosis)
  • CSF: uniformly bloody, then xanthochromic

SAH is not a traumatic haematoma. Epidural: skull fracture tears the middle meningeal artery; biconvex clot; lucid interval then rapid decline. Subdural: torn bridging veins (elderly, shaken infants); crescent-shaped clot; slow course. AVM: tangled vessels without capillaries, young men, seizures and bleeds.

29.4 CNS infections: meningitis and brain abscess

Microbes reach the CNS by four routes: haematogenous (commonest), direct implantation (trauma, meningomyelocele), local extension (sinus, mastoid, dental) and along peripheral nerves (rabies, herpes viruses).

Meningitis is inflammation of the leptomeninges (pia–arachnoid) and the CSF in the subarachnoid space: acute pyogenic (bacterial), aseptic (usually viral) or chronic (tuberculous, spirochaetal, cryptococcal). Spread into the brain is meningoencephalitis.

Aseptic (viral) meningitis, usually enteroviral: similar onset but a milder, self-limiting course; only mild lymphocytic leptomeningeal infiltrate.

Tuberculous meningitis (chronic meningitis)

Spreads from a pulmonary or miliary focus; weeks of headache, malaise, confusion and vomiting, often with cranial nerve palsies.

  • Gelatinous, fibrinous exudate at the base of the brain, filling the cisterns and encasing cranial nerves
  • Small white tubercles; tuberculoma (caseous mass) in brain
  • Lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages; caseating granulomas with Langhans giant cells
  • Obliterative endarteritis → infarcts; arachnoid fibrosis → hydrocephalus

Pakistan is among the five countries with the highest tuberculosis burden (WHO estimates incidence around 266 per 100,000), so tuberculous meningitis is a common cause of chronic meningitis, especially in children. Suspect it with weeks of fever, headache and a cranial nerve palsy. GeneXpert MTB/RIF on CSF gives a rapid answer and detects rifampicin resistance.

Brain abscess

A localised focus of liquefactive necrosis with inflammation, usually bacterial (streptococci, staphylococci, anaerobes). Sources: local extension (mastoid, sinus, teeth), trauma or surgery, and haematogenous spread. Predisposed: acute bacterial endocarditis (multiple abscesses), cyanotic congenital heart disease (right-to-left shunt bypasses the lung filter), bronchiectasis.

  • Cavity of pus with a fibrous capsule; marked surrounding oedema
  • Centre: necrosis and neutrophils → granulation tissue → collagen capsule → outer reactive gliosis (gemistocytes)
Clinical: progressive focal deficit + raised ICP; fever may be absent. CSF: cells and protein up, glucose normal. Risks: herniation, rupture into ventricle, meningitis, venous sinus thrombosis.

29.5 Demyelinating diseases

Demyelinating diseases destroy normal myelin with relative sparing of axons; dysmyelinating diseases (leukodystrophies) make abnormal myelin from the start because of an inherited metabolic defect. Conduction fails without myelin, so symptoms appear although axons survive.

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune demyelinating disorder marked by episodes of neurological deficit separated in time, caused by white-matter lesions separated in space. It is the commonest demyelinating disease; women are affected about twice as often; onset is usually in young adults. Most cases are relapsing–remitting, later secondary progressive; about 10% are progressive from the start.

!HLA-DRB1*15:01 + EBV, low vitamin D -> Loss of tolerance to myelin -> *CD4+ TH1 and TH17 cells enter CNS -> Macrophages, microglia activated -> !Demyelinated plaque Loss of tolerance to myelin -> B cells: oligoclonal IgG -> !Demyelinated plaque
  • Well-circumscribed, slightly depressed, glassy grey-tan plaques in white matter
  • Sites: periventricular, optic nerves and chiasm, brainstem, cerebellum, spinal cord
  • Active: myelin breakdown with lipid-laden macrophages; perivenular lymphocytic cuffs; axons relatively preserved
  • Inactive: no myelin, few oligodendrocytes, dense gliosis, axons reduced
  • Shadow plaque: thin, partly regenerated myelin, ill-defined edge
Clinical: optic neuritis (painful unilateral visual loss, common first event); brainstem signs (diplopia, nystagmus, internuclear ophthalmoplegia); cord signs (weakness, spasticity, bladder dysfunction). CSF: mildly raised protein rich in IgG, mild lymphocytosis in a third, oligoclonal IgG bands (made by plasma cells inside the CNS, absent from serum) in most patients.

29.6 Neurodegenerative diseases

One idea explains them all: misfolded proteins. In each disease a specific protein misfolds, resists degradation and aggregates as inclusions, spreading cell to cell in a prion-like way, while a particular group of neurons dies. Ask three questions: which protein, which inclusion, which neurons. The neurons lost decide the symptoms: cortex → dementia; basal ganglia → movement disorder; motor neurons → weakness.

Alzheimer disease (AD)

The commonest cause of dementia in older adults; incidence climbs from about 3% at 65–74 years to nearly half beyond 85. Most cases are sporadic; 5–10% are familial and early-onset. Clinically: insidious memory loss → disorientation, aphasia, behavioural change → mute and bed-bound after 5–10 years; death usually from pneumonia.

!APP (amyloid precursor protein) -> β- then γ-secretase (presenilin) -> *Aβ42 peptide -> Oligomers, plaques, microglia -> !Synapse and neuron loss: dementia *Aβ42 peptide -> Tau hyperphosphorylated: tangles -> !Synapse and neuron loss: dementia
  • Cortical atrophy: narrow gyri, wide sulci (frontal, temporal, parietal)
  • Hippocampus and entorhinal cortex shrunken early
  • Hydrocephalus ex vacuo
  • Neuritic plaques: dilated, tortuous dystrophic neurites around a central Aβ amyloid core, 20–200 µm; diffuse plaques lack neurites
  • Neurofibrillary tangles: paired helical filaments of hyperphosphorylated tau; flame-shaped in pyramidal cells; “ghost” tangles outlive the neuron
  • Amyloid angiopathy almost always

Genes in AD. APP (chr 21): an extra copy in Down syndrome gives Alzheimer changes by the 40s. PSEN1 (chr 14), PSEN2 (chr 1): γ-secretase parts; early-onset familial AD. APOE ε4 (chr 19): main risk allele in sporadic AD, lowers age of onset; ε2 protects. Normal α-secretase cleavage cuts inside Aβ and prevents its formation.

Parkinson disease (PD)

Parkinsonism (resting pill-rolling tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, masked face, stooped festinating gait) results from damage to the nigrostriatal dopaminergic pathway. Idiopathic PD is the commonest cause; others are dopamine-blocking drugs and the toxin MPTP. Levodopa helps early; many patients later become demented (cortical Lewy bodies: dementia with Lewy bodies).

  • Pallor of the substantia nigra and locus coeruleus
  • Loss of pigmented neurons, gliosis
  • Lewy bodies: round eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions with a dense core and pale halo (α-synuclein)
Genes: SNCA (α-synuclein) and LRRK2 (commonest familial) are dominant; Parkin, PINK1, DJ-1 are recessive and point to defective mitophagy. Themes: protein aggregation, mitochondrial failure, poor protein clearance.

Huntington disease (HD)

Autosomal dominant chorea and dementia. The HTT gene (chr 4p16.3) has an expanded CAG repeat (normal 11–34; disease alleles usually 40 or more) → polyglutamine huntingtin misfolds (toxic gain of function). Longer repeats give earlier onset (anticipation), mainly through paternal transmission.

  • Striking atrophy of the caudate, less of the putamen; dilated lateral ventricles
  • Loss of medium spiny striatal neurons, gliosis; intranuclear huntingtin inclusions
Onset in the 40s–50s; chorea often precedes dementia; depression and suicide; death about 15 years after onset.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (motor neuron disease)

Death of lower (anterior horn, brainstem) and upper (Betz cell) motor neurons: muscle wasting (amyotrophy) plus scarring of the lateral corticospinal tracts (sclerosis). LMN signs (wasting, fasciculations) mix with UMN signs (spasticity, hyperreflexia); sensation, bladder and eye movements are spared. Most cases are sporadic; 5–10% familial: C9orf72 repeat expansion (commonest), SOD1, TDP-43, FUS. Morphology: thin anterior roots, loss of anterior horn cells, pale lateral columns, neurogenic muscle atrophy. Death from respiratory failure.

Anterior horn disease in a Pakistani child is polio until proven otherwise. Pakistan and Afghanistan remain the last countries with endemic wild poliovirus type 1. Polio causes acute, asymmetric, purely lower motor neuron flaccid paralysis after a febrile illness; ALS is a slowly progressive adult disease with both upper and lower motor neuron signs.

CNS slides in 10 seconds. A cavity of foamy macrophages with a gliotic wall: old infarct. Pus in the subarachnoid space: acute meningitis; granulomas at the base: TB. Pus with granulation tissue, collagen capsule and outer gliosis: abscess. Pale, sharply bordered area on myelin stain with axons kept: MS plaque. Silver stain with plaques and flame-shaped tangles: Alzheimer.

(a) Old (remote) cerebral infarct, MCA territory. (b) Lipid-laden macrophages; gliotic wall of reactive astrocytes with new capillaries. (c) Embolism (heart, carotid) or atherosclerotic thrombosis.

Silver-stained cerebral cortex: rounded extracellular structures with a dense central core ringed by swollen, twisted nerve processes; many neurons contain dark flame-shaped fibrillary bundles. (a) Diagnosis. (b) Name the two lesions and their proteins. (c) Which genetic syndrome predisposes to early onset?

Answer

(a) Alzheimer disease. (b) Neuritic plaques (Aβ core); neurofibrillary tangles (hyperphosphorylated tau). (c) Down syndrome (extra APP gene, chromosome 21).

15 one-liners

  1. Vasogenic oedema = leaky BBB; cytotoxic = swollen cells from energy failure.
  2. Uncal herniation: ipsilateral dilated pupil, PCA infarct, Duret bleeds.
  3. Tonsillar herniation compresses the medulla and kills.
  4. Red neurons at 12–24 h are the first sign of hypoxic death.
  5. Most vulnerable: CA1 (Sommer sector), Purkinje cells, cortical layers 3, 5, 6.
  6. Embolic infarcts outnumber thrombotic; MCA territory; often turn red.
  7. Infarct: neutrophils to 48 h, macrophages to 3 weeks, gliotic cavity later.
  8. Hypertensive ICH: putamen; Charcot–Bouchard microaneurysms.
  9. Lobar bleeds in the elderly: cerebral amyloid angiopathy.
  10. Berry aneurysm: ACA–ACoA, no elastic lamina, ADPKD, thunderclap headache.
  11. Bacterial CSF: neutrophils, high protein, very low glucose.
  12. TB meningitis: basal exudate, endarteritis, hydrocephalus, cobweb clot.
  13. MS: periventricular plaques, axons spared, oligoclonal bands.
  14. Alzheimer: Aβ plaques + tau tangles; APP on chr 21; APOE ε4 risk.
  15. PD: Lewy bodies (α-synuclein); HD: CAG repeats, caudate atrophy.
