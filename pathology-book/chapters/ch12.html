12.1 Haemolytic anaemias: common ground

Haemolytic anaemia is anaemia caused by premature destruction of red cells (life span shorter than the normal 120 days), with a compensatory increase in erythropoiesis.

Features shared by all

  • Raised erythropoietin → erythroid hyperplasia of marrow and reticulocytosis (polychromasia); extramedullary haematopoiesis in severe cases.
  • Hb breakdown products accumulate: unconjugated (acholuric) jaundice, pigment gallstones, raised LDH, low haptoglobin.
  • Chronic marrow expansion in childhood deforms bone (crew-cut skull).
  • Iron is not lost (except in intravascular haemolysis), so iron overload can follow transfusions.

12.2 Hereditary spherocytosis (HS)

An inherited defect of the red cell membrane skeleton that makes cells spherical, rigid and easy prey for the spleen. It is commonest in northern Europeans; about 75% of cases are autosomal dominant. Mutations hit ankyrin (commonest), band 3, spectrin or band 4.2.

!Ankyrin, spectrin, band 3 mutation -> Weak membrane skeleton -> Membrane lost as small blebs -> Low surface area to volume: spherocyte -> Rigid cell trapped in splenic cords -> *Eaten by splenic macrophages

Why the spleen kills spherocytes. To pass from the splenic cords into the sinusoids, a 7–8 µm red cell must squeeze through slits only 2–3 µm wide. A normal biconcave disc has spare membrane and folds easily; a sphere has no spare membrane, so it gets stuck, loses more membrane in the hostile, glucose-poor cords and is eaten. Remove the spleen and the spherocytes live almost normally.

  • Spherocytes: small, round, dark-staining cells with no central pallor
  • Polychromasia (reticulocytosis)
  • After splenectomy: Howell–Jolly bodies
  • Spleen: moderately enlarged (500–1000 g), congested cords, prominent macrophages
  • Marrow erythroid hyperplasia
  • Pigment gallstones in 40–50% of adults
Clinical: anaemia, splenomegaly and jaundice, often from childhood. Aplastic crisis when parvovirus B19 shuts down red cell production; haemolytic crisis with infections. Labs: raised MCHC (dehydrated cells), increased osmotic fragility (cells burst in mildly hypotonic saline), reduced eosin-5-maleimide (EMA) binding on flow cytometry, direct Coombs negative. Treatment: splenectomy in severe cases (vaccinate first).

Spherocytes are not specific to HS. Warm AIHA also produces spherocytes, because macrophages nibble antibody-coated membrane. The direct Coombs test separates them: negative in HS, positive in AIHA. A family history and raised MCHC favour HS.

12.3 Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency

G6PD drives the hexose monophosphate shunt, the red cell’s only source of NADPH, which keeps glutathione in its reduced form to neutralise oxidants. The gene is X-linked, so hemizygous males are affected. It is common in malaria-endemic regions of Africa, the Mediterranean, the Middle East and Asia. In the A⁻ variant (Africa) the enzyme is unstable and only older red cells are deficient, so attacks are mild and self-limited; the Mediterranean variant is more severe.

!Oxidant stress: infection, drugs, fava beans -> Low NADPH, little reduced glutathione -> Hb oxidised, precipitates: Heinz bodies Hb oxidised, precipitates: Heinz bodies -> *Membrane damage: intravascular haemolysis Hb oxidised, precipitates: Heinz bodies -> Splenic macrophages pluck Heinz bodies -> *Bite cells: extravascular haemolysis

Triggers

  • Infections (the commonest): oxidants from activated phagocytes.
  • Drugs: antimalarials (primaquine, chloroquine), sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, dapsone.
  • Fava beans (favism), mainly Mediterranean variant.

Picture

  • Episodic, acute intravascular haemolysis 2–3 days after exposure: pallor, jaundice, dark urine (haemoglobinuria); also neonatal jaundice.
  • Smear: bite cells (degmacytes), blister cells, a few spherocytes; Heinz bodies only on supravital stain (crystal violet).
  • Between attacks the blood is normal.

A normal G6PD level during an attack can be false. The oldest, most deficient cells have just been destroyed and young reticulocytes carry more enzyme. Repeat the assay a few weeks after recovery.

12.4 Sickle cell disease

A point mutation in codon 6 of the β-globin gene replaces glutamic acid with valine, producing HbS. Homozygotes (HbSS) have sickle cell anaemia; heterozygotes (sickle trait, about 40% HbS) are usually well. The trait protects against falciparum malaria, which explains its high frequency in Africa, the Middle East and parts of India.

!HbS (Glu6Val in β-globin) -> Deoxygenation -> HbS polymerises into long fibres -> *Sickled, rigid red cells *Sickled, rigid red cells -> Membrane damage: irreversible sickling -> Chronic extravascular haemolysis: anaemia, jaundice, gallstones *Sickled, rigid red cells -> Sticky cells, endothelial activation, low NO -> Microvascular occlusion: pain crises, infarcts

What makes cells sickle more (or less)

  • HbF inhibits polymerisation: infants are well until 5–6 months; hydroxyurea raises HbF.
  • Other Hb: HbA in trait protects; HbC co-polymerises (HbSC disease, milder than SS).
  • MCHC: dehydration increases sickling; coexistent α-thalassaemia lowers MCHC and eases disease.
  • Low pH and low O2; slow transit (spleen, marrow, inflamed tissue).

Diagnosis

  • Smear: sickled cells, target cells, polychromasia; Howell–Jolly bodies once the spleen is lost.
  • Sickling test: sodium metabisulfite deoxygenates blood and induces sickling.
  • Hb electrophoresis / HPLC: HbS with no HbA (SS); HbS about 40% (trait); prenatal DNA testing.
  • Spleen: enlarged in childhood (congested red pulp), then repeated infarcts and fibrosis shrink it to a tiny nubbin by adolescence: autosplenectomy
  • Bone: marrow hyperplasia (crew-cut skull, prominent cheekbones), infarcts, avascular necrosis of femoral head, Salmonella osteomyelitis
  • Kidney: papillary necrosis, inability to concentrate urine
  • Lung: acute chest syndrome
  • Brain: stroke; eye: retinopathy
  • Leg ulcers, priapism, pigment gallstones
  • Extramedullary haematopoiesis; secondary haemosiderosis

12.5 The thalassaemias

Wrong amount, not wrong shape. Sickle cell disease is a qualitative defect: the globin is abnormal. Thalassaemia is a quantitative defect: normal globin, too little of it. The missing chain causes small, pale cells; the partner chain left unpaired causes the real damage. Normal adult Hb: HbA (α2β2) about 97%, HbA2 (α2δ2) 2–3.5%, HbF (α2γ2) under 1%. β-globin is coded on chromosome 11 (2 genes per cell); α-globin on chromosome 16 (4 genes per cell).

β-thalassaemia

Caused mostly by point mutations: β⁰ alleles make no β chain, β⁺ alleles make a reduced amount. Splicing mutations are the commonest (β⁰ or β⁺); promoter mutations reduce transcription (β⁺); chain-terminator (nonsense, frameshift) mutations give β⁰.

!β-globin mutation (β0 or β+) -> Little or no HbA -> Microcytic, hypochromic cells !β-globin mutation (β0 or β+) -> Excess free α chains precipitate -> *Ineffective erythropoiesis + splenic haemolysis -> Severe anaemia -> High erythropoietin -> Marrow expansion: crew-cut skull, facies High erythropoietin -> Extramedullary haematopoiesis: hepatosplenomegaly *Ineffective erythropoiesis + splenic haemolysis -> Low hepcidin: more iron absorbed -> !Iron overload (plus transfusions)

α-thalassaemia

Usually caused by deletion of α-globin genes. Severity rises with each gene lost. Free β chains (as HbH, β4) and γ chains (as Hb Barts, γ4) form soluble tetramers, so precipitation and ineffective erythropoiesis are milder than in β-thalassaemia; but these tetramers bind O2 so tightly that they release almost none to tissues.

β-thalassaemia is the commonest single-gene disorder seen in Pakistani wards. The carrier rate is about 5–7%, and an estimated 5,000 or more children are born with thalassaemia major every year. Cousin marriage is common, which raises the chance that both parents carry the gene. Several provinces have passed thalassaemia prevention laws encouraging premarital or carrier screening, and cascade screening of relatives of every affected child is recommended. Screening is simple: CBC (low MCV/MCH, Mentzer index) then HPLC for HbA2; carrier couples can be offered prenatal diagnosis.

Thal major: “HbF Holds the Fort”

No β chains → HbA gone → the fetal haemoglobin (α2γ2) Holds the Fort, so HbF is the main Hb. In the trait, the δ chain steps up: HbA2 > 3.5% is the carrier’s fingerprint.

12.6 Acquired haemolytic anaemias

Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA)

Antibodies against the patient’s own red cells. Diagnosis rests on the Coombs (antiglobulin) test. Direct Coombs (DAT): the patient’s red cells are mixed with anti-human IgG or anti-C3; agglutination shows the cells are already coated. Indirect Coombs: the patient’s serum is tested against reagent red cells to detect free antibody (cross-matching, antenatal screening).

Cold haemolysin type (paroxysmal cold haemoglobinuria): an IgG Donath–Landsteiner antibody binds at low temperature, fixes complement and lyses cells on warming; mostly children after viral infections.

Paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria (PNH)

!Acquired PIGA mutation in a stem cell -> No GPI anchor -> Loss of CD55 (DAF) and CD59 -> *Complement lyses red cells *Complement lyses red cells -> Intravascular haemolysis, haemoglobinuria *Complement lyses red cells -> Thrombosis (hepatic, portal, cerebral veins)
  • The only acquired intrinsic red cell defect; PIGA is X-linked, so one hit is enough.
  • Only a minority have classic night-time haemoglobinuria.
  • Venous thrombosis (Budd–Chiari) is the leading cause of death; iron deficiency from urinary loss.
  • Linked to aplastic anaemia; may evolve to AML or MDS.
  • Diagnosis: flow cytometry for CD55/CD59-deficient cells. Treatment: eculizumab (anti-C5).

Microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia

Red cells are cut by fibrin strands or damaged endothelium in small vessels, or by prosthetic valves in large ones: intravascular haemolysis with schistocytes (helmet, triangle and burr cells). Causes: DIC, TTP, HUS, malignant hypertension, SLE, disseminated cancer (see Chapter 15).

A 2-year-old boy from a cousin marriage has been pale since 8 months of age. He has frontal bossing, a protuberant abdomen with liver 4 cm and spleen 6 cm. Hb 5.1 g/dL, MCV 58 fL; the film shows target cells, basophilic stippling and many nucleated red cells. HPLC: HbF 92%, HbA2 3%, no HbA.

Reasoning: onset after HbF switch-off + severe microcytic anaemia + marrow expansion + hepatosplenomegaly + HbF dominant with no HbA = β⁰-thalassaemia major. Plan: regular transfusion with chelation, HSCT if a matched sibling exists, and carrier screening of the parents and relatives with counselling for future pregnancies.
