Pathology: All-in-One · Before you begin

Exam craft for pathology

Knowing pathology and scoring in pathology are different skills. These pages teach the second: how examiners award marks, and how to collect them.

1 Writing a 5-mark SEQ

Examiners mark against a key: a list of points, each worth a fraction of a mark. An answer laid out in a predictable order lets the examiner find those points in seconds. For any disease, use the same five-part frame and leave out the parts the question does not ask for.

Time per mark

  • Before you start: minutes per mark = minutes in the paper ÷ total marks. For example, 45 marks in 120 minutes is about 2.5 minutes a mark: roughly 12 minutes per 5-mark SEQ, with 10 minutes left to review.
  • Split time by marks inside a question: in a 2 + 3 question the 2-mark part gets two-fifths of the time, however much you know about it.
  • Attempt every part. The first marks of a question are the easiest to earn; the last mark of a long answer is the hardest.

Presentation that earns marks

  • Use the question's own parts as headings: a), b), c).
  • Write numbered points, not paragraphs; underline the key words.
  • Draw the pathogenesis as a flowchart and give it a title. A 1-minute diagram often carries a full mark.
  • “Differentiate” means a table with a “Feature” column; label every table and diagram.
  • Write morphology under two sub-headings, Gross and Microscopy.
Worked example 11 + 2 + 2 marksDefine cirrhosis. Describe its pathogenesis and morphology.
How a full-mark answer is laid out

a) Definition (1). A diffuse process of the whole liver in which fibrous septa divide the parenchyma into regenerative nodules, with loss of normal architecture and vascular shunting.

b) Pathogenesis (2). Causes first (chronic HCV/HBV, alcohol, fatty liver disease, biliary disease, haemochromatosis, Wilson), then the flowchart:

*Chronic liver injury -> Hepatocyte death and inflammation -> Kupffer cells release TGF-β, PDGF -> !Stellate cells become myofibroblasts -> Collagen in space of Disse -> *Fibrous septa + regenerative nodules Hepatocyte death and inflammation -> Surviving hepatocytes regenerate -> *Fibrous septa + regenerative nodules

c) Morphology (2). Gross: shrunken, firm liver with a nodular surface; micronodular (nodules < 3 mm, e.g. alcohol) or macronodular (> 3 mm, e.g. viral). Microscopy: bridging fibrous septa, regenerative nodules without normal lobular arrangement, ductular reaction, variable inflammation.

Where the marks are: the three key words of the definition (diffuse, fibrosis, nodules); the stellate cell in the flowchart; the nodule size cut-off. Each is a mark-earning point, so make each one easy to see.

2 Describing a gross specimen

At a gross station you have about a minute. Describe in a fixed order, out loud or on paper, and give the diagnosis last: a good description earns marks even when the diagnosis is wrong.

  1. Organ — which organ, which part; whole, bisected or resected
  2. Cut surface — or outer surface, whichever is shown
  3. Size — estimate in cm, against the jar or a ruler
  4. Colour — uniform or variegated; haemorrhage, necrosis, pigment
  5. Consistency — as it looks: firm, soft, friable, gritty, cystic
  6. Lesion — number, site, shape: nodule, ulcer, cyst, mass, cavity
  7. Borders — circumscribed or infiltrative; capsule; spread
  8. Diagnosis — last; if unsure, add a two-item differential

Specimen: half of a kidney in a jar.

SayThis is a bisected nephrectomy specimen. At the upper pole there is a rounded tumour about 6 cm across. Its cut surface is variegated: golden-yellow areas with grey-white, red-brown haemorrhagic and cystic areas. It looks soft, is well circumscribed and pushes the kidney aside; a plug of tumour fills the renal vein. The diagnosis is renal cell carcinoma, clear cell type.

Why yellow? Lipid and glycogen in the clear cells.

3 Identifying a slide in the OSPE

Slides are recognised by pattern at low power, then confirmed by cells at high power. Work through the same five steps every time, starting at the lowest power.

  1. Tissue — low power first: which organ? Find a landmark: villi, glomeruli, alveoli, follicles.
  2. Architecture — is the normal structure kept or lost? Glands, sheets, papillae, nodules?
  3. Cells — a mixture suggests inflammation; one monotonous or pleomorphic population suggests a neoplasm.
  4. Special feature — look for one clue from the table.
  5. Diagnosis — commit: name it, point to two features you can see, add one related fact.

Slide: lung. Alveolar walls are intact, but every alveolus is packed with neutrophils, strands of fibrin and red cells.

Answer: lobar pneumonia, red hepatisation. Features: neutrophil–fibrin exudate filling alveoli; preserved alveolar walls. Related fact: commonest cause Streptococcus pneumoniae.

Worked example 4 · Data station2 minutes

Diabetic aged 19, drowsy, deep sighing breaths. pH 7.18, PaCO2 22 mmHg, HCO3− 9, Na+ 136, Cl− 100 mmol/L.

Step by step

pH ↓ with HCO3 ↓ = metabolic acidosis. Gap = 136 − 109 = 27: high. Expected PaCO2 = 1.5 × 9 + 8 = 21.5 ± 2; measured 22, so compensation is appropriate (Kussmaul breathing). Diabetic ketoacidosis: confirm with blood glucose and ketones.

5 BCQs: one best answer

  1. Cover the options. Read the stem and last line; answer in your head first.
  2. Find the keywords: age, sex, time course, site, exposure, the one abnormal test.
  3. Uncover and match. If your answer is there, it is usually right; if not, eliminate and choose between the last two.
  4. Read the qualifier: most common, most specific, earliest, except.
  5. Do not change an answer without a clear reason; know the paper’s negative-marking rule.

A 45-year-old woman has tiredness, weight gain and a firm, painless, diffusely enlarged thyroid. Which finding is most likely on fine-needle aspiration?

  1. Clear, grooved nuclei with inclusions
  2. Lymphocytes and Hürthle cells
  3. Granulomas with multinucleate giant cells
  4. Spindle cells in an amyloid stroma
  5. Crowded follicular cells in microfollicles

Cover, then predict: middle-aged woman + hypothyroid features + painless diffuse goitre = Hashimoto thyroiditis, so expect lymphocytes and Hürthle cells. Uncover: B matches. Answer B. Every distractor is the right answer to another question: A papillary carcinoma, C de Quervain (painful) thyroiditis, D medullary carcinoma, E follicular neoplasm.

6 The viva

Examiners judge clarity as much as knowledge: give a short, structured answer, then stop and let the examiner lead.

The 1-1-1 answerDefinition, one cause, one feature. “What is a granuloma?” — A focus of chronic inflammation made of activated macrophages that have become epithelioid cells, often with giant cells and a rim of lymphocytes. The commonest cause in Pakistan is tuberculosis, and its typical feature there is central caseous necrosis.

Good habits

  • Listen to the whole question; a one-second pause is fine.
  • For a specimen, begin: “This is a specimen of… showing…”.
  • Give numbers with units; offer to draw a flowchart if it helps.

When you do not know

  • Ask for the question to be repeated or rephrased.
  • Bridge from what you know: “I am not sure of the exact figure, but I know that…”.
  • Never invent: an honest “I don’t remember” costs less than a confident wrong answer.
  • Accept correction politely and move on; never argue.
